## Introduction

S1 used science to explain single results: why a dry surface browns, why an egg sets. S2 asks the learner to **predict**
results and to **test** them. `SCI-2` comes first in the stage because every later S2 course leans on it:
- **proteins:** what heat does to them, and what salt does (brining, juiciness), which the meat, poultry and fish
  courses use;
- **starches and gluten:** what thickens sauces and builds dough structure (sauces, pastry, bread);
- **fats and emulsions:** the stocks and sauces;
- **water, salt, sugar and acid:** everything from browning to leavening;
- **microbiology:** why some risks survive cooking, and how hurdles keep food safe.

**The method is the first module.** A fair kitchen experiment has one variable, a control, blind tasting and a written
plan and result. Every module then runs one such experiment. The learner leaves the course able to answer a cooking
question with data instead of opinion, which is the start of `RD-TEST` as a working habit.

The science follows McGee [S16] and López-Alt [S17], kept to what explains a result at the stove. Safety figures come
only from [assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment).

**In the spiral.**
- `SC-PROT`, `SC-STRC`, `SC-EMUL`, `SC-BRWN` and `SC-MICR` reach level 3 here. Their applied parts (a mayonnaise broken
  and repaired, a seared crust, the hurdles of one's own preserves) are completed in `SAU-2`, `MEA-2` and `PRS-3`.
- `SCI-3` (S3) takes the science to level 4.

**Pacing.** About three weeks, in evening sessions. The brining comparison needs a day of waiting; the starch samples
are checked again after chilling and freezing.

<!-- END generated:module-sci-2-m1 -->

#### SCI-2.M1.L1 — Designing a kitchen experiment

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M6.L1` · moves RD-TEST→2*

*Objectives:* [K] variables, controls, blind tasting, repetition; [S] write a test plan and a result table
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m1-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The question** comes first, written so that it can be answered. "Does dry-brining make chicken juicier?" can be
  tested; "what is the best chicken?" cannot.
- **Variables:**
  - the **independent variable** is what the cook changes (one only);
  - the **dependent variable** is what is measured (weight loss, time, a taster's choice);
  - the **controlled variables** are everything held the same (cut, size, pan, heat, salt).
- **A control** is the unchanged version to compare against.
- **Blind tasting** with coded samples keeps expectation out ([assessment §5](../../04-assessment.md#5-calibration-tests),
  [S20]).
- **Repetition:** one trial can be luck. Two or three, or several tasters, make a result worth trusting.
- **The plan and the result table** are written before cooking. The conclusion says only what the data support, and
  names the next question.

**Sessions:**
1. Plan and run a small test (90 min): write a plan for a quick question (for example "does salting pasta water at 10
   or 20 g/l change the taste of the pasta?"), run it with a blind taster, and fill in the result table.

**Cues:** a plan another person could repeat; a conclusion no bigger than the data.

**Faults:**
- Changing two things at once.
- No control.
- The cook tasting unblinded.
- A conclusion from one taste.
- Writing the plan after the result.

#### SCI-2.M1.X1 — A test of your own design

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-sci-2-m1-x1 -->
*comparison, framework* — Design and run a two-variable test of your choice, with a written plan.
<!-- END generated:exercise-sci-2-m1-x1 -->

**Evidences:** RD-TEST→2

**Aim:** answer a cooking question of the learner's choice with a fair test.

**Conditions:** framework support: the plan and report template. Two versions that differ in one variable (the outline's
"two-variable test": two levels of one variable), with a control, blind tasting by at least one other person, and a
written plan before cooking.

**Inputs:** depends on the question; something cheap and quick, for example roasted carrots at 180 against 220 °C, or
mashed potato with butter added hot against cold.

**Procedure:**
1. Write the question, the variable, the controls and the result table.
2. Cook both versions.
3. Code them and have them tasted blind.
4. Measure what can be measured (time, weight, colour photo).
5. Write the conclusion and the next question.

**Evidence:** log, form — the plan and the filled-in result table on the template, with the tasters' forms. This meets
the `RD-TEST` level 2 success row (a guided comparison with one variable changed, recorded on a template).

**Time:** 1 h.

<!-- END generated:module-sci-2-m2 -->

#### SCI-2.M2.L1 — Denaturation and water loss

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `EGG-1.M1.L2` · moves SC-PROT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] how heat unfolds and tightens proteins; juiciness and temperature; collagen introduced
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m2-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Heat unfolds muscle proteins**, which then tighten and squeeze out water. The hotter the meat, the tighter the
  proteins and the more juice is lost. This is why doneness, not time, decides juiciness, and why a chicken breast at
  80 °C is drier than one taken to the safe 74 °C and rested.
- **The safe minimums** ([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)) and the
  juiciness ladder sit together: cooking a little above the safe minimum, not far above, is the craft.
- **Collagen**, the connective tissue, behaves the other way. It is tough at the temperatures that keep muscle juicy,
  and only turns into gelatin after long, moist cooking at a simmer. Tender cuts are cooked fast and briefly; collagen-
  rich cuts slowly (`MEA-2`, `SLW-3`).

**Sessions:**
1. Weigh the loss (60 min): two equal pieces of chicken breast cooked to 74 °C and to about 82 °C, weighed before and
   after, sliced and tasted.

**Cues:** a clearly higher weight loss and a drier texture in the overcooked piece.

**Faults:**
- Cutting into the meat to check, which loses juice.
- Probing once and too early.
- Judging juiciness without weighing.

#### SCI-2.M2.L2 — Salting, brining and marinating

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m2-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 1 d · builds on `SCI-2.M2.L1` · moves SC-PROT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] osmosis, diffusion, salt-protein interaction; what marinades can and can't do
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m2-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Salt dissolves part of the muscle proteins**, which then hold water better while cooking: brined meat loses less
  juice and seasons through.
- **Dry-brining:** salt rubbed on (about 1–1.5 % of the weight), rested uncovered in the fridge for hours or
  overnight. The salt draws out moisture, dissolves in it, and is drawn back in by diffusion; the dry surface browns
  well.
- **Wet-brining:** soaking in salted water. It adds water as well as salt, which makes the meat juicier but the skin
  harder to crisp. It needs fridge space and time limits.
- **Marinades** mostly flavour the surface: salt travels in, most other flavours only a few millimetres. Strong acid left
  for long makes the surface mushy or chalky. A marinade with raw meat in it is handled as raw meat (`SAF-1.M1.Q6`).

**Sessions:**
1. Salt the chicken (30 min): set up the dry-brine and wet-brine pieces for the exercise the next day.
2. Cook and weigh (60 min): the exercise below.

**Cues:** the dry-brined surface matt and dry; the brined pieces seasoned through when tasted.

**Faults:**
- Brining at room temperature.
- A wet brine left too long, which makes the meat spongy and salty.
- Salting a surface just before searing and leaving it wet.
- Expecting a marinade to tenderise a tough cut.

#### SCI-2.M2.X1 — Chicken breast: unsalted, dry-brined and wet-brined

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-sci-2-m2-x1 -->
*comparison, recipe* — Chicken breast: unsalted, dry-brined and wet-brined; weigh before and after cooking.
<!-- END generated:exercise-sci-2-m2-x1 -->

**Evidences:** SC-PROT→3

**Aim:** measure what salting does to cooking loss, with everything else held constant.

**Conditions:** recipe support: a fixed comparison. Three pieces of the same size from the same pack; the same pan,

**Inputs:** 3 chicken breasts (or 6 thighs); salt; a fridge-safe container for the wet brine.

**Procedure:**
1. Weigh the pieces.
2. Dry-brine one (1 % salt) uncovered in the fridge overnight; wet-brine one (5 % brine) for 2 hours before cooking;

6. Write the explanation.

80 %: the `SC-PROT` level 3 success row (a dry-brined and an unsalted piece compared by cooking loss and explained).

**Time:** 1.5 h over two days; the chicken is dinner.

*Objectives:* [K] compare starch thickeners; explain gluten development and how to control it.

#### SCI-2.M3.L1 — Starch thickening

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m3-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `STA-1.M1.L1` · moves SC-STRC→3*

*Objectives:* [K] flour, corn starch, potato starch, arrowroot: gel temperature, clarity, breakdown, freezing
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m3-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Starches thicken by gelatinising** (`STA-1.M1.L1`), each with its own behaviour:
  - **flour:** cloudy and opaque, needs to simmer for a while to lose its raw taste, stable in long cooking; used in a
    roux;
  - **corn starch:** fairly clear, thickens near the boil, thins out with long stirring or strong acid, weeps after
    freezing;
  - **potato starch:** very clear, thickens at a lower temperature, breaks down if boiled long;
  - **arrowroot:** clear and glossy, tolerates acid, thickens below the boil, freezes well.
- **Slurry or roux:** starch is dispersed in cold liquid or fat first, so the granules separate and don't lump.
- **Cold and frozen:** on cooling, some starch gels set firm and others weep water (retrogradation); the choice depends
  on whether the sauce will be chilled or frozen.

**Sessions:**
1. Three starches (60 min): the comparison of `SCI-2.M3.X1`, tasted and photographed hot.

**Cues:** clear differences in gloss, clarity and body, and the learner's predictions confirmed.

**Faults:**
- Dry starch into hot liquid, which lumps.
- Too little cooking for flour.
- Boiling potato starch hard.
- Freezing a corn-starch sauce.

#### SCI-2.M3.L2 — Gluten

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m3-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M3.L1` · moves SC-STRC→3, IN-BAK→2*

*Objectives:* [K] glutenin and gliadin; water, mixing, rest, fat and sugar as controls
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m3-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Gluten** forms when two wheat proteins, glutenin (strength and elasticity) and gliadin (extensibility), meet water
  and are worked. Mixing and kneading align them into a stretchy network that traps gas: the structure of bread.
- **The controls:**
  - **water:** more water, more gluten;
  - **mixing:** more, more gluten;
  - **rest:** the network relaxes and becomes easier to shape;
  - **fat:** coats the proteins and "shortens" them, which is why pastry is short;
  - **sugar:** competes for water and weakens the network.
- **Flour types:** strong bread flour (more protein), all-purpose and cake flour (less) (`IN-BAK`).
- **When gluten is wanted, and when not:** bread and pasta, yes; pastry, cakes and pancakes, as little as possible.

**Sessions:**
1. Short and long (60 min): two small doughs of flour and water, one mixed for 1 minute, one kneaded for 8; rested,
   stretched (the windowpane test) and photographed; a third with butter rubbed in first.

**Cues:** the long-mixed dough stretches thin without tearing; the short one tears; the buttered one crumbles.

**Faults:**
- Overworking pastry.
- Too little kneading for bread.
- Shaping without resting.

<!-- END generated:module-sci-2-m4 -->

#### SCI-2.M4.L1 — Fats and oils

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m4-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · moves SC-EMUL→2*

*Objectives:* [K] saturation, smoke point, flavour, butter versus clarified butter; [J] choose a fat
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m4-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Saturation:** saturated fats (butter, lard, coconut) are solid when cool; unsaturated oils (rapeseed, sunflower,
  olive) are liquid. This decides texture in baking and mouthfeel in sauces.
- **The smoke point:** where a fat starts to break down and smoke. Refined oils have a high smoke point; extra virgin
  olive oil and butter a lower one, because of their flavour compounds and, in butter, milk solids.
- **Clarified butter** (milk solids and water removed) has the flavour of butter and a much higher smoke point.
- **Choosing a fat:** high heat needs a refined oil or clarified butter; flavour at the end, raw olive oil or butter;
  baking texture, solid fats.

**Sessions:**
1. Clarify and compare (45 min): clarify butter; heat a neutral oil, whole butter and clarified butter in three pans
   and note when each smokes or browns.

**Cues:** whole butter browning and smoking first; the clarified butter clear and calm.

**Faults:**
- Searing in whole butter from the start.
- Deep-frying in extra virgin olive oil.
- Reusing burnt fat.

#### SCI-2.M4.L2 — Emulsions

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m4-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M4.L1`, `EGG-1.M4.L2` · moves SC-EMUL→3*

*Objectives:* [K] oil-in-water and water-in-oil; emulsifiers (lecithin, mustard), droplet size, temperature; why they break
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m4-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **An emulsion** is droplets of one liquid dispersed in another that doesn't mix with it.
  - **Oil-in-water:** oil droplets in water, as in a vinaigrette, mayonnaise or hollandaise.
  - **Water-in-oil:** water droplets in fat, as in butter.
- **Stability:**
  - **emulsifiers** sit between oil and water and keep droplets apart: lecithin in egg yolk, mucilage in mustard;
  - **small droplets**, from vigorous whisking, settle slowly;
  - **thickness:** a thicker continuous phase slows separation;
  - **temperature** matters: too hot and egg emulsions curdle, too cold and butter sets.
- **Why they break:** oil added too fast, too much oil for the emulsifier, the wrong temperature. The repair starts a
  new emulsion (a fresh yolk or a spoon of water) and adds the broken one slowly (`SAU-2`).

**Sessions:**
1. Temporary and stable (45 min): a vinaigrette shaken without and with mustard; a quick mayonnaise from one yolk.
2. Too fast (45 min): a second mayonnaise with the oil added too fast so that it breaks, then repaired with a new yolk.

**Cues:** the mustard vinaigrette stays cloudy and thick for minutes; the repaired mayonnaise thick and glossy.

**Faults:**
- Oil in a stream at the start.
- Cold yolks straight from the fridge with warm oil.
- Giving up on a broken emulsion.

### SCI-2.M5 — Water, salt, sugar and acid

*Scope:* water activity, sugar and salt as preservers, pH in cooking.

#### SCI-2.M5.L1 — Water activity

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m5-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · moves SC-MICR→2*

*Objectives:* [K] free water; how salt and sugar bind it (jam, cures, candied fruit)
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m5-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Water activity** is how much of a food's water is free to be used by microbes and to take part in reactions. It is
  not the same as water content: jam is wet but its water is bound by sugar.
- **Salt and sugar bind water.** That is how cures, salted fish, jam and candied peel keep: microbes cannot get the
  water they need.
- **In cooking:** salt draws water out of vegetables (salting courgette or cabbage), and sugar keeps cakes moist.

**Sessions:**
1. Salt and sugar draw water (45 min): sliced cucumber or courgette salted and sugared in two bowls; weigh the water
   drawn out after 30 minutes.

**Cues:** a clear pool of liquid under both, more under the salt per gram.

**Faults:**
- Confusing "dry" with "low water activity".
- Thinking a low-sugar jam keeps like a full-sugar one.

#### SCI-2.M5.L2 — Acids and bases

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m5-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M2.L1` · moves SC-BRWN→3, IN-BAK→2*

*Objectives:* [K] pH in cooking: baking soda and browning, acid and green vegetables, buttermilk and leavening
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m5-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **pH in cooking:**
  - **alkali** (baking soda) speeds browning and softens vegetables and onions;
  - **acid** keeps red cabbage red, turns green vegetables olive, firms vegetables and curdles milk.
- **Leavening:**
  - baking soda needs an acid (buttermilk, yoghurt, brown sugar) to release carbon dioxide;
  - baking powder carries its own acid;
  - too much soda tastes soapy and browns too much.
- **Measuring pH at home** with strips is optional; the effects are visible.

**Sessions:**
1. Soda onions (45 min): the comparison of `SCI-2.M5.X1`.

**Cues:** the soda onions brown far faster and turn soft and jammy.

**Faults:**
- Too much soda, which makes them mushy and soapy.
- Comparing at different heats.

### SCI-2.M6 — Microbiology for cooks

<!-- BEGIN generated:module-sci-2-m6 -->
*3 h · 2 lessons · 1 practical sessions · reaches SC-MICR 3, SA-HRPR 1, SA-TEMP 4*

*Scope:* growth, spores and toxins; fermentation; preservation hurdles.

*Objectives:* [K] explain why some risks survive cooking; [J] judge when a process needs a tested method.

*Check (outline):* scenario quiz; a safety item failed means a resit.
<!-- END generated:module-sci-2-m6 -->

#### SCI-2.M6.L1 — Growth, spores and toxins

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m6-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SAF-1.M3.L3` · moves SC-MICR→3, SA-HRPR→1*

*Objectives:* [K] spore-formers and heat-stable toxins; why reheating doesn't always save food; fermentation as controlled growth
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m6-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Growth needs** time, a suitable temperature, moisture, nutrients, the right acidity and, for some, the absence of
  oxygen.
- **Spore-formers** (*Clostridium botulinum*, *C. perfringens*, *Bacillus cereus*) survive cooking as spores and grow
  when cooked food cools slowly or is held warm. *C. botulinum* grows without oxygen in low-acid food, which is why
  garlic or herbs in oil need refrigeration
  ([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)) and preserves need tested methods [S27].
- **Heat-stable toxins** (from *Staphylococcus aureus* and *B. cereus*) are not destroyed by reheating: once formed, the
  food is unsafe.
- **Fermentation** is controlled growth: lactic acid bacteria lower the pH and crowd out pathogens (yoghurt,
  sauerkraut, sourdough). It is safe because of the conditions, not by luck.

**Sessions:** theory only (1.5 h): for six foods in the learner's kitchen, name the organism of most concern and why
cooking does or doesn't remove it.

**Cues:** the learner explains why reheated rice can make people ill.

**Faults:**
- "Cooking kills everything".
- Infused oils left in the cupboard.
- A home ferment without salt or without a tested ratio.

#### SCI-2.M6.L2 — Hurdles

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m6-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M6.L1` · moves SC-MICR→3, SA-TEMP→4*

*Objectives:* [K] temperature, acid, salt, water activity and oxygen as combined hurdles; [J] apply them to leftovers and storage; [S] a hurdle plan for three of your own foods
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m6-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Hurdles:** each factor that slows microbes:
  - temperature (cold, or hot holding);
  - acid (low pH);
  - salt or sugar (low water activity);
  - oxygen (its absence or presence);
  - competing microbes (fermentation);
  - preservatives.
- **Hurdles combine.** One alone may not be enough, while two or three together make a food safe: a pickle is acidic
  and salted; a cured ham is salted, dried and cold-stored.
- **Applying them at home:**
  - leftovers rely on temperature (fast cooling, the fridge);
  - a home preserve relies on a tested combination [S27].
- **Cooling a batch** by the measurable standard
  ([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)):
  - from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours;
  - to 5 °C or below within 6 hours in total;
  - by shallow containers or an ice bath.

**Sessions:**
1. A hurdle plan (60 min): write the hurdle plan for three foods the learner makes (for example a soup for the week,
   quick pickles, a garlic oil), and cool the soup by the plan with a log.

**Cues:** each food has at least one named hurdle with a number; the cooling log meets the standard.

**Faults:**
- Relying on smell.
- Leaving a pot of soup to cool on the stove overnight.
- Making garlic oil and storing it at room temperature.

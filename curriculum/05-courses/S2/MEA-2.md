## Introduction

Meat rewards understanding more than any other ingredient. A cut's place on the animal says almost everything about how
to cook it:
- **muscles that worked hard** (shoulder, leg, neck) have more connective tissue and flavour. They need long, moist
  heat;
- **muscles that rested** (loin, fillet) are tender and lean. They need a short, hot cook and a precise doneness.

Most failures at home are a mismatch between cut and method, or a doneness missed by a few degrees.

`MEA-2` builds the understanding first:
- **meat science:** muscle, collagen, fat and colour;
- **cuts and buying:** beef, pork and lamb, with their Dutch names, at the butcher and the supermarket.

Then it applies it to the four methods a home cook uses most:
- **steaks and chops**, seared and finished, judged by touch and confirmed by the probe;
- **roast joints**, sear-first against reverse sear, carved and plated for guests;
- **minced meat**, handled to the stricter rules that mince needs;
- **a first stew**, which prepares the braising of `SLW-3`.

**Doneness** is chosen, not guessed. The learner states a target before cooking and is judged against it after
resting. The safe figures come from assessment §9:
- **pork, lamb and other intact meat:** 63 °C for 15 seconds;
- **minced meat:** 75 °C;
- **whole roasts:** the time-temperature table;
- **beef steaks:** may be served rare when seared on both faces with the colour changed on every outside surface.

**In the spiral.**
- `TS-PRO` level 3 gets its meat part here; `POU-2` and `FSH-2` add poultry and fish.
- `IN-MEA` reaches level 3.
- `JD-CUES` level 3 is practised again with the touch test.
- The first stew is the bridge to `SLW-3`, where braising, stewing and collagen-rich cuts are taught in depth.

**Pacing.** About five weeks. Steaks, chops and burgers fit weeknights. The joint and the stews are weekend cooking, and
the stews freeze. Meat is the most expensive ingredient of S2: Good options are given everywhere, and every exercise is
a household meal.

#### MEA-2.M1.L1 — Muscle, collagen and fat

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M2.L1` · moves IN-MEA→2, SC-PROT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] fibres, connective tissue and marbling; working versus resting muscles; tenderness
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m1-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Muscle fibres** are bundles of protein, wrapped in connective tissue. Tenderness depends on three things:
  - **fibre thickness:** fine-grained cuts are more tender;
  - **the amount and kind of connective tissue:** collagen softens to gelatin with long, moist heat; elastin (silverskin)
    never softens and is trimmed;
  - **how far the fibres contract** with heat (`SCI-2.M2.L1`).
- **Working and resting muscles:**
  - **resting muscles** along the back (loin, fillet, ribeye) are tender, for quick, dry heat;
  - **working muscles** (shoulder, leg, shank, neck, brisket) have more collagen and more flavour, for long, moist heat,
    or for mincing or thin slicing across the grain.
- **Fat:**
  - **marbling** (fat within the muscle) adds juiciness and flavour;
  - **the fat cover** protects a roast;
  - **inter-muscular fat** runs between muscles in shoulder and neck cuts.
- **The grain:** the direction of the fibres. Slicing across it shortens them, so a firm cut eats more tenderly.
- **Predicting a cut:** from its place on the animal, its grain, its marbling and its connective tissue, the learner says
  how it will behave and which family of method suits it (`CK-METH`).

**Sessions:** theory only (2 h): sort photos of twelve cuts by tenderness and method, with a line of reasoning for each.

**Cues:** for any cut on the counter, the learner can say whether it worked or rested, what will happen to it on a hot
pan, and what will happen in a stew.

**Faults:**
- Treating every lean cut as tender.
- Searing a shin as a steak.
- Stewing a fillet.
- Slicing with the grain.

#### MEA-2.M1.L2 — Colour and doneness

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m1-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `MEA-2.M1.L1` · moves SC-PROT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] myoglobin, colour by temperature, the doneness scale; [S] read a cross-section
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m1-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Myoglobin** makes meat red. It is more plentiful in working muscles and in older animals. With heat it changes from
  red to pink to grey-brown. The colour of a cross-section therefore shows the temperature it reached.
- **The doneness scale for beef** (core after resting, a culinary target, not a safety figure):

  | Doneness | Core | Cross-section |
  |---|---|---|
  | Rare | About 50–52 °C | Deep red, cool to warm centre |
  | Medium-rare | About 55–57 °C | Red-pink, warm |
  | Medium | About 60–63 °C | Pink |
  | Medium-well | About 65–68 °C | Faint pink |
  | Well done | 70 °C or more | Grey-brown throughout |

- **Safety and doneness** (assessment §9):
  - a whole-muscle, intact beef steak may be served rare once it is seared on top and bottom with the colour changed
    on every outside surface, because the bacteria are on the outside;
  - pork, lamb and other intact meat reach 63 °C for 15 seconds;
  - minced meat reaches 75 °C;
  - nothing rare is served to risk groups.
- **Juiciness against doneness:** the hotter the core, the more the fibres contract and the more juice is lost. The
  learner will weigh it (`SCI-2.M2.L1`).
- **Reading a cross-section:** an even colour from edge to centre, against a grey band under the crust (`HEA-2.M1`).
  The photo is taken on a cut through the thickest part, in daylight.

**Sessions:**
1. Read the cross-sections (45 min): the cuts of `MEA-2.M1.X1` photographed side by side; each matched to its reading
   and named on the scale.

**Cues:** the learner names the doneness of a cross-section photo within one step of the scale.

**Faults:**
- Judging colour under yellow kitchen light.
- Cutting at the thin end.
- Calling a grey band "medium".

#### MEA-2.M1.X1 — One steak at five temperatures

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-mea-2-m1-x1 -->
*comparison, recipe* — Thin slices of the same steak cooked to five temperatures; photograph and taste.
<!-- END generated:exercise-mea-2-m1-x1 -->

**Evidences:** SC-PROT→3

**Aim:** see, taste and weigh what doneness does to the same piece of meat.

**Conditions:**
- **Recipe support:** the recipe Phase 6 writes.
- **The meat:** a thick steak (rump, *kogelbiefstuk*, or a sirloin of about 3 cm) cut into five equal pieces. Each is
  seared on every outside surface and finished in the oven to a different core: about 50, 55, 60, 65 and 70 °C.
- **The weighing:** every piece is weighed before and after cooking.
- **The tasting:** each piece is sliced thin and tasted by the household on the form.

**Inputs:** for 4, as a tasting with a salad and bread: one thick steak of 500–600 g.

**Procedure:**
1. Weigh the pieces.
2. Sear and finish each to its target, probing as it goes.
3. Rest and weigh them again.
4. Slice them and photograph the five cross-sections in a row in daylight.
5. Taste them on the form.
6. Calculate the cooking loss in % for each.
7. Explain the trend in three sentences.

**Evidence:** measure, written, quiz:
- **`SC-PROT` level 3, the doneness part:** five pieces weighed before and after cooking, with the loss in % against
  the core temperature, the trend explained, and this check at 80 %. The brining comparison of the row was shown in
  `SCI-2.M2.X1` and `POU-2.M6.X1`.
- **The reference set:** the five cross-section photos become the learner's reference for `MEA-2.M3`.

**Time:** 1 h.

#### MEA-2.M2.L1 — Beef cuts

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M1.L1` · moves IN-MEA→3*

*Objectives:* [K] the primal areas and the main cuts; tender versus tough; cut to method; Dutch names
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The primal areas:** neck and chuck, rib, loin, round (the hind leg), brisket and plate, shank.
- **The cuts a Dutch shop sells**, by method:

  | Cut (NL) | Area | Character | Method |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | *Ossenhaas* | Fillet | The most tender, lean, mild | Sear and roast, short |
  | *Entrecote*, *ribeye* | Loin, rib | Tender, marbled, a fat edge | Sear, grill |
  | *Rosbief* | Loin or round | Lean, fine-grained | Roast, then slice thin |
  | *Kogelbiefstuk*, *biefstuk* | Round | Lean, firmer | Sear quickly, slice across the grain |
  | *Sukadelappen*, *runderlappen* | Chuck, shoulder | Collagen-rich | Stew, braise (*stoofvlees*) |
  | *Klapstuk*, *riblappen* | Brisket, plate | Fatty, collagen-rich | Braise, stew |
  | *Schenkel* | Shank | Collagen and marrow bone | Braise, stock |
  | *Rundergehakt* | Trim | Mixed | `MEA-2.M5` |

- **Tender against tough** is a scale, not two boxes. A *kogelbiefstuk* is tender enough for a quick sear only if it is
  cut thin and sliced across the grain.
- **Cut to method:** the learner predicts the method of an unfamiliar cut from its area and the look of it.

**Sessions:** theory only (2 h): a cut-to-method card for beef, built in the learner's own words, with the Dutch names.

**Cues:** for each cut on the card, the learner can give its area, its character and a method, with a reason.

**Faults:**
- *Sukadelappen* fried as steaks.
- *Ossenhaas* cooked past medium.
- *Rosbief* sliced thick and with the grain.

#### MEA-2.M2.L2 — Pork and lamb cuts

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M1.L1` · moves IN-MEA→3*

*Objectives:* [K] the main cuts and their methods; Dutch names
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Pork:**

  | Cut (NL) | Area | Method |
  |---|---|---|
  | *Varkenshaas* | Tenderloin | Sear and roast to 63 °C |
  | *Karbonade*, *haaskarbonade* | Loin chop | Sear, pan-roast |
  | *Fricandeau* | Leg | Roast, slice thin |
  | *Procureur*, *schouder* | Shoulder and neck | Roast slowly, stew, pull |
  | *Buikspek*, *speklap* | Belly | Roast slowly, braise, crisp |

- **Lamb** (mostly Dutch spring and summer lamb, from the Texel or salt-marsh flocks):

  | Cut (NL) | Area | Method |
  |---|---|---|
  | *Lamsrack*, *lamskoteletten* | Rib | Sear, roast to 63 °C |
  | *Lamsbout* | Leg | Roast |
  | *Lamsschouder*, *lamsnek* | Shoulder and neck | Braise, stew |

- **Pork today** is lean. A loin chop overcooked past about 70 °C dries out, so it is cooked to the safe figure
  (63 °C for 15 seconds, assessment §9) and rested; a trace of pink is normal.

**Sessions:** theory only (1.5 h): extend the cut-to-method card to pork and lamb.

**Cues:** the learner names the shoulder cuts of all three animals and says why they share a method.

**Faults:**
- Cooking pork until grey "to be safe".
- Roasting lamb neck fast.
- Confusing *procureur* with *karbonade*.

#### MEA-2.M2.L3 — Buying meat

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `MEA-2.M2.L1` · moves KM-PURC→2, IN-SEL→3*

*Objectives:* [J] butcher versus supermarket, labels, grading, ageing, price per portion (Good/Best)
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l3 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Butcher or supermarket:**
  - **a butcher** cuts to order, trims, ties, gives advice, and sells the less common cuts (cheek, *klapstuk*, bones);
  - **the supermarket** is cheaper per kilo for standard cuts and reliable for mince and chops.
- **Labels:**
  - ***Beter Leven*** stars;
  - **organic (*biologisch*);**
  - **origin:** the country of birth, rearing and slaughter;
  - **"*weidevlees*"** (pasture-raised);
  - **the use-by date.**
- **Quality signs:**
  - colour: bright, not grey or brown at the edges;
  - fine marbling in the cuts that need it;
  - firm, dry surfaces;
  - a clean smell;
  - no pooling of liquid in the pack.
- **Ageing:**
  - **dry-ageing** (weeks, unwrapped in a chilled room) concentrates the flavour and tenderises; it is expensive;
  - **wet-ageing** (in the vacuum pack) tenderises without concentrating.
- **Price per portion,** not per kilo, with bone and trim counted. Good and Best:
  - **Good:** supermarket chuck for stews, pork shoulder, a *kogelbiefstuk* sliced thin;
  - **Best:** a dry-aged *entrecote* or a pasture-raised joint from a butcher, once, for a guest dinner.

**Sessions:**
1. A first visit (45 min): to a butcher, with the questions of `MEA-2.M2.X1` written beforehand.

**Cues:** the learner can explain each purchase: why this cut, this quality and this price per portion.

**Faults:**
- Buying by the price per kilo alone.
- Not asking the butcher.
- Buying a steak for a stew.

### MEA-2.M3 — Steaks and chops

<!-- BEGIN generated:module-mea-2-m3 -->
*6.5 h · 3 lessons · 6 practical sessions · reaches TS-PRO 3, TS-DRY 3, JD-CUES 3*

*Objectives:* [S] cook steaks and chops to a chosen doneness; [J] judge by touch.

#### MEA-2.M3.L1 — Steak

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-2.M2.L2`, `MEA-2.M1.L2` · moves TS-PRO→3, TS-DRY→3*

*Objectives:* [S] temper, dry, sear, baste, rest, slice against the grain
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The sequence:**
  1. **Temper:** out of the fridge for 20–30 minutes, so the core starts less cold.
  2. **Dry and salt:** pat dry; salt well ahead (dry-brine, `SCI-2.M2.L2`) or just before cooking, not in between,
     when the surface is wet with drawn-out juice.
  3. **Sear:** a very hot, heavy pan with a little high-smoke-point oil; the steak is not moved until it releases with
     a crust. Turn it, and sear the edges and the fat cap too, so every outside surface changes colour (assessment §9).
  4. **Baste:** butter, garlic and thyme for the last minutes, spooned over.
  5. **Rest:** 5 minutes for a steak of 2–3 cm (`HEA-2.M2.L2`).
  6. **Slice** across the grain.
- **Thickness decides the method:**
  - **under about 2 cm:** in the pan only;
  - **thicker:** sear and finish in a moderate oven, or turn it often over medium heat for an even interior.
- **The stated doneness:** the learner states the target, chooses a pull temperature a few degrees below it, and checks
  the core after the rest.

**Sessions:**
1. A pan-only steak (45 min) of about 2 cm, to medium-rare; probed and cut.
2. A thick steak (45 min) of about 4 cm, seared and finished in the oven, to medium.

**Cues:**
- A deep brown crust.
- Fat edges rendered and golden.
- An even pink from edge to centre, with a thin grey band.
- The probe within 3 °C of the target after the rest.

**Faults:**
- A wet steak in a cold pan, so it greys and steams.
- Moving it too early.
- Edges left raw-looking.
- Slicing at once.
- Slicing with the grain.

#### MEA-2.M3.L2 — Pork and lamb chops

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l2 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-PRO→3*

*Objectives:* [S] render the fat edge, sear, finish; [K] safe pork temperatures
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The fat edge:** a chop is first stood on its fat edge in the pan, so the fat renders and crisps. Then the faces are
  seared, and the chop is finished over lower heat or in the oven.
- **Snipping the fat edge** every few centimetres stops the chop curling.
- **Safe pork and lamb temperatures:** 63 °C for 15 seconds at the core (assessment §9), reached after the rest. Pull
  a chop a degree or two below, because its carryover is small. A trace of pink at the bone is normal at that
  temperature. Lamb follows the same figure: served pink, not red.
- **Juiciness:** brine or dry-brine a lean pork chop (`POU-2.M6.L1`), and don't cook it past the figure by much.

**Sessions:**
1. Pork chops (45 min): render, sear, finish; probe after resting; compare one brined and one plain.
2. Lamb chops or a pork chop with the bone (45 min): the same sequence, with a quick pan sauce.

**Cues:**
- A crisp, golden fat edge.
- A chop that lies flat.
- The probe at or above 63 °C after the rest, and not far above it.

**Faults:**
- A pale, chewy fat edge.
- A chop that curls.
- Pork cooked to 75 °C and dry.
- Lamb served red.

#### MEA-2.M3.L3 — Doneness by touch

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l3 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M3.L1` · moves JD-CUES→3, TS-PRO→3*

*Objectives:* [J] touch, colour of juices and spring, calibrated against the thermometer
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l3 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The touch test:** press the centre of the steak with a fingertip:
  - rare yields softly, like the base of the thumb with the hand relaxed;
  - medium springs back;
  - well done is firm.

  The hand test is a guide; each learner calibrates it against the thermometer.
- **Other cues:**
  - **beads of juice** on the top surface: pink juice appears around medium-rare to medium, and clear juice later;
  - **the spring** after a press;
  - **the time** for the thickness, from the learner's own log.
- **The cue-then-reading entry** (`POU-2.M6.L2`):
  1. Predict the core from the cues.
  2. Write the prediction down.
  3. Probe, and log the difference.

  The aim is at least eight of ten within ±3 °C (`JD-CUES` level 3).

**Sessions:**
1. Calibrate the finger (45 min): three steaks or chops cooked to three dones; press, predict, probe, log.
2. Predict a meal (45 min): a weeknight steak or chop; predict and probe at the pull and after the rest.

**Cues:** predictions that converge on the readings over the sessions.

**Faults:**
- Pressing at the edge.
- Pressing with a fork.
- Probing before predicting.
- Trusting the hand test on a cut never tried before.

#### MEA-2.M4.L1 — Roasting a joint

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m4-l1 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 3 h · builds on `HEA-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-DRY→3, SC-BRWN→3*

*Objectives:* [S] beef or pork joint: sear-first versus reverse sear; [K] edge-to-centre gradient
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m4-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The edge-to-centre gradient:** in a hot oven the outer layers overshoot long before the centre arrives. The result
  is a thick grey band (`HEA-2.M1`). A lower oven narrows the gradient but browns less.
- **Two answers:**
  - **sear-first:** brown the joint in a hot pan, then roast at a moderate heat;
  - **reverse sear:** roast in a low oven until the core is a few degrees below the target, rest, then sear hard in a
    very hot pan or oven.

  The reverse sear gives a more even interior and a crust at the last moment. The sear-first is quicker and gives more
  fond for a gravy.
- **Safe roasting** (assessment §9): a whole roast reaches its core figure from the time-temperature table, for
  example:
  - 60 °C held for 12 minutes;
  - 62.8 °C held for 4 minutes.

  The holding time may include the rise after the oven, so the learner logs the core every 5 minutes after it comes
  out. Pork and lamb joints reach at least 63 °C. A rare roast below the table is not taught at this stage.
- **Browning** (`SC-BRWN`):
  - a dry surface and a hot pan;
  - the joint not crowded;
  - the fond brown, not black.

  Onions caramelised in the roasting fat go into the gravy.

**Sessions:**
1. The comparison (60 min active, about 3 hours in total): two small joints of the same cut, such as pork loin or beef
   topside of about 600 g each. One is seared first, the other reverse-seared, both to the same target. The core is
   logged after the oven, and the two cross-sections are photographed side by side.

**Cues:**
- An even, deep brown crust.
- A thin grey band on the reverse-seared joint.
- The core log meeting the table.

**Faults:**
- A wet joint going into the pan.
- Searing in too small a pan.
- Carving straight from the oven.
- Pulling a joint at the target, so it overshoots.

### MEA-2.M5 — Minced meat

#### MEA-2.M5.L1 — Mince safety

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m5-l1 -->
*1 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SAF-1.M3.L2` · moves SA-TEMP→4, SA-HYG→4*

*Objectives:* [K] why mince carries more risk than whole cuts; [S] storage, handling and core temperature
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m5-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Why mince carries more risk:** bacteria live on the surface of meat. In a steak they stay there and are killed by
  the sear. Mincing spreads the surface through the whole mass, so the centre of a burger is as contaminated as the
  outside.
- **The figure:** minced meat reaches 75 °C at the core (assessment §9), the Voedingscentrum's figure. The Food Code
  also allows 68 °C for 17 seconds, 66 °C for 1 minute or 63 °C for 3 minutes, but the program uses 75 °C because it
  needs no timing. A pink centre in a burger is never "medium".
- **Storage:**
  - mince in the fridge at 4 °C, on the bottom shelf;
  - used by its use-by date, or frozen on the day it is bought;
  - thawed in the fridge, not on the counter.
- **Handling:**
  - mince is mixed and shaped by hand, so the work order puts it last (`KNF-2.M2`);
  - hands are washed after shaping and before touching anything else;
  - the mixing bowl, board and tray go straight into the hot wash.

  Gloves don't replace hand washing.
- **Cooked mince dishes** (*ragù*, meatballs in sauce) are cooled and stored like any batch: 57 °C to 21 °C within
  2 hours, to 5 °C within 6 hours, and reheated to 74 °C (§9).

**Sessions:** theory only (1 h): write the work order and the safety plan for the meatball comparison of
`MEA-2.M5.X1`.

**Cues:** a plan with mince last, hand washing marked at each change, and the cooling of the leftovers planned.

**Faults:**
- Serving a pink burger.
- Thawing mince on the counter.
- Shaping meatballs and then touching the salt jar.
- Probing only the edge of a burger.

#### MEA-2.M5.L2 — Burgers and meatballs

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m5-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M5.L1` · moves TS-PRO→3, FL-TEXT→2*

*Objectives:* [S] fat content, salt timing, *panade*, gentle mixing; [J] texture choices
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m5-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Fat content:**
  - lean beef mince (about 10 % fat) dries out in a burger;
  - about 20 % fat, or half beef and half pork (*half-om-half*), stays juicy.
- **Salt timing:**
  - salt mixed into mince dissolves proteins that bind it, which gives a springy, sausage-like texture: right for a
    meatball that has to hold together, wrong for a tender burger;
  - burgers are salted on the surface just before cooking.
- **A *panade*:** bread soaked in milk, mixed into the meat. It holds moisture and interrupts the protein network, so
  meatballs stay tender and juicy.
- **Gentle mixing:**
  - just until combined;
  - shaped lightly with cold, wet hands;
  - overworking makes mince tough.
- **Texture choices:**
  - a loose, coarse burger;
  - a tender meatball with a *panade*;
  - a firm, springy one for a soup.
- **Doneness:**
  - the probe goes into the centre of the thickest piece: 75 °C;
  - a dimple pressed into the centre of a burger stops it doming.

**Sessions:**
1. Burgers (60 min): two burgers from 20 % fat mince, one salted in the mix and one on the surface; probed to 75 °C;
   cut and compared.
2. Meatballs (60 min): a batch of meatballs browned and finished in a tomato sauce; probed to 75 °C.

**Cues:**
- A juicy, loosely textured burger.
- Meatballs that hold their shape but are tender inside.
- 75 °C at the centre.

**Faults:**
- Lean mince for burgers.
- Salt mixed into burger mince hours ahead.
- Overworked, dense meatballs.
- A burger that domes and cooks unevenly.

#### MEA-2.M6.L1 — Stewing beef

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m6-l1 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 3 h · builds on `HEA-2.M5.L1` · moves TS-CMB→2*

*Objectives:* [S] cut choice, browning in batches, liquid, low heat; [J] judge tenderness
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m6-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The cut:** collagen-rich chuck (*sukadelappen*, *runderlappen*), *klapstuk* or cheek. Lean cuts dry out and turn
  stringy in a stew. Large pieces of 4–5 cm hold together.
- **Browning in batches:**
  - pat the meat dry;
  - brown it in a hot casserole, a few pieces at a time, with space between them;
  - each batch leaves fond.

  A crowded pot steams the meat grey.
- **The liquid** comes about two thirds of the way up the meat. A covered pot in a low oven (about 150–160 °C) or on
  the lowest flame barely bubbles. A rolling boil toughens the meat before the collagen softens (`HEA-2.M5.L1`).
- **Judging tenderness:**
  - a piece yields to a fork with little pressure and pulls apart along its fibres;
  - it is not falling to threads.

  This usually takes 2.5–3.5 hours; the time is a guide, the fork decides.
- **Better the next day:** the stew is cooled within the limits of assessment §9, chilled, and the fat is lifted off.

**Sessions:**
1. A stew (60 min active, about 3 hours in total): brown in batches, deglaze, braise in the oven, and test with a fork
   from the second hour.

**Cues:**
- A deep brown sear on every batch.
- A liquid that barely moves.
- Meat that yields to a fork.

**Faults:**
- Crowding the pot.
- Lean meat.
- A boil.
- Stopping on the clock rather than the fork.
- A thin, greasy sauce.

#### MEA-2.M6.L2 — Building flavour in a stew

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m6-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `STK-2.M1.L1` · moves FL-ARCH→2, FL-SEAS→3*

*Objectives:* [S] aromatics, fond, wine or beer, herbs, finishing acid; [J] season at the end
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m6-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The layers:**
  1. **The fond** of the browned meat.
  2. **An aromatic base** (`STK-2.M1.L1`): onions, carrot and celery sweated or browned in the same pot.
  3. **Tomato purée,** fried until it darkens.
  4. **The deglazing liquid,** and the cooking liquid:
     - beer for a Flemish *stoofvlees*;
     - red wine for a *bœuf bourguignon*;
     - stock.
  5. **Herbs and spices:** bay, thyme and cloves; for *stoofvlees*, a slice of bread with mustard and a little syrup or
     brown sugar.
  6. **A finishing acid:** a splash of vinegar or mustard at the end, to lift the richness.
- **Season at the end** (`FL-SEAS`): the liquid reduces, so most of the salt goes in during the last half hour,
  weighed and noted, tasted with the calibrated form.
- **The sauce:** reduced uncovered at the end, or thickened by the bread or a little *beurre manié*, until it coats a
  spoon.

**Sessions:**
1. Finish and season (45 min): reduce the sauce of the stew from `MEA-2.M6.L1`; season it in steps, weighing the salt;
   add the finishing acid; taste on the form.

**Cues:**
- A glossy sauce that coats the meat.
- A balance where the richness is lifted by acid.
- The salt noted in grams.

**Faults:**
- Salting at the start.
- A flat, heavy stew without acid.
- Burnt tomato purée.
- Unreduced, watery liquid.

## Introduction

Baking is the part of cooking where the cook can't taste and adjust along the way. A cake is right or wrong when it
comes out of the oven. Three things decide it:
- **the formula:** the proportions of flour, sugar, fat, eggs, liquid and leavener;
- **the method:** how they are combined;
- **the oven:** how it sets them.

So baking asks for precision (weighed, never measured in cups) and for understanding what each ingredient does,
because that understanding is what lets a baker adjust a formula and diagnose a failure.

`BAK-2` builds that understanding:
- **the fundamentals:** baker's percentages, the function of each ingredient, tins, ovens and allergens;
- **quick breads and batters:** the muffin method, pancakes and scones, and chemical leaveners;
- **shortcrust:** *pâte brisée* and *pâte sucrée*, rolled, lined and blind-baked without shrinking;
- **cakes:** the creaming and foaming methods, diagnosing faults from the crumb, and a filled and finished layer cake;
- **custards and creams:** *crème pâtissière*, *crème anglaise*, baked custards and whipped cream;
- **biscuits:** a test of one variable, and a review of the course's baking log.

**The cross-section** is the evidence of baking. Every bake is cut and photographed against reference images:
- the crumb of a cake;
- the base and edge of a tart;
- the rise of a muffin.

The learner bands their own result before the AI review and the tasters do, which trains judgment (`JD-REFL`).

**In the spiral.**
- `TS-BAK` reaches level 3 with four of its seven elements: quick breads, shortcrust, cakes and pastry cream. Choux
  and laminated pastry come in `PST-3`.
- `IN-BAK` and `KM-MATH` reach level 3; the bread formulas of `BRD-2` use the same percentages.
- The custards complete `TS-EGG` level 4 and `IN-DAI` level 3, after `SAU-2` and `LEG-2`.

**Pacing.** About seven weeks, the longest course of S2. Most bakes fit a weekend afternoon; the custards and pastry
need resting time in the fridge. Ingredients are cheap, and the bakes are shared with the household, neighbours or
colleagues, who become the tasters.

#### BAK-2.M1.L1 — Baker's percentages

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `WRK-1.M3.L3` · moves KM-MATH→3, RD-RATO→2*

*Objectives:* [S] express and scale a formula in baker's %; [K] why baking needs precision
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Why precision:** a few grams of baking powder or salt, or 10 % more liquid, change a bake visibly, and once it is
  in the oven nothing can be corrected. Everything is weighed on the scale (1 g), and leaveners and salt on the
  precision scale (0.1 g) where the quantity is small (`TST-1`). Liquids are weighed too: 1 ml of water weighs 1 g.
- **Baker's percentages:**
  - the total flour is 100 %;
  - every other ingredient is a percentage of the flour's weight.

  A formula of 500 g of flour, 325 g of water, 10 g of salt and 5 g of yeast is 100 %, 65 %, 2 % and 1 %. The
  percentages show the character of a formula at a glance:
  - **hydration**, the water as a percentage of the flour;
  - **sweetness;**
  - **richness.**
- **Scaling with baker's percentages:**
  1. Choose the flour weight, or work it out from the total dough weight: the total divided by the sum of the
     percentages.
  2. Multiply each percentage by the flour weight.
- **Ratios as a memory aid** (`RD-RATO`):
  - pound cake 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 (flour, butter, sugar, eggs);
  - shortcrust 2 : 1 (flour to fat);
  - a basic biscuit 3 : 2 : 1 (flour, fat, sugar).

  A ratio card gives a formula from memory, and baker's percentages turn it into grams.
- **Eggs in formulas:** a medium egg is about 50 g without the shell (yolk about 18 g, white about 32 g). Eggs are
  weighed, not counted.

**Sessions:**
1. Convert and scale (45 min): a pound cake and a bread formula written in baker's percentages, and scaled to a
   different tin size and a different dough weight.

**Cues:** formulas written in a table with grams and percentages; the percentages add up to the stated total; the
scaled formula fits the tin.

**Faults:**
- Measuring flour by volume.
- Percentages of the total rather than of the flour.
- Counting eggs instead of weighing them.
- Rounding leaveners to the nearest 5 g.

#### BAK-2.M1.L2 — What each ingredient does

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M3.L2` · moves IN-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [K] flour (protein), sugar, fats, eggs, liquids, chemical leaveners, salt
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Flour:** starch for structure, and protein that forms gluten with water and mixing (`SCI-2.M3.L2`).

  | Flour (NL) | Protein | Use |
  |---|---|---|
  | Cake flour (*zeeuwse bloem*, patent flour) | Low, about 8–10 % | Cakes, tender pastry, biscuits |
  | Plain or all-purpose (*tarwebloem*) | About 10–11 % | Most baking |
  | Bread flour (*broodmeel*, *tarwebloem* for bread) | High, about 12–14 % | Bread, chewy textures |
  | Wholemeal (*volkorenmeel*) | Protein plus bran | Bread, rustic bakes; absorbs more water |

- **Sugar:** sweetness and more:
  - it holds water, so a bake stays moist;
  - it tenderises, because it competes with gluten and starch for water;
  - it browns (`SC-BRWN`);
  - in creaming, it traps air.
- **Fats:**
  - **butter** gives flavour, and its water turns to steam;
  - **oil** gives a moist, tender crumb;
  - **solid fat in flakes** gives flaky layers.

  Fat coats flour and shortens gluten: hence "shortcrust".
- **Eggs:** structure (the proteins set), emulsifying (the yolk), leavening (a whipped foam), colour and richness.
- **Liquids:** they hydrate flour and starch, dissolve sugar and salt, and make steam.
- **Chemical leaveners:**
  - **baking soda** (sodium bicarbonate) needs an acid in the formula, such as buttermilk, yoghurt, honey or brown
    sugar, and works at once;
  - **baking powder** is soda with its own acid, and most is double-acting: some gas on mixing, more in the oven.

  Too much leavener gives a coarse crumb, a soapy taste and a collapse.
- **Salt:** flavour, and in bread it controls yeast and tightens gluten.

**Sessions:**
1. The flour comparison (50 min): one muffin formula baked with cake flour and with bread flour, cut and photographed
   in cross-section, as part of `BAK-2.M1.X1`.

**Cues:** the learner explains each ingredient's job in a formula before baking it.

**Faults:**
- Bread flour in a tender cake.
- Swapping soda and powder one for one.
- Cutting the sugar in half "for health", so the cake turns dry and pale.

#### BAK-2.M1.L3 — Tins, ovens and allergens

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAF-1.M4.L1` · moves SA-ALLG→3, KM-EQPT→3*

*Objectives:* [S] prepare tins; check the oven; [K] gluten, egg, milk and nuts in baking; cross-contact
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l3 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Preparing tins:**
  - grease and line them with baking paper (base and sides for long bakes);
  - butter and flour fluted tins.

  The tin size changes the depth and so the bake time: a smaller tin means a deeper cake and a longer, lower bake.
- **Tin care:** carbon-steel and tinned tins rust if left wet. They are dried in the cooling oven and kept lightly
  oiled. The cast-iron skillet of `EQP-1` is cared for the same way: seasoned with a thin layer of oil baked on.
- **The oven:**
  - preheat for at least 15–20 minutes;
  - check it with the oven thermometer (`EQP-1`), because a dial can be off by 10–20 °C or more;
  - know its hot spots (`EQP-1.M2`);
  - fan ovens bake hotter and faster: about 20 °C lower than conventional.
- **Checking the scale:** a litre of water weighs 1000 g, and a 500 g pack of butter about 500 g. The results go into
  the calibration log (`KM-EQPT`).
- **Allergens in baking** (`SA-ALLG`):
  - **the usual allergens:** gluten (wheat, spelt, rye, barley, oats), egg, milk, nuts, peanuts, soy, sesame and
    lupin (some flours);
  - **hidden sources:** chocolate (milk, soy), marzipan (almonds), some baking powders (wheat starch), sprinkles;
  - **cross-contact:** flour dust travels, so an allergen-free bake is made first, with washed bowls, tins and
    utensils, and stored apart.

**Sessions:**
1. Tins and oven (40 min): line a round tin and a loaf tin; place the oven thermometer and compare it with the dial at
   180 °C; weigh a litre of water; season or oil the carbon-steel tins and the skillet, and photograph them.

**Cues:**
- Tins that release cleanly.
- The oven offset written on a label on the oven.
- No rust.

**Faults:**
- Baking in a cold oven.
- Trusting the dial.
- A rusted tin.
- Flour from an ordinary bake dusting an allergen-free one.

#### BAK-2.M2.L1 — The muffin method

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m2-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M1.L2` · moves TS-BAK→2*

*Objectives:* [S] wet into dry, minimal mixing; [K] soda versus powder and their acids
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m2-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The method:**
  1. Whisk the dry ingredients together: flour, leavener, salt, and sugar where it is dry.
  2. Whisk the wet ones together: eggs, milk or buttermilk, melted butter or oil.
  3. Pour the wet into the dry.
  4. Fold with a spatula in a dozen strokes, until just combined. A few streaks and lumps remain.
- **Why minimal mixing:** more mixing develops gluten, and the muffins come out tough, peaked and full of tunnels.
- **Soda versus powder:**
  - **soda** reacts at once with the acid, so the batter is baked without delay;
  - **double-acting powder** gives a second rise in the oven, so the batter can wait a little.

  A formula with an acid ingredient and too little soda bakes dense and tastes sour; too much soda tastes soapy and
  browns too fast.
- **Baking:** a hot start (about 200 °C) for a domed top, then lower. Done when a skewer comes out clean and the top
  springs back.

**Sessions:**
1. Mixed right and mixed wrong (45 min): two batches of the same muffin formula, one folded in a dozen strokes and one
   beaten for a minute; cut and photographed.
2. Soda and powder (45 min): a buttermilk muffin with soda, and a milk muffin with powder; compare the rise and taste.

**Cues:**
- A lumpy batter that is barely mixed.
- Muffins with rounded tops and an even, tender crumb, without tunnels.

**Faults:**
- Overmixing.
- Batter left standing too long with soda.
- An oven not up to heat.
- Overfilling the cups.

#### BAK-2.M2.L2 — Pancakes and scones

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m2-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] pancakes; rubbing in for scones; [J] judge the batter and dough
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m2-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Pancakes:**
  - **thick pancakes:** the muffin method, with a thicker batter that is rested briefly;
  - **Dutch *pannenkoeken*** and *crêpes*: a thin batter whisked smooth and rested for 30 minutes, so the gluten relaxes
    and the starch hydrates.

  A medium-hot pan, lightly greased. The first one tests the heat.
- **Rubbing in (scones):**
  1. Cut cold butter into the flour and rub it with the fingertips until it looks like breadcrumbs. The fat coats the
     flour and stays in small pieces, which make steam and flakiness.
  2. Add the liquid and bring it together with a knife.
  3. Pat it out without kneading.
  4. Cut straight down, without twisting, so the scones rise evenly.
- **Judging the dough:**
  - a scone dough is soft and slightly sticky, not dry;
  - a pancake batter coats a spoon and flows.

  Each is adjusted by a spoon of flour or liquid, not by kneading.

**Sessions:**
1. Pancakes (45 min): a thick pancake batter and a thin *pannenkoek* batter; cook a stack of each for breakfast or
   dinner (with seasonal toppings: leek and cheese, or bacon and *stroop*).
2. Scones (45 min): rubbed-in scones, cut and baked; one scone twisted when cut, to see the uneven rise.

**Cues:**
- An even golden colour on the pancakes, with bubbles that burst before turning.
- Tall scones with straight sides and a split middle.

**Faults:**
- A cold pan, so the pancakes turn pale and greasy.
- Butter rubbed in until it melts.
- Kneaded scone dough.
- Twisting the cutter.

#### BAK-2.M3.L1 — *Pâte brisée*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 h · builds on `BAK-2.M1.L2` · moves TS-BAK→2*

*Objectives:* [S] rub in, add water sparingly, rest; [K] gluten and fat in pastry
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The formula:** about 2 : 1 flour to cold butter by weight, a pinch of salt, and just enough cold water (about
  20–25 % of the flour) to bring it together. An egg yolk adds richness and makes it easier to handle.
- **Rubbing in:** cold butter cut into the flour by fingertips, a pastry cutter or a few pulses of a food processor,
  until it looks like coarse crumbs with some pea-sized pieces left.
- **Water sparingly:** added a spoon at a time and mixed with a knife or fork until the dough just holds when squeezed.
  More water develops more gluten, and the pastry turns tough and shrinks.
- **Gluten and fat:** fat coats the flour and shortens the gluten strands. The more evenly the butter is rubbed in, the
  more tender and short the pastry; the bigger the pieces, the flakier.
- **Resting:** the dough is pressed into a flat disc, wrapped and chilled for at least an hour. The gluten relaxes and
  the butter firms, so the pastry rolls easily and shrinks less.

**Sessions:**
1. *Pâte brisée* (45 min, plus an hour's rest): one batch rubbed in by hand; the dough's water noted; rested and frozen
   or kept for `BAK-2.M3.L2`.

**Cues:**
- Coarse crumbs with visible butter.
- A dough that just holds together.
- No greasy, sticky patches.

**Faults:**
- Warm hands melting the butter.
- Too much water.
- Kneading.
- Skipping the rest.

#### BAK-2.M3.L2 — Rolling, lining and blind baking

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] roll evenly, line a tin, blind bake; [J] prevent shrinking and soggy bases
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Rolling:**
  - on a lightly floured surface, from the centre outwards;
  - turn the dough a quarter after every few rolls, so it stays round and doesn't stick;
  - an even 3–4 mm.
- **Lining:**
  1. Roll the pastry over the pin and drape it over the tin.
  2. Ease it into the corners without stretching it, because stretched pastry shrinks back.
  3. Leave a little overhang.
  4. Chill the lined tin for 20–30 minutes.
- **Blind baking:**
  1. Line the pastry with baking paper and fill it with baking beans, dried beans or rice.
  2. Bake at about 180–190 °C until the edges are set and pale gold.
  3. Remove the beans and bake again until the base is dry and golden.

  Trim the overhang with a knife after baking, for a clean edge.
- **Preventing shrinking and soggy bases:**
  - **against shrinking:** a rested, unstretched dough, chilled in the tin;
  - **against a soggy base:** a fully blind-baked base, brushed with egg wash in the last minutes to seal it, a hot
    oven shelf, and the filling added just before baking.

**Sessions:**
1. Line and blind bake (60 min): roll and line a 23 cm tin; chill; blind bake fully; photograph the base and edge.
2. Fix a fault (45 min): line a second tin, stretching the pastry and skipping the chill on purpose; blind bake;
   compare the shrinking.

**Cues:**
- An even thickness.
- The edges standing up straight after baking.
- A dry, golden base without cracks.

**Faults:**
- Stretching the pastry into the tin.
- Rolling unevenly, so it bakes unevenly.
- Removing the beans too early, so the sides slump.
- An underbaked, pale base.

#### BAK-2.M3.L3 — *Pâte sucrée*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l3 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 h · builds on `BAK-2.M3.L2` · moves TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] the creaming method for sweet pastry; handle a soft dough
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l3 -->

**Concepts:**
- ***Pâte sucrée*** is a sweet, crisp, biscuit-like pastry, made by the creaming method:
  1. Cream soft butter with sugar.
  2. Beat in egg.
  3. Mix in the flour just until combined.

  The sugar and the creaming give a fine, crisp crumb.
- **A soft dough:** chill it well before rolling, and roll it between two sheets of baking paper. Patch any cracks
  with off-cuts, because *sucrée* forgives patching.
- **Baking:** at a slightly lower heat than *brisée* (about 170–180 °C), because the sugar browns quickly.

**Sessions:**
1. *Pâte sucrée* (45 min, plus an hour's rest): creamed, chilled, rolled between paper, lined and frozen for
   `BAK-2.M3.X2`.

**Cues:**
- A smooth, soft dough that firms in the fridge.
- Rolled without tearing between the papers.

**Faults:**
- Rolling the dough warm.
- Over-mixing after the flour.
- Baking too hot, so the edge burns.

#### BAK-2.M4.L1 — The creaming method

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M1.L2` · moves TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] cream butter and sugar; add eggs without splitting; fold in flour
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Creaming:**
  1. Beat soft butter (cool to the touch, about 18–20 °C) with sugar for 3–5 minutes, until pale and fluffy. The sugar
     crystals cut air pockets into the fat, and that air is the start of the rise.
  2. Add the eggs at room temperature, one at a time or in a stream, beating well after each. The yolk's emulsifiers
     hold the water of the egg in the fat. Cold eggs or adding them too fast split the mixture, which looks curdled.
     A spoon of the flour rescues it.
  3. Fold in the flour (and any liquid) gently, just until combined, so the air stays in and little gluten develops.
- **Butter cakes:** pound cake (*cake* in Dutch), *boterkoek*-style, a Victoria sponge. The baking powder supports the
  creamed air.
- **Done:** a skewer in the centre comes out clean, the top springs back, and the cake shrinks just away from the tin.
  It cools in the tin for 10 minutes, then on a rack.

**Sessions:**
1. A pound cake (60 min plus baking): creamed and weighed; one test of creaming time (1 minute against 5 minutes) in
   two small loaf tins.
2. A Victoria-style sponge (60 min plus baking): creamed, in two tins, for the layer cake practice.

**Cues:**
- Butter and sugar pale and fluffy.
- A smooth, uncurdled mixture after the eggs.
- A flat or gently domed top with an even crumb.

**Faults:**
- Cold butter that won't cream.
- Melted butter.
- Eggs from the fridge.
- Overmixing after the flour.
- Opening the oven early.

#### BAK-2.M4.L2 — Sponge (*génoise*)

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M4.L1` · moves TS-BAK→3, SC-EMUL→3*

*Objectives:* [S] whisk eggs to the ribbon; fold without losing air; [K] foams
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **A foam does the rising.** In a *génoise*:
  1. Whisk whole eggs and sugar, warmed over a bain-marie to about body temperature, then off the heat, until they
     triple in volume and reach the ribbon stage (`SAU-2.M5.L1`).
  2. Sift and fold in the flour.
  3. Fold in a little melted butter.

  There is no chemical leavener: the air in the egg foam expands in the oven, and the egg proteins set around it.
- **Foams** (`SC-EMUL`): a foam is gas dispersed in a liquid, stabilised by proteins. Sugar stabilises an egg foam;
  fat and rough folding collapse it. Warming helps the sugar dissolve and the eggs to whisk higher.
- **Folding:** a large spatula, cutting down through the middle and turning the batter over, while turning the bowl.
  The flour is added in two or three parts. The melted butter is first mixed with a little batter, then folded in.
- **Baking:** straight into a lined tin and into the oven at once, because the foam starts to collapse. Done when it
  springs back and shrinks from the sides.

**Sessions:**
1. Whisk to the ribbon (60 min): whisk eggs and sugar warmed and unwarmed; compare the volume and the ribbon.
2. A *génoise* (60 min plus baking): folded, baked, cut through and photographed.

**Cues:**
- A pale, thick foam that holds a ribbon for a few seconds.
- A batter that stays light after folding.
- An even, fine, springy crumb.

**Faults:**
- Under-whipped eggs.
- Stirring rather than folding.
- Butter poured in hot or all at once.
- A batter left waiting.

#### BAK-2.M4.L3 — Diagnosing cakes

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l3 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `BAK-2.M4.L2` · moves JD-TRBL→3, FL-TEXT→2*

*Objectives:* [J] sunken, domed, dense, dry, tunnelled: causes and fixes
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l3 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Reading the crumb:**

  | Fault | Likely causes | Fix next time |
  |---|---|---|
  | Sunken centre | Too much leavener or sugar; oven door opened early; underbaked | Weigh the leavener; check the oven; bake to the skewer |
  | High dome, cracked top | Oven too hot; too much flour; too much mixing | Lower the heat (check with the thermometer); weigh the flour; fold less |
  | Dense, heavy | Under-creamed; a collapsed foam; too little leavener; curdled batter | Cream longer; fold gently; weigh |
  | Dry | Overbaked; too little fat or sugar; too much flour | Bake to the skewer; weigh |
  | Tunnels | Overmixing after the flour | Fold just to combine |
  | Sticky or sinking edges | Underbaked | Bake longer; check the oven |

- **One fault, one change:** the cause is diagnosed from the crumb and the log (time, oven reading, weights). The next

  or taster band.

**Sessions:**

#### BAK-2.M4.L4 — Buttercreams, fillings and finishing a cake

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l4 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `BAK-2.M4.L2` · moves TS-BAK→3, JD-TRBL→3*

*Objectives:* [S] American and Swiss meringue buttercream; [S] split, fill, crumb-coat and finish a layer cake; [K] why buttercream splits and how to rescue it
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l4 -->

**Concepts:**
- **American buttercream:** soft butter beaten with icing sugar and a little milk. It is quick, sweet and stable.
- **Swiss meringue buttercream:**
  1. Whisk egg whites and sugar over a bain-marie until the sugar dissolves and the mixture is hot, about 70 °C on the
     probe.
  2. Whip it to a stiff, glossy meringue, and cool it to room temperature.
  3. Beat in soft butter piece by piece.

  It is less sweet and silky.
- **Why it splits and how to rescue it:** buttercream is an emulsion of fat and the water in the meringue.
  - **Too cold** (the butter or the meringue): it curdles and looks lumpy. Warm the bowl briefly over the bain-marie or
    with a warm towel, and beat.
  - **Too warm:** it turns soupy. Chill it for 15 minutes, and beat again.
- **Finishing a layer cake:**
  1. Level the cooled cakes and split them if needed.
  2. Fill them: buttercream, a compote or a cream.
  3. Apply a thin crumb coat, which seals in the crumbs, and chill.
  4. Apply a final coat smoothed with a palette knife.

  Chilling between steps makes each easier.

**Sessions:**
1. Buttercream and a cake (75 min, over two days): a Swiss meringue buttercream, deliberately split by adding butter
   that is too cold, then rescued; the sponges of `BAK-2.M4.L1` filled, crumb-coated, chilled overnight and finished.

**Cues:**
- A silky buttercream that holds a peak.
- A level cake with straight sides.
- No crumbs in the final coat.

**Faults:**
- Throwing away a split buttercream.
- Filling warm cakes.
- Skipping the crumb coat.
- Buttercream too warm to spread.

#### BAK-2.M5.L1 — *Crème pâtissière*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `EGG-1.M4.L1`, `SCI-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-EGG→4, TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] temper, boil to set the starch, cool safely; [K] why it must boil and a *crème anglaise* mustn't
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- ***Crème pâtissière*** (pastry cream): milk, yolks, sugar and a starch (cornflour or flour), boiled to a thick, smooth
  cream.
- **The method:**
  1. Heat the milk.
  2. Whisk yolks, sugar and starch.
  3. Temper them with the hot milk.
  4. Return everything to the pan and whisk constantly as it comes to the boil.
  5. Boil for 1–2 minutes.
- **Why it must boil:** the starch must gelatinise fully to thicken and lose its raw taste. The boil also destroys an
  enzyme in the yolk (amylase) that would otherwise digest the starch and thin the cream overnight. The starch protects
  the yolk from curdling.
- **Why *crème anglaise* mustn't boil:** with no starch, the yolks set and curdle above about 84 °C. It is stirred to
  *nappe*, about 80–84 °C (`EGG-1.M4.L1`), then strained into a cold bowl.
- **Cooling safely:**
  1. Spread the cream in a shallow tray.
  2. Cover the surface with film, against a skin.
  3. Cool it from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C within 6 hours in total (assessment §9).
  4. Keep it at most 2 days.

**Sessions:**
1. *Crème pâtissière* (45 min): boiled for the full time; cooled in a tray with the temperature logged.
2. *Crème anglaise* (40 min): stirred to *nappe*, the temperature probed, strained and cooled in an ice bath; served
   with a baked fruit or the cake of `BAK-2.M4`.

**Cues:**
- A pastry cream thick, glossy and smooth after the boil.
- A *crème anglaise* that coats the spoon with a clean line through it, without grains.

**Faults:**
- Pastry cream taken off before it boils, so it thins overnight.
- Lumps from not whisking into the corners.
- A *crème anglaise* boiled until it curdles.
- A deep bowl of hot cream in the fridge.

#### BAK-2.M5.L2 — Baked custards

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 h · builds on `EGG-1.M4.L1` · moves TS-EGG→4*

*Objectives:* [S] *crème caramel* and a baked custard; water bath; [J] judge the wobble
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **A baked custard:** eggs, milk or cream and sugar, set in the oven without starch. Examples are *crème caramel*,
  *crème brûlée* and *pots de crème*.
- **The water bath:** ramekins in a deep tin of hot water in a moderate oven (about 150–160 °C). The water never goes
  above 100 °C, so the custard sets gently and evenly, without bubbles or curdling.
- ***Crème caramel*:** a dry or wet caramel poured into the moulds first. The custard sets on it, and the caramel
  dissolves into a sauce in the fridge. It is chilled for at least 4 hours before unmoulding.
- **Judging the wobble:** done when the edges are set and the centre wobbles like a jelly, not a liquid, when the
  ramekin is nudged. It sets further as it cools. A knife in the centre comes out with a thin coat.
- **Adapting:** a larger dish takes longer than ramekins at the same heat. The learner adapts the timing and checks the
  wobble.

**Sessions:**
1. *Crème caramel* (60 min, plus 4 hours' chilling): caramel, custard and water bath; four ramekins and one larger
   dish; the wobble judged and the times logged; unmoulded.

**Cues:**
- A smooth, glossy custard with no bubbles.
- A clean unmoulding with a pool of caramel.
- An even texture right through when cut.

**Faults:**
- No water bath, so the custard bubbles and curdles.
- Baking until the centre is firm, so it turns grainy and weeps.
- A burnt caramel.

#### BAK-2.M5.L3 — Whipped cream

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M4.L1` · moves IN-DAI→3*

*Objectives:* [S] soft, medium and stiff peaks; *chantilly*; [J] rescue over-whipped cream
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l3 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Whipping:** cream of at least 30 % fat (Dutch *slagroom*, about 35 %), cold, in a cold bowl. The fat droplets
  partly crystallise and join into a network around the air bubbles (`SCI-2.M4.L1`).
- **The peaks:**
  - **soft:** the peak flops over; for folding into mousses and serving with cake;
  - **medium:** the tip bends; for filling;
  - **stiff:** it stands up; for piping and stabilising.
- ***Chantilly*:** whipped with a little icing sugar and vanilla.
- **Rescuing over-whipped cream:** when it turns grainy, fold in a few spoons of liquid cream by hand. Taken much
  further, it becomes butter and buttermilk, which can be used.
- **Stabilising** for a filling: a spoon of *mascarpone*, or *kwark* folded in.

**Sessions:**
1. Peaks and a rescue (30 min): whip cream to soft, medium and stiff peaks, photographing each; over-whip a portion
   and rescue it.

**Cues:**
- Peaks that match the target.
- A smooth, glossy surface.
- The rescued cream smooth again.

**Faults:**
- Warm cream that won't whip.
- Low-fat cream.
- An electric whisk left running until it turns grainy.

## Introduction

In S2 the learner cut a chicken into eight pieces following a video, filleted a round fish and portioned meat. `BUT-3`
takes away the guidance and adds what a home cook gains most from:
- **whole birds:** supremes, a boned leg, a fully boned chicken, and a duck broken down into breasts, legs, fat and
  carcass;
- **whole fish:** round and flat fish filleted cleanly, with the frames and heads used;
- **joints:** trimmed, tied, butterflied, stuffed and rolled for roasting;
- **yield and cost:** what a usable kilo really costs, and when buying whole pays.

Buying whole is cheaper per usable kilo, gives better fresh quality, and leaves bones and trim for stocks, fat for
cooking and skin for crisping. It also takes skill and time, which this course builds and then prices honestly in the
yield tests.

Meat is from Dutch farms (a free-range chicken or duck from a poulterer, Dutch pork and lamb). Fish is whole, from a
fishmonger: North Sea round fish (mackerel, whiting) or Dutch-farmed trout, and North Sea flat fish (plaice, dab),
MSC-labelled or in season.

**In the spiral.**
- `TS-FAB` reaches level 3 across three modules: the bird in `BUT-3.M1`, the flat fish in `BUT-3.M2`, the tied roast
  in `BUT-3.M3`.
- `KM-MATH` reaches level 4 and `KM-PURC` level 3 in the yield tests; `KMG-3` takes costing to a whole week.
- `KM-WAST` reaches level 4 with a week planned around a whole fish or bird.
- `MS-S3` re-tests the fabrication on its prep day (`MS-S3.M1.L2`) and the costing in its week plan (`MS-S3.M1.L1`).

**Pacing.** About four weeks, cooking what is cut as household meals. Each bird and fish is bought for a planned week of
dishes, so nothing waits.

#### BUT-3.M1.L1 — Supremes and a *ballotine* introduced

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m1-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `POU-2.M2.L2` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] cut supremes with the wing bone; bone a leg for stuffing
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m1-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The supreme** (*suprême*): a breast with the first wing joint attached and the skin on. The wing tip and middle
  joint are cut off; the bone is cleaned (*French-trimmed*) by scraping the meat and sinew back.
- **Taking the breast off the frame:** a cut along each side of the breastbone, the knife tip following the ribs,
  short strokes, the flesh lifted with the other hand; the wing joint separated at the shoulder.
- **Boning a leg for stuffing** (a leg *ballotine*): the thigh bone and drumstick bone freed from the inside, the meat
  turned back like a sleeve over the knuckle, the tendons cut; the leg kept whole, ready to be stuffed and tied.
- **Hygiene** (`POU-2.M1`): raw poultry on its own board, hands washed after, the board and knife washed in hot soapy
  water at once.
- **Using everything:** carcass and trim for stock, skin for crackling or fat, the oyster kept with the thigh.

**Sessions:**
1. Supremes (75 min): two supremes cut from a whole chicken, the bones French-trimmed; the rest of the bird portioned
   for the week; weights logged.
2. A boned leg (75 min): one leg boned for stuffing, stuffed with a simple forcemeat or mushroom filling, tied and
   roasted; the other leg boned flat for the grill.

**Cues:**
- A clean breastbone with almost no meat on it.
- The wing bone clean and white.
- The boned leg whole, with no tear through the skin.

**Faults:**
- Long sawing strokes.
- Cutting through the skin when boning the leg.
- Leaving the inner fillet on the frame.
- Raw chicken on the vegetable board.

#### BUT-3.M1.L2 — Boning a bird; duck

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m1-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BUT-3.M1.L1` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] bone a whole chicken; break down a duck (breasts, legs, fat, carcass)
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m1-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Boning a whole chicken from the back:**
  1. Cut along the backbone.
  2. Work the flesh off the frame on each side, the knife tip against the bone.
  3. Free the leg and wing joints from the frame.
  4. Lift out the frame without piercing the skin of the breast.

  The result is a flat bird, ready to stuff and roll (a whole *ballotine*), or to grill flat.
- **Duck is different:**
  - a thick fat layer under the skin, which is rendered, not discarded;
  - breasts taken off the frame like a chicken's; the skin scored without cutting the meat;
  - legs taken off at the hip, kept for confit (`PRS-3.M4`);
  - all loose fat and skin trimmed off, rendered slowly with a little water until the cracklings are golden, and the fat
    strained and kept in the fridge;
  - the carcass roasted for a dark stock.
- **Planning a duck:** the two breasts for one meal, the legs for confit, the fat for potatoes, the carcass for stock
  and soup.

**Sessions:**
1. A boned chicken (75 min): a whole chicken boned from the back, stuffed and rolled, tied and roasted; the frame to
   stock.
2. A duck (90 min): a duck broken down into breasts, legs, fat and carcass for `BUT-3.M1.X1`; the fat rendered.

**Cues:**
- The boned chicken in one piece, with no holes in the skin.
- The duck fat clear and white when cold.
- The cracklings crisp.

**Faults:**
- Piercing the breast skin from inside.
- Throwing away the duck fat.
- Rendering the fat too hot, so it browns and tastes burnt.

#### BUT-3.M2.L1 — Filleting a round fish

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `FSH-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] mackerel, trout or sea bass: two clean fillets, minimal waste
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Before filleting:** the fish scaled (from tail to head, under water or in a bag), gutted and rinsed, the bloodline
  along the backbone scraped out, and dried.
- **Two fillets from a round fish** (mackerel, whiting, trout):
  1. A cut behind the head and the gill flap, down to the bone.
  2. The knife turned flat along the backbone, from head to tail, in long strokes, the blade pressed against the bones.
  3. Over the top of the rib cage, then the fillet freed at the tail.
  4. Turned over, and the same on the other side.
- **Trimming:** the belly edge and fins trimmed; the pin bones felt with a fingertip and pulled with tweezers towards
  the head; the fillet portioned.
- **Skinning:** the fillet skin-down, the tail end held (with salt on the fingers for grip), the knife angled slightly
  down and the skin pulled back and forth against it.
- **Keeping it cold:** fish filleted straight from the fridge, the fillets back on a cold plate, covered, in the fridge at
  4 °C (assessment §9).

**Sessions:**
1. Mackerel or trout (60 min): two fish filleted, pin-boned and weighed; one fillet skinned.
2. A larger round fish (60 min): a whiting or a farmed trout of 600–800 g; fillets portioned for a meal.

**Cues:**
- A frame with very little flesh, held up to the light.
- Smooth fillets with no ragged steps.
- No pin bones under the fingertip.

**Faults:**
- Sawing, so the fillet tears.
- The knife angled up into the flesh.
- Pin bones pulled away from the head, tearing the flesh.
- Fish left on the counter while working.

#### BUT-3.M2.L2 — Filleting a flat fish

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BUT-3.M2.L1` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] plaice or sole: four fillets; skinning
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Four fillets from a flat fish** (plaice, dab, sole):
  1. A cut down the centre line, along the backbone, from head to tail.
  2. The knife flat, working outwards over the bones in long strokes, lifting the fillet.
  3. Freed at the edge along the fins; the same on the other side of the line.
  4. The fish turned over, and two more fillets from the white side.
- **Skinning:** dark-skinned fillets usually skinned; sole can be skinned whole by pulling the dark skin from the tail
  after a nick.
- **Whole flat fish** are often cooked on the bone (plaice in butter); filleting is for dishes that need a fillet.
- **Yield** is lower than for round fish (heavier head and frame), which the yield sheet shows.

**Sessions:**
1. Plaice (60 min): two plaice filleted into four fillets each, skinned, weighed.
2. A second flat fish (60 min): dab or another plaice without the reference video; the fillets cooked the same evening.

**Cues:**
- Four whole fillets, no ragged edges.
- A frame with very little flesh on it.

**Faults:**
- Starting at the edge instead of the centre line.
- Cutting through the bones.
- Tearing the thin tail end.

#### BUT-3.M2.L3 — Frames and heads

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l3 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 h · builds on `STK-2.M2.L4` · moves KM-WAST→4*

*Objectives:* [S] *fumet*, soup or crisp skin from the trim; [J] what to keep and what to discard
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l3 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The *fumet*** (`STK-2.M2.L4`): white-fish frames and heads, gills removed and blood rinsed out, sweated with
  aromatics, covered with water (and wine), simmered for no more than 30 minutes, strained and cooled by the standard of
  assessment §9.
- **Other uses:** a fish soup from the frame and the scraps scraped off it; fish skin dried and baked or fried crisp;
  cheeks cut from larger heads.
- **[J] What to keep and what to discard:**
  - keep white-fish frames and heads; flat fish frames make an excellent *fumet*;
  - discard the gills (bitter), the guts and any bloodline;
  - oily fish frames (mackerel) make a strong, often unpleasant stock: use them for a robust soup only, or discard;
  - frames that are not used within the day are frozen.

**Sessions:**
1. A *fumet* and crisp skin (60 min active, 1 h in total): a *fumet* from the plaice frames, cooled and logged; skins
   baked crisp as a garnish.

**Cues:**
- A clear, fresh-smelling *fumet*.
- Skin crisp and dry.

**Faults:**
- Gills left in.
- A *fumet* boiled for two hours.
- Mackerel frames in a delicate stock.

#### BUT-3.M3.L1 — Trimming and tying

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `KNF-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] trim a pork loin or shoulder; tie a roast evenly
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m3-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Trimming a joint** (a pork loin or a shoulder):
  - the silverskin (the thin, shiny membrane) removed with the knife tip under it and the blade angled up;
  - the fat cap trimmed to an even 5–8 mm and scored;
  - loose flaps and sinew removed and kept for stock or mince.
- **Why tie:** an even cylinder cooks evenly, holds a stuffing, and slices neatly.
- **The butcher's knot** (a slip knot that holds tension) and two ways to tie:
  - separate loops every 2–3 cm, each tied with the slip knot;
  - one continuous string with half-hitches along the joint.

  Tied firmly but not so tight that the string cuts in.
- **Seasoning:** a joint salted the day before (dry-brined, `SCI-2.M2`) tastes seasoned through.

**Sessions:**
1. Trim and tie (60 min): a pork loin trimmed and tied with separate loops; roasted to the §9 figure for pork (63 °C for
   15 seconds).
2. Tie again (45 min): a second, smaller joint or a rolled belly tied with one continuous string; untied and retied
   until even.

**Cues:**
- Loops evenly spaced.
- An even cylinder.
- No silverskin left.

**Faults:**
- String so tight it cuts in.
- Loops too far apart.
- Taking off half the meat with the silverskin.

#### BUT-3.M3.L2 — Butterflying and stuffing

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 h · builds on `BUT-3.M3.L1` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] butterfly a joint or a leg of lamb; stuff and roll
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m3-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Butterflying:** a thick joint opened into an even sheet:
  - a loin cut along its length, two-thirds through, then opened like a book;
  - or rolled out with a spiral cut;
  - a boned leg of lamb opened and the thick parts cut and laid flat.

  The aim is an even thickness, so it cooks evenly.
- **Stuffing:** a stuffing that adds moisture and flavour (herbs, breadcrumbs, mushrooms, apple or dried fruit, sausage
  meat), cooled before it goes in; spread thin, leaving a border.
- **Rolling and tying:** rolled tightly from the long side, the seam underneath, tied every 2–3 cm.
- **Safety:** a stuffed joint is cooked until the stuffing's centre reaches 74 °C (stuffed foods, assessment §9).
  Stuffing with sausage meat follows the same rule.
- **Resting and slicing:** rested, the strings cut away, sliced across into rounds that show the spiral.

**Sessions:**
1. A butterflied, stuffed and rolled joint (75 min active, 2 h in total): a pork shoulder steak or a lamb leg
   butterflied, stuffed with a seasonal stuffing, rolled, tied and roasted; the stuffing's centre probed.

**Cues:**
- Even thickness when opened.
- A neat spiral in the slice.
- The stuffing's centre at 74 °C.

**Faults:**
- Uneven thickness.
- Warm stuffing in a raw joint.
- Probing the meat but not the stuffing.

#### BUT-3.M4.L1 — Yield tests

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m4-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `WRK-1.M3.L3` · moves KM-MATH→4, KM-PURC→3*

*Objectives:* [S] weigh whole, usable and trim; cost per usable kilo; [J] whole versus prepared
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m4-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **The yield test:**
  - weigh the whole item as bought (the as-purchased weight);
  - weigh each usable part (the edible portion) and the trim;
  - yield % = usable weight ÷ purchased weight × 100.
- **Cost per usable kilo** = price paid ÷ usable weight. A whole chicken at €8 per kilo with a 65 % meat yield costs
  about €12.30 per kilo of meat, before counting the value of the frame for stock.
- **Valuing the by-products:** a frame that replaces a bought stock, fat that replaces bought fat; counted at what
  they replace, or left out to stay conservative.
- **Cooking loss:** a second yield, from raw to cooked (a roast loses 20–30 % of its weight), used when costing a portion
  as served.
- **[J] Whole or prepared:** compare the cost per usable kilo, add the time at a value the learner chooses, and decide;
  whole often wins on cost and quality, prepared on time.
- **Costing a menu:** cost per portion from the measured yields; scaled with care, because not everything scales in a
  straight line (seasoning, braising liquid, the time a larger roast takes).

**Sessions:**

#### BUT-3.M4.L2 — Boning safely

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m4-l2 -->
*1 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `KNF-1.M1.L3` · moves SA-PHYS→4*

*Objectives:* [S] mesh glove, stable board, cutting away from the body
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m4-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Where boning cuts happen:** the knife slipping off a bone, cutting towards the hand or body, a greasy handle.
- **The safe station:**
  - a stable board on a damp cloth or a non-slip mat, at hip height;
  - a dry, grease-free handle and dry hands;
  - a mesh (cut-resistant) glove on the hand that holds the meat;
  - the knife moving away from the body and from the holding hand;
  - short, controlled strokes with the tip, not force;
  - the knife never left under trim or in the sink.
- **A sharp knife is a safe knife:** a blunt boning knife needs force and slips (`KNF-2`).

**Sessions:** theory only.

**Cues:** the learner can set up and explain the station.

**Faults:**
- Cutting towards the holding hand.
- A slippery handle.
- Catching a falling knife.

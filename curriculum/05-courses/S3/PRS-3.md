## Introduction

Preserving turns a glut into a pantry: the late-summer cucumbers into pickles, the autumn cabbage into sauerkraut, the
plums into jam, the duck legs into confit. It is also the area of home cooking where a mistake can be most dangerous,
because the hazards (botulism above all) cannot be seen, smelled or tasted. `PRS-3` therefore works by one rule: **every
method is a tested method**, from a named source, followed exactly where safety depends on it.

The course has six parts:
- **principles:** the hurdles (acid, salt, sugar, drying, fat, cold) matched to each method, and how to recognise an
  untested or unsafe recipe;
- **pickling:** quick fridge pickles by ratio, and water-bath canning of acid pickles by a tested recipe;
- **lacto-fermentation:** sauerkraut at a weighed salt percentage, a mixed ferment, and telling normal from spoiled;
- **salt curing and confit:** a cure by weight, and confit stored safely;
- **drying and sugar preserves:** a low oven, jams and a chutney;
- **cooking with preserves:** the pantry used, labelled and rotated.

The figures come from [assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment), whose preserving
rows were checked for this stage: the pH line of 4.6, home canning of acid foods only by a tested recipe, sauerkraut
salt at 2.25–2.5 % with its fermentation times, nitrite curing salt, and the cold-storage rules. The tested recipes come
from the National Center for Home Food Preservation [S27] or an equivalent authority; Phase 6 names each one. The
Netherlands lies at sea level, so the lowest-altitude processing times of a tested recipe apply.

The produce is Dutch and seasonal: cucumbers, beans, courgettes, beetroot, cabbage, carrots, onions and pumpkin; Dutch
fruit for the jams (rhubarb, strawberries in June, plums, apples, pears). Marmalade needs citrus, which is imported:
it is taught as a method and is optional in January, when Seville oranges are in season.

**In the spiral.**
- `TS-PRS` reaches level 3 and `SA-HRPR` level 4 with `SAF-3`; `SC-MICR` completes level 3 with the learner's own
  preserves.
- `JD-CUES` reaches level 4 in reading a ferment.
- `KM-WAST` and `IN-STO` reach level 4: the preserves use surplus and trim, and the pantry is stored and rotated for
  quality.
- `MS-S3` uses a preserve made in this course in its week plan (`MS-S3.M1.L1`).

**Pacing.** About five weeks of active time spread over two to three months, following the harvest; the ferments and
the confit run in the background.

#### PRS-3.M1.L1 — Hurdles and tested methods

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SAF-3.M1.L2` · moves SA-HRPR→4, SC-MICR→3*

*Objectives:* [K] acid, salt, sugar, drying, fat, cold; [J] recognise an untested or unsafe recipe [S27]
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m1-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **[K] Each method and its hurdles** (`SCI-2.M6`):

  | Method | Main hurdles | Typical storage |
  |---|---|---|
  | Quick pickle | Acid, cold | Fridge |
  | Water-bath pickle | Acid (pH 4.6 or below), heat, a sealed jar | Cupboard until opened |
  | Lacto-ferment | Salt, then the acid the bacteria make, no air | Cool, then the fridge |
  | Salt cure | Salt (less free water), cold | Fridge, then cooked |
  | Confit | Cooking, fat (no air), cold | Fridge or freezer |
  | Drying | Little free water | Airtight, dry |
  | Jam, chutney | Sugar, acid, heat, a sealed jar | Cupboard if processed; fridge once opened |

- **Why combinations matter:** each hurdle alone is often not enough; together they stop the organisms that matter. A
  recipe that removes one (less vinegar, less sugar, less salt) can break the combination.
- **[K] What only a tested method can make safe:** anything canned; low-acid foods (all fresh vegetables except most
  tomatoes, assessment §9); garlic, herbs or vegetables kept in oil; cured meats eaten without cooking.
- **[J] Recognising an untested or unsafe recipe**, by a checklist:
  1. Is the source an authority that tested it (NCHFP or equivalent), or does it name one?
  2. Does a canned recipe give a processing time and jar size?
  3. Is a low-acid food being water-bath canned?
  4. Does it say "to taste" for vinegar, sugar or salt where they are hurdles?
  5. Is something kept in oil at room temperature?
  6. Is there oven canning, open-kettle canning (filled hot and sealed without processing) or wax sealing?
  7. Does a cure for meat eaten raw lack a nitrite dose (the §9 nitrite row)?

**Sessions:** theory only.

**Cues:** for any preserve, the learner names its hurdles and whether it needs a tested method.

**Faults:**
- "Grandma always did it this way."
- Halving the sugar in a canned jam.
- Garlic oil in the cupboard.
- Water-bath canning beans.

#### PRS-3.M2.L1 — Quick pickles

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 1 d · builds on `PRS-3.M1.L1` · moves TS-PRS→2, RD-RATO→3*

*Objectives:* [S] brine ratios of vinegar, water, salt and sugar; [J] vary aromatics
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m2-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **A quick (fridge) pickle:** vegetables in a hot or cold brine of vinegar, water, salt and sugar, kept in the fridge,
  ready in hours to a day.
- **The brine ratio:** vinegar and water, salt and sugar by weight. The ratio comes from a tested refrigerator-pickle
  recipe (Phase 6 names it, from [S27]), and that recipe's storage time applies; the vinegar is never diluted below it.
- **[J] Varying the aromatics:** mustard seed, dill, bay, chilli, garlic, peppercorns, juniper, fennel seed; the acid
  (wine, cider or rice vinegar at the same strength); sugar up or down for sweet-sour or sharp.
- **The vegetables:** firm and fresh: cucumbers, beans, carrots, red onions, beetroot, radishes, cauliflower,
  courgettes in summer; cabbage and roots in winter. Cut evenly; packed tight.
- **Jars:** clean and hot-washed; labelled with the content and the date.

**Sessions:**
1. Two quick pickles (60 min): red onions and cucumbers in a hot brine by the ratio; tasted after an hour and a day.
2. A third with varied aromatics (45 min): a seasonal vegetable with different spices and acid; compared.

**Cues:**
- Crisp after a day.
- Balanced sour, salt and sweet.
- Every jar labelled and dated.

**Faults:**
- Watering down the vinegar.
- Soft, overcooked vegetables.
- Jars without dates.

#### PRS-3.M2.L2 — Water-bath canning

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m2-l2 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `PRS-3.M2.L1` · moves TS-PRS→3, SA-HRPR→4*

*Objectives:* [S] jars, headspace, processing time, seal check; tested recipes only
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m2-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **When:** acid foods only (pH 4.6 or below, assessment §9): fruit, jams, pickles and sauerkraut with enough acid, by a
  tested recipe. Low-acid foods need pressure canning, which the program does not teach.
- **The equipment:** canning jars with new two-piece lids (or the tested recipe's jar type), a deep pot with a rack so
  the jars sit off the bottom and are covered by water as the tested recipe says, a jar lifter, a funnel.
- **The steps of a tested recipe:**
  1. Jars washed and kept hot.
  2. Food prepared exactly as written (cut, amounts, vinegar strength).
  3. Jars filled to the stated **headspace** (the gap under the lid), air bubbles released, rims wiped.
  4. Lids on, finger-tight.
  5. Processed in boiling water for the stated time, timed from when the water returns to the boil.
  6. Lifted out and left undisturbed to cool, as the tested recipe says.
- **The seal check:** the lid curved down and not flexing when pressed; jars that didn't seal go into the fridge and are
  used like a fridge pickle.
- **Storage:** cool, dark and dry; labelled with content and date; a bulging lid, spurting liquid, mould or an off smell
  means the jar is thrown away unopened, without tasting (`SAF-3.M1.L3`).

**Sessions:**
1. A canned pickle (2 h): the pickle of `PRS-3.M2.X2`, processed by its tested recipe, seals checked and logged.

**Cues:**
- Headspace measured.
- The time counted from the return to the boil.
- Every lid sealed and curved down.

**Faults:**
- Starting the timer when the jars go in.
- Reusing old flat lids.
- Adding extra vegetables.
- Tasting from a jar with a bulging lid.

#### PRS-3.M3.L1 — Sauerkraut

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 wk · builds on `SCI-2.M6.L1` · moves TS-PRS→3*

*Objectives:* [S] salt by weight (2.25–2.5 %), packing, weighting, temperature; [K] the bacterial succession
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m3-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Salt by weight** (assessment §9, [S36]): 2.25–2.5 % of the shredded cabbage's weight, weighed, not measured by the
  spoon. Too little softens the kraut; too much slows the fermentation and can turn it pink.
- **Packing and weighting:** the salted cabbage pressed until its own juice rises and covers it; kept under the brine
  with a weight (a plate and a bag of brine, or glass weights); air kept out.
- **Temperature and time** (§9, [S27]): about 3–4 weeks at 21–24 °C; 5–6 weeks at 16–18 °C; below about 16 °C it may
  not ferment, and above about 24 °C it can turn soft.
- **[K] The bacterial succession:** first, bacteria that tolerate salt and make gas and some acid (*Leuconostoc*); as the
  acid builds, *Lactobacillus* species take over and bring the pH down further. The acid and the salt together keep
  spoilage organisms and pathogens out.
- **Done:** bubbling has stopped and the flavour is pleasantly sour; fully fermented kraut keeps for several months in
  the fridge, tightly covered [S27].

**Sessions:**
1. Make it (60 min, then weeks): the sauerkraut of `PRS-3.M3.X1` weighed, salted, packed and weighted; the log started.

**Cues:**
- Brine above the cabbage.
- Bubbles in the first days.
- A clean, sour smell.

**Faults:**
- Salt by the spoon.
- Cabbage above the brine.
- A warm spot beside the heating.

#### PRS-3.M3.L2 — Fermented vegetables and troubleshooting

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 2 wk · builds on `PRS-3.M3.L1` · moves TS-PRS→3, JD-CUES→4*

*Objectives:* [S] a kimchi-style mixed ferment; [J] kahm yeast versus mould; when to discard
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m3-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **A kimchi-style mixed ferment:** Chinese or savoy cabbage salted, rinsed and mixed with a paste of chilli, garlic,
  ginger, spring onion and a fish or soy element, with carrots or radishes; packed and fermented, then kept in the
  fridge. Dutch-grown cabbage, radishes and spring onions suit it.
- **Brine ferments** for whole or cut vegetables (cucumbers, carrots, beans): a weighed brine, the vegetables kept under
  it.
- **[J] Normal against spoiled:**
  - **normal:** bubbling, cloudy brine, a sour smell, a little white sediment;
  - **kahm yeast:** a flat, white, wrinkled film on the surface; harmless but off-tasting; skimmed off, and the
    vegetables under the brine are fine;
  - **mould:** fuzzy, raised, coloured (green, black, pink, blue) spots; **discarded**, the whole ferment;

#### PRS-3.M4.L1 — Salt curing

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m4-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 wk · builds on `SAF-3.M1.L3` · moves TS-PRS→3*

*Objectives:* [S] equilibrium curing of a duck breast or pork belly; [K] when nitrite is needed and the dosage
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m4-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **Equilibrium curing:** the salt weighed as a percentage of the meat's weight (with the meat and its cure in a bag or
  a covered container in the fridge), so the meat can never be over-salted: it reaches that salt level throughout,
  given enough time. The percentage and the time come from the tested recipe Phase 6 names.
- **[K] When nitrite is needed and the dosage** (assessment §9, `SAF-3.M1.L3`):
  - needed for meat that is eaten without cooking or kept long (air-dried hams, cold-smoked meat, fermented sausages);
  - not needed for the program's cures, which are cooked before eating (a pork belly cured into bacon, a duck breast
    cured and then cooked) and kept in the fridge for the cure time, then cooked or frozen;
  - where a tested recipe calls for nitrite curing salt, the added nitrite stays at or below 80 mg per kg of meat (as
    the nitrite ion), calculated from the label and weighed on a 0.01 g scale.
- **The cure:** salt, sugar and aromatics (pepper, juniper, bay, thyme); the meat turned daily; at the end rinsed,
  dried and cooked (roasted, pan-fried or hot-smoked to its §9 figure).

**Sessions:**
1. A cure by weight (45 min, then about a week): a piece of pork belly or a duck breast weighed, the cure weighed by
   percentage and logged; cooked at the end of the tested cure time.

**Cues:**
- Every weight in the log.
- The meat firm and evenly seasoned when cooked.

**Faults:**
- A cure by eye.
- Curing salt used where none is needed.
- Eating the cured raw belly uncooked.

**Concepts:**

#### PRS-3.M5.L1 — Drying

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m5-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `SCI-2.M5.L1` · moves TS-PRS→3*

*Objectives:* [S] herbs, tomatoes, mushrooms and fruit in a low oven; [K] water activity and storage
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m5-l1 -->

**Concepts:**
- **[K] Water activity:** microbes need free water; drying removes it until they can't grow. The food is dry when it is
  leathery or brittle (by type) with no moist centre.
- **The low oven:** the lowest setting (often 50–70 °C), the door slightly ajar for moisture to escape, the food in a
  single layer on a rack; or an air-dryer.
- **What dries well at home:**
  - herbs (woody ones best), air-dried in bunches or on a rack;
  - tomatoes halved and seasoned (Dutch tomatoes in late summer);
  - mushrooms sliced;
  - apple and pear rings (dipped in acidulated water against browning).
- **[K] Storage:** cooled completely, then airtight in a cool, dark place, labelled; checked after a few days for
  moisture (condensation means back into the oven). Dried tomatoes or herbs put into oil become an oil preserve: in the
  fridge and used soon (§9).

**Sessions:**
1. A drying session (90 min active, a day in total): herbs, tomatoes and apple rings, weighed before and after; stored
   and checked after three days.

**Cues:**
- No moisture in the centre.
- No condensation in the jar after three days.

**Faults:**
- Too hot an oven that cooks instead of drying.
- Packing warm.
- Dried tomatoes in oil on the shelf.

#### PRS-3.M5.L2 — Jams, marmalade and chutney

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m5-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M5.L1` · moves TS-PRS→3, IN-VEG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] sugar, pectin, acid and the set test; a seasonal chutney; [K] fruit and vegetable pectin
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m5-l2 -->

**Concepts:**
- **[K] How a jam sets:** pectin (from the fruit), sugar and acid together form a gel when the mixture is boiled to the
  setting point. High-pectin fruit (apples, plums, redcurrants, citrus peel) sets easily; low-pectin fruit (strawberries,
  rhubarb, pears) needs added pectin, lemon juice, or a high-pectin fruit mixed in.
- **[K] Vegetable pectin:** vegetables have pectin too, which is why a chutney thickens as it reduces; acid keeps
  vegetables firm and sets the colour (red onion and red cabbage turn bright pink with acid; `VEG-1.M1` compared carrots in acid and alkali).
- **The set test:** a spoonful on a cold plate wrinkles when pushed; or the temperature of the tested recipe on a
  thermometer.
- **Sugar** is a hurdle (`PRS-3.M1`): a full-sugar jam by a tested recipe, water-bath processed, keeps in the cupboard
  until opened, then in the fridge; a low-sugar jam is kept in the fridge or freezer.
- **Marmalade:** citrus peel, pith and juice cooked long; the peel's pectin sets it. Citrus is imported; it is taught as
  a method and is optional (Seville oranges in January).
- **Chutney:** vegetables or fruit with vinegar, sugar and spices, cooked down until a spoon drawn across the pan leaves
  a channel; mellows after a few weeks. Canned only by a tested recipe; otherwise kept in the fridge.
- **Seasonal choices:** rhubarb and strawberry in June, plums in August, apples and pears in autumn; pumpkin, beetroot,
  red onion or green tomato chutney in autumn.

**Sessions:**
1. A jam (75 min): a seasonal jam by a tested recipe, the set tested, jarred and processed or refrigerated.
2. A chutney (90 min): a seasonal chutney cooked to a channel, jarred; a red-onion comparison with and without vinegar
   photographed for colour.

**Cues:**
- A wrinkling set on the cold plate.
- A chutney that holds a channel.
- Every jar labelled.

**Faults:**
- Boiling past the set into a stiff, dark jam.
- Halving the sugar of a canned recipe.
- A chutney too wet to keep.

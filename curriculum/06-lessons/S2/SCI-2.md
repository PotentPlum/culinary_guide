### SCI-2.M1.L1 — Designing a kitchen experiment

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M6.L1` · moves RD-TEST→2*

*Objectives:* [K] variables, controls, blind tasting, repetition; [S] write a test plan and a result table
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** cooks argue endlessly about what works: salt the water or not, sear to "seal in" juices, rest the
dough. A fair test settles it in an evening. It is also how you will improve your own recipes for the rest of the
program: change one thing, compare, and keep what the data supports.

**You need:** 400 g of dried pasta; salt; two pots; the scale; coded bowls; a helper; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The question comes first,* written so that it can be answered. "Does dry-brining make chicken juicier?" can be tested;
"what is the best chicken?" cannot.

*Variables:*

| Variable | What it is | Example (pasta water) |
|---|---|---|
| Independent | What you change: one only | Salt at 10 or 20 g/l |
| Dependent | What you measure or judge | The taster's choice; "under", "right" or "over" |
| Controlled | Everything held the same | Pasta, pot, water volume, cooking time, serving |

*A control* is the unchanged version you compare against: here, the usual 10 g/l (`TST-1.M2.L3`).

*Blind tasting.* Samples are coded by a helper, so neither you nor the taster knows which is which
([assessment §5](../../04-assessment.md#5-calibration-tests), [S20]). Expectation changes what people taste.

*Repetition.* One trial can be luck. Two or three trials, or several tasters, make a result worth trusting. Say how many
you had.

*The plan and the result table* are written before you cook. Afterwards, the conclusion says only what the data support
("two of three tasters preferred…"), and names the next question.

**Practice:**
1. **Plan and run a small test (90 min).** Write a plan for the question "does salting pasta water at 10 or 20 g/l
   change the taste of the pasta?": the variable, the controls, the result table. Cook 200 g of pasta in each pot, the
   same shape and time. The helper codes the bowls. Taste blind with the helper and anyone else at home, and fill in the
   table.

**Look for:** a plan another person could repeat; a conclusion no bigger than the data.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You can't tell what caused the difference | Changing two things at once | One variable; everything else in the controlled list |
| Nothing to compare against | No control | Keep the usual version as the control |
| The result matches what you hoped | The cook tasting unblinded | A helper codes the samples |
| A firm claim from one bite | A conclusion from one taste | Several tasters or trials; say how many |
| A plan that fits the result | Writing the plan after the result | Plan and table first |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the plan, the filled-in table and the conclusion in the experiment log.

### SCI-2.M1.X1 — A test of your own design

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-sci-2-m1-x1 -->
*comparison, framework* — Design and run a two-variable test of your choice, with a written plan.
<!-- END generated:exercise-sci-2-m1-x1 -->

**Task:** answer a cooking question of your choice with a fair test: two versions that differ in one variable, with a
control, blind tasting by at least one other person, and a written plan before cooking.

**You need:** the ingredients for your question, something cheap and quick: for example roasted carrots at 180 against
220 °C, or mashed potato with the butter added hot against cold; coded plates; a helper; the scale; a timer; a phone.

**Framework:** the plan and report template.

| Part | What it is | Your decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Question | One sentence that can be answered by tasting or measuring | The question |
| Variable | The one thing that differs, at two levels | Which, and the two levels |
| Control | The usual version | Which of the two it is |
| Controlled | Everything held the same: ingredient, size, pan, heat, time, serving | The list |
| Measures | What is measured (time, weight, a colour photo) and what is tasted | Which |
| Result table | Rows for each version, columns for each measure and each taster | The layout |
| Tasting | Coded by a helper; at least one taster besides you | Who |
| Conclusion | What the data support, and the next question | — |

*Procedure:*
1. Write the question, the variable, the controls and the result table.
2. Cook both versions.
3. Code them and have them tasted blind.
4. Measure what can be measured: time, weight, a colour photo.
5. Write the conclusion and the next question.

**Show:** the plan and the filled-in result table on the template, with the tasters' forms. This meets the `RD-TEST`
level 2 success row: a guided comparison with one variable changed, recorded on a template.

**Time:** 1 h.

### SCI-2.M2.L1 — Denaturation and water loss

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `EGG-1.M1.L2` · moves SC-PROT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] how heat unfolds and tightens proteins; juiciness and temperature; collagen introduced
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a dry chicken breast is the most common fault in home cooking, and its cause is simple: it was
cooked too far. Weighing what the meat loses turns "juicy" from an opinion into a number, and shows you why the
thermometer, not the clock, decides.

**You need:** 2 chicken breasts of the same size from one pack; salt; oil; the sauté pan; the probe thermometer; the
scale; a board kept for raw meat (`SAF-1.M2`); a plate; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Heat unfolds muscle proteins.* Raw proteins are folded tight. Heat unfolds them (denaturation), and they then bond to
each other (coagulation) and tighten. As they tighten, they squeeze out water. The hotter the meat, the tighter the
proteins and the more juice is lost. This is why doneness, not time, decides juiciness: a chicken breast at 80 °C is
drier than one taken to the safe 75 °C and rested.

*The safe minimum and the juiciness ladder sit together*
([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)):
- poultry, minced meat and offal: 75 °C;
- fish and intact meat: 63 °C.

The craft is to cook a little above the safe minimum, not far above it. Probe the thickest part, take the meat off at
the figure, and let it rest.

*Collagen,* the connective tissue, behaves the other way. It is tough at the temperatures that keep muscle juicy, and
only turns into gelatin after long, moist cooking at a simmer. So tender cuts are cooked fast and briefly, and
collagen-rich cuts (shin, cheek, thigh) slowly (`MEA-2`, `SLW-3`).

*Weighing the loss.* Weigh raw, cook, rest 5 minutes, weigh again. Loss in % = (raw − cooked) ÷ raw × 100.

**Practice:**
1. **Weigh the loss (60 min).** Weigh both breasts. Salt them lightly and cook them in the same pan over medium heat,
   turning now and then. Take one off when the thickest part reads 75 °C, the other at about 82 °C. Rest both, weigh
   them, and calculate the loss. Slice and taste them side by side.

**Look for:** a clearly higher weight loss and a drier texture in the overcooked piece.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Juice on the board, a dry breast | Cutting into the meat to check | Probe instead of cutting |
| An undercooked centre, or a dry one | Probing once and too early | Probe the thickest part, more than once near the end |
| "They taste the same" | Judging juiciness without weighing | Weigh before and after |

**Safety:** poultry is safe at 75 °C at the thickest part (assessment §9). Raw chicken has its own board and is never
rinsed (`SAF-1.M2`); wash your hands and the probe after touching it.

**Record:** the weights, the loss in % and a tasting note for each piece.

### SCI-2.M2.L2 — Salting, brining and marinating

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m2-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 1 d · builds on `SCI-2.M2.L1` · moves SC-PROT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] osmosis, diffusion, salt-protein interaction; what marinades can and can't do
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** salting meat ahead of time is the cheapest way to make it juicier and better seasoned. Knowing what
dry-brining, wet-brining and marinating each do, and what they can't do, lets you choose one for a dish instead of
following a recipe's habit.

**You need:** the inputs of `SCI-2.M2.X1`; a rack or plate; a fridge-safe container with a lid; the scale.

**Explanation:**

*Salt changes the proteins.* Salt dissolves part of the muscle proteins, which then hold water better while cooking:
salted meat loses less juice, and it is seasoned through.

*Three methods:*

| Method | How | Effect | Watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dry-brine | Salt rubbed on, about 1–1.5 % of the weight; rested uncovered in the fridge for hours or overnight | Seasons through, holds juice; the dry surface browns well | Give it time: the salt draws out moisture first, then it is drawn back in |
| Wet-brine | Soaked in salted water (for example 5 %) in the fridge | Adds water as well as salt: juicier, but the skin is harder to crisp | Needs fridge space and a time limit |
| Marinade | Oil, acid, aromatics, often salt | Flavours the surface: salt travels in, most other flavours only a few millimetres | Strong acid left long makes the surface mushy or chalky |

For a 200 g breast at 1 %: 2 g of salt. For a 5 % brine: 50 g of salt in 1 litre of water.

*What a marinade can't do.* It doesn't tenderise a tough cut: collagen needs slow cooking. And a marinade with raw meat
in it is raw meat: it stays in the fridge, and it is boiled before it is used as a sauce, or thrown away
(`SAF-1.M1.Q6`).

**Practice:**
1. **Salt the chicken (30 min).** Set up the pieces of `SCI-2.M2.X1` the evening before: weigh them, dry-brine one on a
   rack, leave one unsalted, both in the fridge.
2. **Cook and weigh (60 min).** The next day, wet-brine the third piece for 2 hours, then run the exercise.

**Look for:** the dry-brined surface matt and dry; the brined pieces seasoned through when tasted.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A risk, and no benefit | Brining at room temperature | Always in the fridge |
| Spongy, salty meat | A wet brine left too long | Time it: about 2 hours for a breast |
| A grey, steaming sear | Salting a surface just before searing and leaving it wet | Salt well ahead, or pat dry just before |
| A tough stew after a night in wine | Expecting a marinade to tenderise a tough cut | Slow, moist cooking for collagen |

**Safety:** brines and marinades with raw meat stay in the fridge, covered, on the bottom shelf, and are thrown away
after use or boiled before serving (`SAF-1.M1`).

**Record:** the setup times and weights (in `SCI-2.M2.X1`).

### SCI-2.M2.X1 — Chicken breast: unsalted, dry-brined and wet-brined

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-sci-2-m2-x1 -->
*comparison, recipe* — Chicken breast: unsalted, dry-brined and wet-brined; weigh before and after cooking.
<!-- END generated:exercise-sci-2-m2-x1 -->

**Task:** measure what salting does to cooking loss, with everything else held constant: three pieces of the same size
from the same pack, the same pan, heat and final core temperature; only the salting differs.

**You need:** 3 chicken breasts (or 6 thighs, two per version); fine salt; 1 litre of water; a fridge-safe container
with a lid; a rack; kitchen paper; oil; the sauté pan; the probe thermometer; the scale; three coded plates; a helper.

**Recipe:**
1. **Weigh** each piece and label it A, B or C.
2. **The evening before:** dry-brine A with 1 % of its weight in salt, rubbed on all over; set it on the rack in the
   fridge, uncovered. Put B, unsalted, in the fridge, covered.
3. **Two hours before cooking:** dissolve 50 g of salt in the litre of cold water (5 %) in the container, and submerge
   C in it, covered, in the fridge.
4. **Pat** all three dry with kitchen paper and weigh them again.
5. **Cook** them together in the oiled pan over medium heat, turning now and then, each to 75 °C at the thickest part,
   probed. Take each off when it reaches 75 °C.
6. **Rest** 5 minutes and weigh. Calculate the cooking loss in % from the weight before cooking.
7. **Taste blind.** The helper slices and codes them; season B at the table, so seasoning doesn't decide.
8. **Explain** the results by what salt does to the proteins.

**Show:** the weights and the loss in %, with the explanation, and this module's quiz at 80 %: the `SC-PROT` level 3
success row, a dry-brined and an unsalted piece compared by cooking loss and explained.

**Time:** 1.5 h over two days; the chicken is dinner.

### SCI-2.M3.L1 — Starch thickening

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m3-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `STA-1.M1.L1` · moves SC-STRC→3*

*Objectives:* [K] flour, corn starch, potato starch, arrowroot: gel temperature, clarity, breakdown, freezing
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** flour, corn starch, potato starch and arrowroot all thicken, but they give different sauces: cloudy
or clear, stable or thinning, freezing well or weeping. Choosing the right one is the difference between a glossy fruit
glaze and a gluey one, or a gravy that survives the freezer.

**You need:** the inputs of `SCI-2.M3.X1`: 1.5 litres of stock and the starches; three small saucepans; a whisk; three
cups; the scale.

**Explanation:**

*Starches thicken by gelatinising* (`STA-1.M1.L1`): heated in liquid, the granules swell and thicken it. Each starch has
its own behaviour:

| Starch | Look | Thickens | Weak points | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wheat flour | Cloudy, opaque | Needs to simmer a while to lose its raw taste | Less thickening per gram | A roux, gravies, stews; stable in long cooking |
| Corn starch (*maïzena*) | Fairly clear | Near the boil | Thins with long stirring or strong acid; weeps after freezing | A quick sauce served at once |
| Potato starch (*aardappelzetmeel*) | Very clear | Below the boil | Breaks down if boiled long | A last-minute glaze |
| Arrowroot | Clear and glossy | Below the boil; tolerates acid | Expensive | Acid sauces, sauces to freeze |

*Slurry or roux.* Dry starch stirred into hot liquid lumps: the outside of each clump gels and seals the dry powder
inside. So the starch is first dispersed: in cold liquid (a slurry) or in fat (a roux, `SAU-2`). Then the granules
swell separately.

*Cold and frozen.* As the sauce cools, some starch gels set firm and others weep water (retrogradation). If a sauce
will be chilled or frozen, choose the starch for that.

*Predict first.* Before you cook, write down which sauce you expect to be clearest, thickest and best after freezing.

**Practice:**
1. **Three starches (60 min).** Run the comparison of `SCI-2.M3.X1`, tasted and photographed hot. Chill and freeze the
   portions for the next days.

**Look for:** clear differences in gloss, clarity and body, and your predictions confirmed or explained.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Lumps | Dry starch into hot liquid | A slurry in cold liquid first |
| A pasty, floury taste | Too little cooking for flour | Simmer several minutes after thickening |
| A sauce that thins again | Boiling potato starch hard | Off the heat as soon as it thickens |
| A spongy, weeping sauce after thawing | Freezing a corn-starch sauce | Flour or arrowroot for the freezer |

**Safety:** a thickened sauce spits as it boils: stir with a long spoon and keep your face back (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the predictions and the hot results (in `SCI-2.M3.X1`).

### SCI-2.M3.L2 — Gluten

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m3-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M3.L1` · moves SC-STRC→3, IN-BAK→2*

*Objectives:* [K] glutenin and gliadin; water, mixing, rest, fat and sugar as controls
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** gluten is why bread is chewy and pastry is short, and why the same flour can give both. Once you
know what builds it and what weakens it, the rules of `BAK-2` and `BRD-2` (rest the dough, don't overwork the pastry,
use strong flour for bread) stop being rules and start making sense.

**You need:** 300 g of plain flour (*bloem*) and a little strong bread flour if you have it; water; 25 g of cold butter;
three bowls; the scale; a timer; cling film; a phone.

**Explanation:**

*Gluten forms* when two wheat proteins meet water and are worked:
- **glutenin** gives strength and elasticity;
- **gliadin** gives extensibility, the stretch.

Mixing and kneading line them up into a stretchy network that traps gas: the structure of bread.

*The controls:*

| Control | More of it | Effect on gluten |
|---|---|---|
| Water | More water | More gluten forms |
| Mixing and kneading | More work | More, stronger gluten |
| Rest | Time | The network relaxes and is easier to shape |
| Fat | Rubbed in first | Coats the proteins and "shortens" them: pastry is short |
| Sugar | More sugar | Competes for water and weakens the network |

*Flour types* (`IN-BAK`): strong bread flour has more protein, so more gluten; plain flour (*bloem*) is in the middle;
cake flour has less. Dutch packets show the protein per 100 g.

*When gluten is wanted, and when not.* Bread and pasta: yes. Pastry, cakes and pancakes: as little as possible, so they
are mixed briefly, and pastry is made with cold fat and rested.

*The windowpane test.* Stretch a small piece of dough between your fingers: well-developed gluten stretches into a thin,
translucent sheet without tearing.

**Practice:**
1. **Short and long (60 min).** Make three small doughs of 100 g of flour and 60 g of water each. Mix dough 1 for 1
   minute; knead dough 2 for 8 minutes; for dough 3, rub the butter into the flour first, then mix for 1 minute. Wrap
   and rest all three for 15 minutes. Stretch each, try the windowpane test, and photograph them.

**Look for:** the long-mixed dough stretches thin without tearing; the short one tears; the buttered one crumbles.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Tough, shrinking pastry | Overworking pastry | Mix briefly; cold fat; rest it |
| A dense loaf | Too little kneading for bread | Knead to the windowpane |
| Dough that springs back | Shaping without resting | Rest it, covered, 10–15 minutes |

**Safety:** raw flour can carry bacteria: don't taste raw dough, and wash hands and surfaces after (`SAF-1.M2`).

**Record:** the photos of the three doughs, for `SCI-2.M3.X1`.

### SCI-2.M3.X1 — Three starches, then chill and freeze

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-sci-2-m3-x1 -->
*comparison, recipe* — The same liquid thickened with three starches; compare, then chill and freeze.
<!-- END generated:exercise-sci-2-m3-x1 -->

**Task:** see how starches differ, hot and cold, with the same liquid, the same amount of each starch by weight and the
same heating. The short- and long-mixed doughs of `SCI-2.M3.L2` go into the same report, so both parts of the
`SC-STRC` level 3 row are in one exercise.

**You need:** 1.5 litres of vegetable stock; 20 g each of flour, corn starch and potato starch (and arrowroot if
available, with 500 ml more stock); three small saucepans; a whisk; small containers with lids, two per starch, coded;
the dough photos from `SCI-2.M3.L2`; a phone.

**Recipe:**
1. **Slurry.** For each starch, whisk the 20 g into 100 ml of the cold stock until smooth.
2. **Heat** 400 ml of stock in each pan to a simmer. Whisk in its slurry, and bring back to a simmer, whisking. Simmer

   potato-starch sauce as soon as it thickens.
3. **Hot.** Lift a spoon of each and photograph them side by side; note gloss, clarity and body, and taste.
4. **Chill** a portion of each in its coded container in the fridge for at least 4 hours; photograph a spoonful.
5. **Freeze** a second portion of each overnight; thaw it in the fridge, stir, and photograph it.
6. **Add** the dough photos.
7. **Explain** each result by gelatinisation, retrogradation and gluten.

The rest of the thickened stock becomes soup, eaten within 2 days.

**Show:** the three starches photographed hot, cold and thawed, and the doughs mixed short and long, each explained,
with this module's quiz at 80 %: the `SC-STRC` level 3 success row.

**Time:** 1 h, plus chilling and freezing.

### SCI-2.M4.L1 — Fats and oils

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m4-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · moves SC-EMUL→2*

*Objectives:* [K] saturation, smoke point, flavour, butter versus clarified butter; [J] choose a fat
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the fat you choose decides whether a steak sears or the pan smokes out the kitchen, whether a sauce
tastes of butter, and whether a pastry is flaky. Choosing a fat is a decision with three questions: how hot, what
flavour, what texture.

**You need:** 250 g of unsalted butter; a neutral refined oil (rapeseed or sunflower); three small, identical pans; a
small saucepan; a spoon; a fine sieve lined with kitchen paper or muslin; a small jar; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Saturation.*
- **Saturated fats** (butter, lard, coconut fat) are solid when cool.
- **Unsaturated oils** (rapeseed, sunflower, olive) are liquid.

This decides texture in baking (solid fat makes flakes and shortness) and mouthfeel in sauces.

*The smoke point* is where a fat starts to break down and smoke, giving acrid flavours. Refined oils have a high smoke
point. Extra virgin olive oil and whole butter have a lower one, because of their flavour compounds and, in butter, the
milk solids, which brown and then burn.

*Clarified butter.* Butter is fat, water and milk solids. Melted slowly, the water bubbles off, the milk solids sink or
float as foam, and the clear yellow fat can be poured off. It has the flavour of butter and a much higher smoke point,
and keeps for weeks in the fridge.

*Choosing a fat:*

| Purpose | Fat |
|---|---|
| High heat: searing, stir-frying | A refined oil, or clarified butter |
| Flavour at the end | Raw olive oil, or a knob of butter |
| Baking texture | Solid fats: butter, lard |
| Basting at medium heat | Butter added to oil after the sear (`HEA-2`) |

**Practice:**
1. **Clarify and compare (45 min).** Melt 200 g of butter over low heat and let it bubble gently until the foam has
   separated; skim it, and pour the clear fat through the lined sieve into the jar. Then put 2 tablespoons each of oil,
   whole butter and clarified butter into the three pans over the same medium-high heat. Note when each browns or
   smokes, and take each off as soon as it smokes.

**Look for:** whole butter browning and smoking first; the clarified butter clear and calm.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Burnt, bitter crust | Searing in whole butter from the start | Oil or clarified butter; butter at the end |
| Acrid smoke, wasted oil | Deep-frying in extra virgin olive oil | A refined oil for frying |
| Off flavours in the next dish | Reusing burnt fat | Discard fat that has smoked or darkened |

**Safety:** heat the fats with the extractor on, never leave them, and keep a lid beside the pans: a fat fire is
smothered with a lid or the fire blanket, never water (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the order in which the fats browned and smoked; the jar of clarified butter, labelled and dated.

### SCI-2.M4.L2 — Emulsions

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m4-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M4.L1`, `EGG-1.M4.L2` · moves SC-EMUL→3*

*Objectives:* [K] oil-in-water and water-in-oil; emulsifiers (lecithin, mustard), droplet size, temperature; why they break
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** vinaigrette, mayonnaise, hollandaise, a buttery pan sauce and the pasta of `STA-1` are all
emulsions. Knowing what holds one together lets you make it reliably, and rescue it when it breaks.

**You need:** the jars, oil, vinegar and mustard of `SCI-2.M4.X1`; for two mayonnaises, 2 pasteurised egg yolks (a
carton of pasteurised yolk, about 20 g a yolk) plus 1 more for the repair, 400 ml of neutral oil, lemon juice or
vinegar, salt; two bowls; a whisk; a damp cloth under the bowl; a measuring jug.

**Explanation:**

*An emulsion* is droplets of one liquid dispersed in another that doesn't mix with it:
- **oil-in-water:** oil droplets in water, as in a vinaigrette, mayonnaise or hollandaise;
- **water-in-oil:** water droplets in fat, as in butter.

*What keeps it stable:*
- **emulsifiers** sit between oil and water and keep droplets apart: lecithin in egg yolk, mucilage in mustard;
- **small droplets,** from vigorous whisking, settle slowly;
- **thickness:** a thicker continuous phase slows separation;
- **temperature:** too hot and egg emulsions curdle, too cold and butter sets.

*Why they break:* oil added too fast at the start, too much oil for the emulsifier, or the wrong temperature.

*The repair* starts a new emulsion and adds the broken one to it slowly: a fresh yolk (or a spoon of water or mustard)
in a clean bowl, then the broken sauce whisked in, a teaspoon at a time at first (`SAU-2`).

*A mayonnaise from one yolk:* the yolk with a pinch of salt and a teaspoon of lemon juice; then 150–200 ml of oil,
drop by drop at first, then in a thin stream once it thickens.

**Practice:**
1. **Temporary and stable (45 min).** Make the two vinaigrettes of `SCI-2.M4.X1`, shaken without and with mustard, and
   start their timer. Meanwhile, make a mayonnaise from one yolk.
2. **Too fast (45 min).** Make a second mayonnaise and pour the oil in a stream from the start, so that it breaks.
   Photograph it broken. Repair it with a new yolk in a clean bowl, and photograph each step.

**Look for:** the mustard vinaigrette stays cloudy and thick for minutes; the repaired mayonnaise thick and glossy.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A greasy, split sauce at once | Oil in a stream at the start | Drops first, a stream once it thickens |
| It won't take | Cold yolks straight from the fridge with warm oil | Both at room temperature |
| A bowl of broken sauce thrown away | Giving up on a broken emulsion | A new yolk, and the broken sauce added slowly |

**Safety:** mayonnaise is made with pasteurised yolk, for every guest, and kept covered in the fridge for at most 24
hours (assessment §9, raw egg). Leftover mayonnaise past that is thrown away.

**Record:** the photos of the broken and repaired mayonnaise (in `SCI-2.M4.X1`).

## SCI-2.M5 — Water, salt, sugar and acid

**Knowledge check:** the 8 items of `SCI-2.M5` in the [course file](../../05-courses/S2/SCI-2.md), 1 of them a safety
item. Pass mark 80 %, with every safety item correct.

### SCI-2.M5.L1 — Water activity

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m5-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · moves SC-MICR→2*

*Objectives:* [K] free water; how salt and sugar bind it (jam, cures, candied fruit)
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** jam is wet but keeps for months; cooked rice is wet and is unsafe in a day. The difference is not
how much water a food holds but how much of it is free. That idea explains preserving, curing and why salted cabbage
goes limp.

**You need:** in winter 400 g of white cabbage, finely shredded; in summer 400 g of cucumber or courgette, thinly
sliced; 4 g of salt; 4 g of sugar; two bowls with two sieves; the scale; a small jug; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Water activity* is how much of a food's water is free: free to be used by microbes, and free to take part in reactions.
It is not the same as water content. Jam is wet, but its water is bound by sugar; dry crackers have little water at all.

*Salt and sugar bind water.* Dissolved, they hold water molecules, so microbes can't get the water they need. That is
how these keep:
- **jam and candied peel:** sugar;
- **cures and salted fish:** salt (and drying);
- **hard cheese:** salt, drying and acid.

*Osmosis in the kitchen.* Salt or sugar on the surface of a vegetable draws water out of its cells, towards the
stronger solution outside:
- salting courgette or cucumber before cooking, so it browns instead of stewing (`VEG-1.M4.L5`);
- salting cabbage for coleslaw, so it softens and doesn't water down the dressing;
- sugar keeping cakes moist, because it holds on to their water.

*Weight for weight,* salt draws more water than sugar: a gram of salt holds more water molecules than a gram of sugar.

**Practice:**
1. **Salt and sugar draw water (45 min).** Divide the vegetable between the two sieves set over the bowls. Toss one
   half with the salt and the other with the sugar. After 30 minutes, press each gently and weigh the liquid in each
   bowl. Taste a piece of each.

**Look for:** a clear pool of liquid under both, more under the salt per gram.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Dried, so it keeps" | Confusing "dry" with "low water activity" | Ask what binds the water, and how much is free |
| Mould on a homemade jam | Thinking a low-sugar jam keeps like a full-sugar one | Low-sugar jam lives in the fridge, and is eaten soon |
| No liquid to weigh | Too short a wait, or thick pieces | Thin slices; 30 minutes |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the two weights of liquid, and a line explaining the difference.

### SCI-2.M5.L2 — Acids and bases

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m5-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M2.L1` · moves SC-BRWN→3, IN-BAK→2*

*Objectives:* [K] pH in cooking: baking soda and browning, acid and green vegetables, buttermilk and leavening
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a pinch of baking soda or a spoon of vinegar changes colour, texture, browning and rise. You have
seen pieces of this in `VEG-1` (pigments, firmness) and `HEA-1` (browning). Here they come together, so you can use pH
on purpose.

**You need:** the inputs of `SCI-2.M5.X1`; pH strips if you have them; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*pH in cooking.* Acids (lemon, vinegar, buttermilk, wine) have a low pH; bases or alkalis (baking soda) a high one.

| | Alkali (baking soda) | Acid (lemon, vinegar) |
|---|---|---|
| Browning | Speeds it: onions and pretzels brown fast | Slows it |
| Vegetables | Softens them, even to mush (`VEG-1.M1.L1`) | Firms them |
| Colour | Red cabbage turns blue; greens stay bright | Red cabbage stays red; greens turn olive (`VEG-1.M3.L1`) |
| Milk | — | Curdles it (*hangop*, paneer) |

*Leavening.*
- **Baking soda** (*zuiveringszout*) is a base. It needs an acid (buttermilk, yoghurt, brown sugar, honey) to release
  carbon dioxide, and it works as soon as it is wet: bake at once.
- **Baking powder** (*bakpoeder*) carries its own acid, so it needs only water and heat.
- **Too much soda** tastes soapy and metallic, and browns too much.

*Measuring pH at home* with strips is optional; the effects are visible.

*Small amounts.* A pinch of baking soda is enough to change browning in a pan of onions: about 0.5 g, an eighth of a
teaspoon. More softens them to a paste.

**Practice:**
1. **Soda onions (45 min).** Run the comparison of `SCI-2.M5.X1`.

**Look for:** the soda onions brown far faster and turn soft and jammy.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Mushy, soapy onions | Too much soda | A small pinch only |
| A difference you can't trust | Comparing at different heats | Two identical pans on burners of the same size |
| A flat, soapy cake | Soda without an acid in the batter | Pair soda with buttermilk or yoghurt, or use baking powder |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the photos and times (in `SCI-2.M5.X1`).

## SCI-2.M6 — Microbiology for cooks

**Knowledge check:** the 8 items of `SCI-2.M6` in the [course file](../../05-courses/S2/SCI-2.md), 5 of them safety
items. Pass mark 80 %, with every safety item correct. A safety item failed means a resit.

### SCI-2.M6.L1 — Growth, spores and toxins

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m6-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SAF-1.M3.L3` · moves SC-MICR→3, SA-HRPR→1*

*Objectives:* [K] spore-formers and heat-stable toxins; why reheating doesn't always save food; fermentation as controlled growth
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m6-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** "cooking kills everything" is the most dangerous belief in a home kitchen. Some organisms survive
cooking as spores, and some leave toxins that no amount of reheating removes. Knowing which, and why, is what makes the
rules of `SAF-1` make sense, and what lets you judge a situation the rules don't cover.

**You need:** this lesson; six foods from your own kitchen or fridge; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*What microbes need to grow:* time, a suitable temperature, moisture, nutrients, the right acidity, and, for some, the
absence of oxygen. Take one away, and growth slows or stops.

*Spore-formers* survive cooking as spores and grow when cooked food cools slowly or is held warm:
- *Bacillus cereus*: rice, pasta and grains (`STA-1.M4.L3`);
- *Clostridium perfringens*: large pots of stew, gravy and braised meat cooled slowly;
- *Clostridium botulinum*: grows without oxygen in low-acid food. That is why garlic or herbs in oil need refrigeration
  ([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)), and why preserves need tested methods
  [S27].

*Heat-stable toxins.* *Staphylococcus aureus* (from hands) and *B. cereus* form toxins that reheating does not destroy.
Once they have formed, the food is unsafe, however hot you make it. So the defence is to prevent growth: fast cooling,
cold storage, clean hands.

*Fermentation* is controlled growth. In yoghurt, sauerkraut and sourdough, lactic acid bacteria lower the pH and crowd
out pathogens. It is safe because of the conditions (salt, acid, temperature, a tested ratio), not by luck.

| Food | Organism of most concern | Does cooking remove it? |
|---|---|---|
| Cooked rice kept for tomorrow | *B. cereus* | No: the toxin survives; cool fast |
| Raw chicken | *Campylobacter*, *Salmonella* | Yes, at 75 °C; watch cross-contamination |
| Garlic in oil | *C. botulinum* | Not relevant: it is stored raw; keep it in the fridge |

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): for six foods in your kitchen, name the organism of most concern and why cooking does
or doesn't remove it, in a table like the one above. Then explain, in a few sentences, why reheated rice can make people
ill.

**Look for:** you explain why reheated rice can make people ill.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Food kept "because it will be cooked again" | "Cooking kills everything" | Ask: spores? A toxin? |
| A jar of garlic oil in the cupboard | Infused oils left in the cupboard | The fridge, and used within days |
| A soft, foul ferment | A home ferment without salt or without a tested ratio | Tested ratios only (`PRS-3`) |

**Safety:** the risks in this lesson are prevented by the §9 rules you already use: fast cooling, the fridge at 4 °C,
leftovers within 2 days, garlic or herbs in oil in the fridge, and tested methods for preserves.

**Record:** the table of six foods and the rice explanation.

### SCI-2.M6.L2 — Hurdles

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sci-2-m6-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M6.L1` · moves SC-MICR→3, SA-TEMP→4*

*Objectives:* [K] temperature, acid, salt, water activity and oxygen as combined hurdles; [J] apply them to leftovers and storage; [S] a hurdle plan for three of your own foods
<!-- END generated:lesson-sci-2-m6-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** no single factor keeps most foods safe. A pickle is safe because it is acidic and salted and cold;
a cured ham because it is salted, dried and stored cool. Seeing food as a stack of hurdles lets you judge your own
leftovers, batches and preserves, and see when a process needs a tested method.

**You need:** a batch of soup or braise of 2–3 litres (the week's lunches, for example the vegetable soup of
`TST-1.M4`); two or three shallow containers, or a sink or large bowl with ice and water; the probe thermometer; a
clock; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Hurdles* are the factors that slow microbes:
- **temperature:** cold, or hot holding;
- **acid:** a low pH;
- **salt or sugar:** a low water activity (`SCI-2.M5.L1`);
- **oxygen:** its absence or presence;
- **competing microbes:** fermentation;
- **preservatives:** such as nitrite in a cure.

*Hurdles combine.* One alone may not be enough; two or three together make a food safe. A quick pickle is acidic,
salted and kept in the fridge. Hard cheese is salted, dry and acidic.

*Applying them at home:*
- **leftovers** rely on temperature: fast cooling and the fridge;
- **a home preserve** relies on a tested combination, followed exactly [S27].

*Cooling a batch by the measurable standard*
([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)):
- from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours;
- then to 5 °C or below within 6 hours in total.

A large pot cools far too slowly. Divide it into shallow containers, or stand the pot in a sink of ice water and stir.
Log the probe reading in the centre every 30 minutes.

**Practice:**
1. **A hurdle plan (60 min).** Write the hurdle plan for three foods you make, for example the batch soup, quick
   pickles and a garlic oil: each food's hurdles, with a number for each (a temperature, a time, a salt or acid
   level). Then cool the soup by the plan, with a log, for `SCI-2.M6.X1`.

**Look for:** each food has at least one named hurdle with a number; the cooling log meets the standard.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "It smells fine" | Relying on smell | Pathogens and toxins don't smell; follow the hurdles |
| A pot of soup out overnight | Leaving a pot of soup to cool on the stove overnight | Shallow containers or an ice bath, then the fridge |
| Garlic oil on the shelf | Making garlic oil and storing it at room temperature | In the fridge, used within days |

**Safety:** this lesson is the cooling rule: from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C or below within 6 hours,
then reheated once to 74 °C (assessment §9).

**Record:** the hurdle plan and the cooling log (in `SCI-2.M6.X1`).

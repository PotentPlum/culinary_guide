**Before you start.** Chicken is the protein most home cooks cook most often, and the one they most often cook badly:
breasts dry and stringy, legs underdone at the bone, a tin of pale skin. It is also the protein with the strictest
hygiene. Raw poultry often carries *Campylobacter* and *Salmonella*, and the spread from board, hands and tap matters as
much as the core temperature.

The course treats the bird as a whole: buying it by label and use, handling it cleanly, breaking it down, cooking it
three ways (roasted, sautéed or pan-roasted, and poached), and using every part. It ends with a lab that sets your eye
and fingers against the thermometer, and puts dry-brining to a blind test.

*Doneness is the thread.* Every piece is probed. The safe figure for poultry is 75 °C
([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)), reached after resting. You learn to pull
a breast early enough that carryover takes it there without overshooting, and to cook the legs further, because their
connective tissue needs it. A reading below 75 °C after resting means the piece goes back to cook further: it is never
served.

*Hygiene is the other thread.* Every session with raw poultry uses the station of `POU-2.M1.L2`: a separate board, no
rinsing, hands washed after every raw touch, and a full clean-down.

*Every bird is used completely.* Plan four whole chickens over the course: two for the breakdowns of module 2, whose
pieces feed modules 3 to 5; one for the Sunday roast; one for the project of module 5. Their carcasses fill the stock
bag.

Plan about four weeks. The roasts and the whole-bird project are weekend cooking; sautés and breasts fit weeknights.

**Have ready:** the chef's knife and the boning knife, sharpened; kitchen or poultry shears; the second board for raw
protein, and a tray for raw meat and a plate for cooked; a roasting tin that fits a 2 kg bird; the sauté pan
(ovenproof); the stockpot; the probe, checked in boiling water; shallow containers with lids; kitchen paper; the phone
and phone stand; the scale; the hygiene checklist (`SAF-1`); the tasting form and the comparison template (`SCI-2`);
the journal and the waste log.

### POU-2.M1.L1 — Types, labels and buying

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · moves IN-MEA→2, KM-PURC→2*

*Objectives:* [K] chicken, duck, turkey, guinea fowl; welfare and quality labels; [J] choose by use and budget (Good/Best)
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a slow-growing chicken can cost two or three times as much as a standard broiler, and it cooks
differently. Reading the label and the price per kilo tells you what you are paying for, and which piece to buy
for the dish you have in mind.

**You need:** a visit to a supermarket and, if there is one near you, a poulterer (*poelier*); the journal; a phone for
photos of labels and prices.

**Explanation:**

*The birds:*
- **chicken:** from a fast-growing broiler of about six weeks to a slow-growing bird of two to three months, with
  firmer, more flavourful meat;
- **duck:** dark, fatty meat, with a thick fat layer under the skin;
- **turkey:** large and lean, mostly bought as parts in the Netherlands;
- **guinea fowl (*parelhoen*):** between chicken and game.

*The parts and their method:*

| Part | Meat | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Breast | Lean, quick to overcook | Sauté, pan-roast, poach |
| Thigh | Dark, more fat and connective tissue | Sauté, braise, roast |
| Drumstick | Dark, sinewy | Roast, braise |
| Wing | Skin and gelatin | Roast, fry, stock |
| Carcass and neck | Bone, gelatin | Stock |

*Labels in Dutch shops:*
- ***Beter Leven*** stars (1 to 3) for animal welfare;
- **organic** (the EU leaf, *biologisch*);
- **free range** (*vrije uitloop*: access to the outdoors) and ***scharrel*** (indoors, with more space than standard):
  neither is organic;
- **"slow-growing"** breeds;
- **the use-by date (*te gebruiken tot*)** on fresh poultry: a safety date, not a quality one. After it, the bird is
  thrown away, however it smells.

*Choosing by use and budget:*
- **Good:** a whole standard chicken. It is cheaper per kilo than parts, and the carcass makes stock.
- **Best:** a slow-growing or free-range bird of 1.5–2 kg, from a poulterer.

For a quick weeknight sauté, thighs give more flavour per euro than breasts.

*The price per kilo of usable meat.* A whole bird gives a little over two thirds of its weight as pieces
(`POU-2.M2.L1`); the rest is carcass for stock. Divide its price per kilo by about 0.7 to compare it with parts.

**Practice:**
1. **A shop visit (45 min).** Compare whole birds and parts on label, weight and price per kilo, and work out the price
   per kilo of usable meat for one whole bird. Photograph three labels. Buy the bird for `POU-2.M2`.

**Look for:** you can say what each label guarantees and what it doesn't; a price per kilo of usable meat worked out.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dry weeknight sauté | Buying breasts for a dish that wants thighs | Match the part to the method |
| Paying organic prices for less | Reading "*scharrel*" as organic | Look for the EU leaf |
| A food-poisoning risk | Using a bird past its use-by date because it smells fine | After the date, it is thrown away |

**Safety:** the use-by date is a safety date; raw poultry goes home cool, in a cool bag in summer, and into the fridge
at 4 °C at once (assessment §9).

**Record:** the three label photos with a line each, and the price comparison.

### POU-2.M1.L2 — Handling raw poultry

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m1-l2 -->
*1 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAF-1.M2.L2` · moves SA-HYG→4*

*Objectives:* [S] no rinsing; a separate board; clean-down; [K] *Campylobacter* and *Salmonella* risks
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** cooking kills the bacteria on chicken, but only in the chicken. The food-poisoning cases at home
come from what the raw bird touched on its way to the oven: a tap, a towel, the salad. A routine you follow without
thinking is the only reliable protection.

**You need:** a chicken or chicken pieces; the raw board, a tray for the raw meat and a plate for the cooked; kitchen
paper; salt in a small bowl (pre-portioned); soap at the sink; a bin within reach; the roasting tin; a helper with the
hygiene checklist (`SAF-1`).

**Explanation:**

*The risks.* Raw poultry often carries *Campylobacter* and *Salmonella*. Cooking to 75 °C (assessment §9) kills them in
the meat. The danger at home is spreading them, by hands, boards, knives, taps, towels and splashes, to food that will
not be cooked.

*No rinsing.* Washing a chicken under the tap does not make it safer. It splashes bacteria over the sink and the
worktop, up to tens of centimetres away. Pat it dry with kitchen paper, and throw the paper straight into the bin.

*The station:*
- a separate board for raw protein (`SAF-1`);
- a tray or plate for the raw meat, and a different one for the cooked;
- the bin and the soap within reach;
- ready-to-eat food finished and put away first, so the raw protein comes last in the work order (`KNF-2.M2`).

*During the work:*
- wash your hands after touching raw poultry and before touching anything else: the tap, a spice jar, the phone, the
  fridge door;
- use one hand for the raw meat and the other for the salt, or pre-portion the salt into a small bowl;
- open the oven door and the bin before you pick up the bird.

*Clean-down,* in order (`SAF-1.M2.L3`): remove, wash, rinse, disinfect, dry. The board and knife go into a hot
dishwasher cycle or are washed in hot water with detergent; the worktop, the tap and any handles you touched are cleaned
and disinfected. The cloth goes into the wash.

*Storage:* in the fridge at 4 °C, on the bottom shelf, in a closed container, so it can't drip onto other food.

**Practice:**
1. **A dry run (30 min).** Set up the station, handle a chicken (unpack, pat dry, season, into the tin), and clean down.
   The helper watches with the hygiene checklist and marks each contact point.

**Look for:** hands washed after each touch of raw meat; no raw-contact item touching ready-to-eat food; a clean station
at the end, with nothing left on the tap or handles.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Splashes across the sink | Rinsing the bird | Pat dry with paper; never rinse |
| Bacteria on the salt and the phone | Touching the salt jar or the phone with raw hands | Pre-portion the salt; wash hands first |
| Cooked meat recontaminated | Using the same tray for raw and cooked chicken | Two trays, or wash between |
| Bacteria spread around the kitchen | Wiping the board with the dishcloth used for everything else | Hot wash; a fresh cloth |

**Safety:** this lesson is the safety rule: no rinsing, hands washed after every raw touch, raw last in the work order,
and a full clean-down (`SAF-1.M2`, assessment §9).

**Record:** the helper's checklist, with each contact point and what you will change.

### POU-2.M2.L1 — Eight pieces

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m2-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `KNF-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-FAB→2*

*Objectives:* [S] legs, thighs, drumsticks, breasts, wings; follow the joints; keep the carcass
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a whole chicken costs much less per kilo than its parts, and gives you a carcass for stock. Breaking
it down takes ten minutes once you know where the joints are, and it is the same skill a butcher uses on any animal:
follow the structure, not the bone.

**You need:** a whole chicken of about 1.5 kg (the first bird of `POU-2.M2.X1`); the chef's or boning knife, sharp
(`KNF-2.M2.L1`); shears; the raw station; the scale; a tray for the pieces; a freezer bag for the carcass; the course
video on the phone stand.

**Explanation:**

*Follow the joints, not the bone.* A knife goes through a joint without force. Where it meets bone, the cut is in the
wrong place: move the tip a few millimetres and feel for the gap.

*The sequence:*
1. **The legs.** Breast up, legs towards you. Cut the skin between leg and body; bend the leg back until the hip joint
   pops; cut through the joint, turning the knife along the back to keep the oyster (the small round of meat on the
   back) on the thigh.
2. **Thigh and drumstick.** Skin side down, find the line of fat across the joint; cut along it, through the joint.
3. **The wings.** Pull each wing out, find the shoulder joint, and cut through it. Cut off the tips for stock.
4. **The breasts.** Off the bone, along each side of the breastbone, keeping the knife against the ribs; or on the bone,
   cutting through the ribs with the heel of the knife or with shears.

*The result:* eight pieces (two drumsticks, two thighs, two wings, two breasts), with the carcass, the wing tips and the
trim frozen for stock (`STK-2`).

*Yield.* Weigh the bird, the pieces and the carcass. The pieces are usually a little over two thirds of the bird's
weight. A lot of meat left on the carcass shows in the numbers.

*The station* is set up as in `POU-2.M1.L2`: the raw board, a tray, hands washed after each raw touch.

**Practice:**
1. **A first breakdown (60 min).** Follow the course video, paused at each step.
2. **A second breakdown (45 min).** Without pausing. Weigh the pieces and the carcass, and log the yield.

**Look for:** the knife finding the joints; the oyster on the thigh; the breastbone scraped clean; pieces of even size,
with the skin intact.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Splinters of bone | Sawing through bone | Find the joint; move the tip |
| A small thigh | Leaving the oyster on the carcass | Turn the knife along the back |
| Torn skin on the breasts | Pulling, or cutting too close to the skin | Keep the knife against the ribs |
| Slow work, slipping knife | A blunt knife, which makes the work slow and unsafe | Hone or sharpen first |

**Safety:** a sharp knife and the raw station of `POU-2.M1.L2`; cut away from the hand that holds the bird. The pieces
go into the fridge at once, covered, on the bottom shelf.

**Record:** the weights and the yield.

### POU-2.M2.L2 — Spatchcock and boned thigh

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m2-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `POU-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-FAB→2*

*Objectives:* [S] spatchcock a chicken; bone a thigh
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a spatchcocked chicken roasts in about two thirds of the time of a whole one, and evenly, because
the legs get more heat and the breast less. A boned thigh cooks in minutes and is the most forgiving piece of chicken
there is.

**You need:** a whole chicken of 1.5–2 kg; two chicken thighs, bone in; shears or a heavy knife; the boning knife; the
raw station; a roasting tin; the probe.

**Explanation:**

*Spatchcocking:*
1. **Breast down,** cut along both sides of the backbone with shears or a heavy knife, and lift it out. The backbone
   goes into the stock bag.
2. **Turn the bird over** and press it flat with the heel of your hand on the breastbone until it cracks.
3. **Tuck the wing tips** behind the breast.

It roasts or grills evenly and fast: the legs are exposed to more heat, and the breast less.

*Boning a thigh:*
1. **Skin side down,** cut along the bone on the inside of the thigh.
2. **Scrape** the meat free around the bone with the tip of the knife.
3. **Cut under the knuckle** at each end, and lift the bone out.
4. **Feel** for cartilage and cut it out.

A boned thigh cooks quickly and evenly: for a sauté, a stuffing or the grill.

*Roasting a spatchcocked bird.* Skin up in the tin, in a hot oven (about 200–220 °C), for about 45 minutes for a 1.5 kg
bird. Probe the breast and the thigh, as in `POU-2.M3.L1`: 75 °C or more after the rest in the breast, and the thigh
above that.

**Practice:**
1. **Spatchcock and bone (50 min).** Spatchcock a chicken and bone two thighs. Roast the spatchcocked bird for dinner,
   probed; freeze the thighs flat, or cook them in a sauté (`POU-2.M4.L2`).

**Look for:** the bird lying flat; the thigh bone coming out clean, with the meat in one piece.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A split breast | Cutting through the breast in place of along the backbone | Breast down; cut beside the backbone |
| Gristle in the bite | Leaving cartilage in the thigh | Feel for it and cut it out |
| Torn, shrinking skin | Piercing the skin | Work from the inside of the thigh |

**Safety:** shears and a heavy knife need a firm grip and a stable board; the raw station applies (`POU-2.M1.L2`).

**Record:** a photo of the flat bird and the boned thighs, and the probe readings of the roast.

### POU-2.M3.L1 — Roasting a whole chicken

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m3-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 1.5 h · builds on `HEA-2.M2.L2` · moves TS-PRO→3, IN-MEA→3*

*Objectives:* [S] dry, season, truss or not, roast, rest; [K] breast versus leg temperatures
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a roast chicken is the classic home meal and the classic home failure, because the bird is two
foods in one: a lean breast that dries out above 75 °C, and legs that are better well above it. Roasting it well is
the art of closing that gap.

**You need:** for each roast, a whole chicken of 1.5–2 kg; salt; butter or oil; a lemon and thyme (optional); the
roasting tin; kitchen string (optional); the probe; a timer; a board for carving; foil.

**Explanation:**

*The problem of the whole bird.* The breast is done at 75 °C and dries out quickly above it. The legs are better well
above that, around 80–85 °C, where their connective tissue softens. Everything in roasting a chicken is about closing
that gap:
- **dry skin:** pat it dry, or dry-brine it uncovered in the fridge overnight (`SCI-2.M2.L2`), for crisp skin and
  seasoned meat;
- **trussing or not:** trussing gives a neat shape, but it shields the thighs and slows them down. An untrussed bird, or
  one with the legs loosely tied, cooks more evenly;
- **position and heat:** a hot oven, about 200–220 °C; the legs towards the back of the oven, where it is usually
  hottest (check your oven table); a spatchcocked bird (`POU-2.M2.L2`) evens it out further.

*Timing* is a guide only: about 1 hour to 1 hour 20 minutes for a 1.5–2 kg bird. The probe decides.

*Probing:*
- the breast at its thickest part, from the neck end, the tip in the centre;
- the thigh near the bone, without touching it.

The breast comes out a few degrees below 75 °C. During the rest it must reach 75 °C or more (assessment §9), confirmed
by the probe, and the legs must be well above that. If the breast reads below 75 °C after the rest, the bird goes back
into the oven.

*Resting:* 15–20 minutes, loosely covered with foil (`HEA-2.M2.L2`). The juices in the tin go into the gravy.

*Carving:* the legs off first, through the hip joint; then each breast off the bone in one piece, sliced across.

**Practice:**
1. **A first roast (90 min, plus resting).** With the probe log: the breast and the thigh every 10 minutes in the last
   half hour, and again after the rest.
2. **A second roast (90 min).** Spatchcocked or dry-brined, compared with the first.

**Look for:** skin browned and crisp; the juices from the thigh running clear; the leg moving loosely in its joint; the
probe reading after the rest at or above the figure.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pink at the thigh bone | Pulling the bird on the breast reading alone, with the thighs underdone | Probe both; the thigh decides |
| A dry, stringy breast | Roasting until the breast reads 80 °C or more | Pull a few degrees below 75 °C; let the rest finish it |
| Pale, soft skin | A wet bird going into the oven | Pat dry, or dry-brine uncovered |
| Juice all over the board | Carving without resting | Rest 15–20 minutes |

**Safety:** the breast at 75 °C or more after the rest, and the thigh above it, probed and logged (assessment §9). The
tin holds hot fat: lift it with both hands and oven gloves.

**Record:** the probe logs of both roasts and a line comparing them.

### POU-2.M3.L2 — Gravy from the pan

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m3-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAU-2.M2.L1`, `SAU-2.M3.L3` · moves TS-SAU→3*

*Objectives:* [S] deglaze the roasting pan, thicken, strain, season
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** the roasting tin holds the most concentrated flavour of the whole meal. A gravy made from it in the
twenty minutes the bird rests ties the plate together, and it is a pan sauce on a larger scale.

**You need:** the roasting tin of the first roast; a heatproof jug; a spoon; 300 ml of stock from the freezer; a glass
of white wine or water; a tablespoon of flour, or cornflour for a slurry; a whisk; a sieve; a small saucepan.

**Explanation:**

*The tin is the fond:* the browned juices and the fat of the roast.

*The sequence,* applying `SAU-2.M2.L1` and `SAU-2.M3.L3`:
1. **Defat:** pour the juices into the jug and let the fat rise; spoon it off and keep it.
2. **Deglaze** the tin on the hob with wine or water, scraping.
3. **Add** the stock and the defatted juices.
4. **Reduce.**
5. **Thicken lightly:** a tablespoon of the fat cooked with a tablespoon of flour as a roux, a slurry, or *beurre
   manié*.
6. **Strain,** and season at the end.

*The consistency.* A pouring gravy is thinner than a *nappe* sauce: it coats the spoon lightly and runs.

*A black tin* gives a bitter gravy: if the fond has burnt, make the gravy in a saucepan from stock and the defatted
juices, without deglazing.

*The fat you keep* roasts the potatoes of the next roast (`POU-2.M5.L2`). Keep it covered in the fridge.

**Practice:**
1. **A gravy from the first roast (30 min).** Defat, deglaze, thicken, strain. Photograph it on a spoon.

**Look for:** a glossy, brown gravy without a layer of fat; it tastes of the roast, not of flour.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A greasy gravy | Not defatted | Let the fat rise; spoon it off |
| Lumps | Lumps from dry flour | Cook it as a roux, or slurry it |
| A bitter gravy | A burnt tin deglazed | Gravy from stock and juices alone |
| Too salty | Salting before reducing | Salt at the end |

**Safety:** the hot tin goes onto the hob with oven gloves; it holds its heat for a long time, so keep the handles
covered with a cloth.

**Record:** the spoon photo and a tasting line.

### POU-2.M3.L3 — A roast dinner timeline

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m3-l3 -->
*1 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `WRK-1.M2.L3` · moves KM-PLAN→3*

*Objectives:* [S] a backwards timeline for a roast with two sides and gravy
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m3-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** the hard part of a Sunday roast is not the chicken but getting the chicken, two vegetables and a
gravy to the table hot at the same moment. A timeline written backwards from the serving time makes it routine.

**You need:** this lesson; the journal; the menu of `POU-2.M3.X1` (the bird, two vegetables, the gravy); your oven
table; a timer.

**Explanation:**

## POU-2.M4 — Sautéing and pan-roasting

item. Pass mark 80 %, with every safety item correct.

### POU-2.M4.L1 — Pan-roasting a breast

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m4-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-2.M2.L2` · moves TS-PRO→3*

*Objectives:* [S] even thickness, sear, finish in the oven, rest; [J] pull at the right temperature
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a chicken breast is the easiest piece of meat to dry out. An even thickness, a sear, a short finish
in the oven and a measured pull make it juicy every time.

**You need:** for each session, 2 chicken breasts, skin on if you can get them (the breasts of `POU-2.M2.X1`); salt;
oil; an ovenproof sauté pan; baking paper and a rolling pin or small pan; the probe; a timer; a board.

**Explanation:**

*Even thickness first.* A breast is thick at one end and thin at the other. Flatten the thick end gently between two
sheets of baking paper, or cut it horizontally into two thinner pieces.

*Pan-roasting:*
1. **Season,** and dry-brine if there is time (an hour or more, uncovered in the fridge).
2. **Sear** skin side down in a hot ovenproof pan until golden, 4–5 minutes, without moving it.
3. **Turn** the breast.
4. **Finish** in an oven at about 200 °C, for about 8–12 minutes depending on the thickness.

The sear gives colour, and the oven cooks it evenly.

*Pulling at the right temperature.* Carryover in a breast is small but real: a few degrees.
- **When to pull:** a few degrees below 75 °C.
- **The rest:** 5 minutes, on a warm plate.
- **The check:** probe again after the rest. The reading must be 75 °C or more (assessment §9). If it is below, the
  breast goes back into the pan or the oven, and is probed again.

Write the pull temperature you choose and the reading after the rest, and adjust next time.

*Skin-on or skinless.* Skin protects the meat and crisps. A skinless breast needs a gentler finish: a lower setting, or
a lid.

**Practice:**
1. **Two breasts (45 min).** One flattened and one not, pan-roasted together. Probe both, rest them, cut them, and
   compare the cross-sections.
2. **A pull-temperature trial (45 min).** Two breasts pulled at two temperatures you choose below 75 °C, for example 3
   and 5 degrees below. Rest them, probe again, and taste. Any breast below 75 °C after the rest goes back to finish.

**Look for:** an even, golden skin; meat moist and opaque to the centre, with juices that run clear; the probe after the
rest at or above the figure.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Raw at one end, dry at the other | A thick end raw while the thin end dries | Flatten, or cut in two |
| Under the figure after the rest | Pulling too early, so the rest doesn't reach the safe figure | Back in the pan; pull a degree later next time |
| Juice on the board, dry meat | Cutting straight away, so the juices run out | Rest 5 minutes |
| Pale skin | Pale skin from a cold pan | A hot pan; don't move it |

**Safety:** 75 °C or more after the rest, probed; below it, the breast is cooked further (assessment §9). The probe is
wiped between raw and cooked readings.

**Record:** the pull temperatures, the readings after the rest, and the cross-section photos.

### POU-2.M4.L2 — Sautéed chicken with a pan sauce

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m4-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SAU-2.M2.L2` · moves TS-DRY→3, CR-COMP→1*

*Objectives:* [S] sauté thighs, build a pan sauce; [S] compose a plate with a vegetable
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a sauté of thighs with a pan sauce is the weeknight chicken dinner: 45 minutes, one pan, and almost
impossible to dry out. It is also your first composed plate, where the protein, a vegetable and a sauce each have a
place.

**You need:** for 2, per session: 4 chicken thighs, bone in or boned (`POU-2.M2.L2`); a shallot; 100 ml of wine or
dry cider; 150 ml of stock; 20 g of cold butter; a green vegetable in season (beans or peas in summer, leeks or savoy
cabbage in winter); salt and pepper; the heavy sauté pan with a lid; the probe; warm plates.

**Explanation:**

*The sauté* (*poulet sauté*): the pieces browned skin side down in a heavy pan, then cooked through, partly covered or
in the oven. The pan sauce is built on the fond (`SAU-2.M2.L2`).

*Thighs forgive.* They stay juicy well above 75 °C and are the easiest piece to sauté. Bone-in thighs take about 25–30
minutes in all; boned thighs about 12–15.

*The pan.* Heavy, and not crowded, so the pieces brown rather than steam (`HEA-1.M3`). Leave a finger's width between
them. Let the skin brown until it releases by itself: turned too early, it tears.

*Composing a plate* (`CR-COMP` level 1):
- the protein as the focus;
- a vegetable in season for colour and contrast;
- the sauce under or beside the meat, not over the crisp skin;
- a clean rim.

*Before you plate,* look at it as a guest would: where does the eye go first? It should go to the chicken.

**Practice:**
1. **A sauté of thighs (60 min).** Brown the thighs skin side down, turn them, and finish them partly covered until the
   thickest reads at least 75 °C near the bone. Rest them on a warm plate while you build a pan sauce with wine or
   cider, stock and cold butter. Cook a green vegetable.
2. **A second sauté (60 min).** A different sauce (cream and mustard, or a *gastrique*) and a different vegetable. Plate
   both versions for the household, and photograph them.

**Look for:** an even, crisp skin; a pan sauce at *nappe*; a plate where the eye goes first to the chicken.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Grey, steamed pieces | Crowding the pan | Fewer pieces, or two pans |
| Torn skin | Turning the pieces too early, so the skin tears | Wait until it releases |
| Soggy skin | The sauce poured over the crisp skin | Sauce under or beside |
| A beige plate | A plate with no colour | A bright vegetable; herbs |

**Safety:** at least 75 °C near the bone, probed (assessment §9). Hot fat spits when the pieces go in: lay them away
from you.

**Record:** the two plate photos and the probe readings.

### POU-2.M5.L1 — Poached poultry

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m5-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M5.L4` · moves TS-MOI→4*

*Objectives:* [S] poach a whole chicken or legs; keep the broth; [J] check doneness
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** poaching gives the most tender chicken there is, and a broth as a bonus. It is the meat for chicken
salad, soup and the *suprême* of `SAU-2`, and it teaches the gentlest of the moist-heat methods.

**You need:** a whole chicken of about 1.5 kg, or 4 legs; 2.5–3 litres of water or light stock; an onion, a carrot, a
leek green, a *bouquet garni* and a few peppercorns (`STK-2.M1`); salt; the stockpot; the probe; a timer; the fine
sieve; shallow containers and an ice bath.

**Explanation:**

*A delicate poach:* poultry in liquid held at about 70–85 °C, below a simmer, the surface barely trembling
(`HEA-1.M5.L4`). A gentle poach keeps the breast tender; a boil tightens the meat and clouds the broth. Find the burner
setting that holds it, as for the stock in `STK-2.M2.L2`, and log the liquid every 15 minutes.

*The liquid:* water or light stock with aromatics, lightly salted (about 8 g per litre). The bird or the legs go in cold
or into hot liquid:
- **starting cold** gives a clearer broth and even cooking of a whole bird;
- **starting hot** is faster for parts.

A whole bird of 1.5 kg takes about an hour to an hour and a quarter once the liquid is at temperature; legs about 40
minutes.

*Checking doneness:*
- probe the thickest part of the thigh and of the breast: 75 °C or more (assessment §9);
- the juices run clear;
- the leg moves loosely.

For a whole bird, the thigh decides: it is the last to get there.

*Keep the broth.* It is a light stock for soups and risottos. Strain it, cool it within the limits of assessment §9
(from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C within 6 hours in total), and freeze it (`STK-2.M2.L5`).

*Why poach:* for the *suprême* (`SAU-2.M3.X2`), salads, soups and sandwiches. The meat stays moist for cold dishes. For
a cold dish, cool the meat by the same standard, in a shallow container.

**Practice:**
1. **A poached chicken or legs (90 min).** Log the liquid temperature every 15 minutes and hold it at 70–85 °C; probe
   the core; strain the broth and cool it, logged.

**Look for:** a surface that trembles but doesn't bubble; moist, tender meat; a clear broth.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Tough meat, cloudy broth | Boiling the liquid | Lower the heat; hold 70–85 °C |
| Pink at the thigh | Taking the bird out on the breast reading alone | Probe the thigh; it decides |
| Broth still warm in the morning | Leaving the broth to cool overnight on the stove | Shallow containers in an ice bath |

**Safety:** 75 °C or more in the thigh and the breast; the broth and any meat for cold dishes cooled by the standard,
and used within 2 days or frozen (assessment §9).

**Record:** the liquid log, the core readings and the cooling log.

### POU-2.M6.L1 — Brined versus unbrined

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m6-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `SCI-2.M2.L2` · moves RD-TEST→2, SC-PROT→3*

*Objectives:* [S] compare juiciness and seasoning with blind tasters
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m6-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** every cooking magazine says that brining makes chicken juicier. In `SCI-2.M2.X1` you measured the
effect on small pieces. Here you test it at the scale of a meal, with blind tasters, and find out whether your household
can taste the difference.

**You need:** 4 chicken legs or breasts, matched in weight within about 10 %; coarse salt; the scale; a roasting tin;
the probe; four coded plates; a helper to code them; the comparison template (`SCI-2.M1.L1`); the tasting forms.

**Explanation:**

*The question:* "does dry-brining make roast chicken juicier and better seasoned at the table?".

*Dry-brining* (`SCI-2.M2.L2`): salt at about 1–1.5 % of the weight, spread evenly, and the pieces rested uncovered in
the fridge for about a day. The salt draws out juice, dissolves in it, and is drawn back in, seasoning the meat and
changing its proteins so they hold more water. *Wet-brining* (soaking in salted water) is named for comparison and not
tested here.

*The design* (`SCI-2.M1.L1`), on the comparison template:
- **one variable:** dry-brined or not;
- **everything else the same:** the same cut, size, oven and pull temperature, roasted in the same tin;
- **the measurements:** each piece weighed before and after cooking, for the cooking loss in %;
- **the tasting:** blind, with plates coded by the helper and the household on the form.

*Reading the result.* One trial is a hint, not a proof (`SCI-2.M1.Q6`). The conclusion says how sure it is: "in this
trial, with four tasters, the brined pieces lost 3 percentage points less and were preferred three times out of four".

**Practice:**
1. **The comparison (60 min, plus a day of brining).** Two pairs of chicken pieces, one pair dry-brined; all roasted
   together to the same pull temperature, each confirmed at 75 °C or more after the rest; weighed, probed, and tasted
   blind.

**Look for:** a template filled in before cooking; pieces matched in weight within about 10 %; plates coded by someone
else.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A result that means nothing | Changing two things at once (for example brined and a hotter oven) | One variable only |
| Weights that don't compare | Unmatched pieces | Within about 10 % |
| Tasters guessing what you hope | Tasters who know which is which | Plates coded by someone else |
| A claim the data can't carry | A conclusion stronger than one trial supports | Say how sure it is |

**Safety:** the brining pieces sit uncovered on a rack over a tray on the bottom shelf, away from other food; every
piece reaches 75 °C or more (assessment §9).

**Record:** the filled template, the weights, the cooking loss and the conclusion (in `POU-2.M6.X1`).

### POU-2.M6.L2 — Doneness by cue and thermometer

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pou-2-m6-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `POU-2.M4.L1` · moves JD-CUES→3, SA-TEMP→4*

*Objectives:* [J] predict doneness by touch, juices and look, then check with the thermometer
<!-- END generated:lesson-pou-2-m6-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** experienced cooks can tell when chicken is nearly done by touch and look. That judgement is
learned by predicting, then checking with the probe, until the two agree. The probe still has the last word.

**You need:** several chicken pieces for a sauté or pan-roast; the probe; the journal, ruled for the prediction log;
shallow containers; an ice bath.

**Explanation:**

*The cues for poultry:*
- **touch:** a raw breast is soft, and a done one springs back firmly when pressed with a fingertip;
- **juices:** pink and cloudy when raw, clear when done;
- **look:** the flesh opaque at the thickest point;
- **legs:** they move loosely, and the meat pulls away from the bone at the drumstick.

*The cue-then-reading entry:*
1. Predict the core temperature from the cues.
2. Write the prediction down.
3. Probe, and log the difference.

| Date | Piece | Cues | Predicted (°C) | Probe (°C) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Ten entries, with at least eight within ±3 °C, show that your cues are calibrated (`JD-CUES` level 3).

*The thermometer still decides.* A cue can say "close", but the safe figure is confirmed by the probe: 75 °C for poultry
(assessment §9). Clear juices alone are not enough in a thick piece.

*Cooling and reheating leftovers* from the lab, with the figures of §9:
- cooled from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C or below within 6 hours in total, in shallow containers;
- reheated to 74 °C for the next day's meal.

**Practice:**
1. **Predict and probe (60 min).** During a sauté or pan-roast of several pieces, predict and log at least four
   readings. Cool the leftover pieces in a shallow container, with the cooling logged.

**Look for:** predictions that close in on the probe reading as the entries accumulate.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A log that proves nothing | Probing before writing the prediction | Write first, then probe |
| Juices lost, a false feel | Pressing the meat with a fork rather than a finger | A fingertip, gently |
| An underdone centre | Trusting clear juices alone in a thick piece | The probe decides |

**Safety:** every piece confirmed at 75 °C or more by the probe; the leftovers cooled by the standard and reheated to
74 °C, once (assessment §9).

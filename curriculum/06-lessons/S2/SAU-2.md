**Before you start.** A sauce turns cooked food into a dish. It brings moisture, richness, acidity or contrast, and it
carries the flavour of the pan, the stock or the vegetable into every bite. Sauces have a reputation for being
difficult, but most of that difficulty comes down to three physical rules:
- how far to reduce;
- how starch thickens;
- how fat stays suspended in water.

The course teaches those rules through the five sauce elements of `TS-SAU`: pan sauces and reductions, starch-thickened
sauces, cold emulsions, warm emulsions and vinaigrettes. It ends with a sauce clinic, where you repair faults you did
not cause and did not see coming.

*Consistency is the thread.* Every sauce is judged on a spoon. Dip the back of a spoon in it and lift it out: at *nappe*
it coats the spoon as a thin, even layer, and a line drawn through it with a finger stays clean. Photograph that line
for every element, on a plain spoon in good light, so the AI review and you judge the same thing.

*The freezer is the main ingredient.* The stocks and *glace* of `STK-2` give pan sauces and *veloutés* their body. If
the freezer is empty, make a white stock before module 2.

*Eggs.* The raw-yolk and warm egg sauces of modules 4 and 5 are made with pasteurised egg yolk, for every guest
([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment), the raw-egg row). Supermarkets sell it
chilled in cartons; about 20 g is one yolk. Eggs for poaching are ordinary fresh eggs.

Plan about five weeks. Most sessions are a weeknight: the sauces sit on meals the household eats anyway, such as a pan
sauce on a sear, a gratin, mayonnaise with boiled potatoes, and *hollandaise* at a weekend brunch.

**Have ready:** the heavy saucepan, a small saucepan and the stainless sauté pan; a balloon whisk (and a small flat
whisk if you have one); a heatproof bowl that sits on a saucepan without touching the water; a fine sieve; a stick
blender and its narrow beaker; a wide-mouthed vacuum flask; the probe; teaspoons and a plain dessert spoon for the
*nappe* photos; a phone and a phone stand; frozen stock and *glace* from `STK-2`; the scale; the tasting form; the
journal and the fault log.

### SAU-2.M1.L1 — What a sauce does

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `STK-2.M2.L1` · moves CK-CLAS→2, CK-TERM→3*

*Objectives:* [K] flavour, moisture, contrast, richness; families (reductions, starch, emulsions, purées, vinaigrettes); the five mother sauces as a map [S11]
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** there are thousands of sauces, but only a handful of ways to hold one together. Once you can say
what holds a sauce together, you know how to make it, how it will fail, and how to rescue it.

**You need:** this lesson; the journal; your memory of five sauces you have made or eaten.

**Explanation:**

*What a sauce adds to a dish:*
- **flavour,** often concentrated;
- **moisture,** for lean or dry food;
- **contrast,** such as acid against fat, or a cold sauce on warm fish;
- **richness:** a little fat that carries aroma.

*The families, by what holds them together:*

| Family | What holds it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Reductions | Water evaporated until the gelatin and dissolved solids give body | A *jus*, a pan sauce |
| Starch-thickened | Gelatinised starch from a roux, a slurry or *beurre manié* (`SCI-2.M3.L1`) | *Béchamel*, gravy |
| Emulsions | Fat suspended in water, or water in fat, held by an emulsifier (`SCI-2.M4.L2`) | Mayonnaise, *hollandaise* |
| Purées | Vegetable fibre and starch | A pepper or tomato sauce |
| Vinaigrettes | A temporary emulsion of oil and acid | A salad dressing |

A creamy look doesn't make a sauce an emulsion: a *béchamel* is creamy because of starch.

*The five mother sauces* (*sauces mères*) are a map of the classical system, not five recipes to learn:

| Mother sauce | Liquid | Thickener | Derivatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Béchamel* | Milk | White roux | *Mornay*, *soubise*, cream sauce |
| *Velouté* | White stock | Blond roux | *Suprême*, *allemande*, Bercy |
| *Espagnole* | Brown stock | Brown roux and tomato | *Demi-glace*, *bordelaise*, Robert |
| Tomato | Tomato and stock | Reduction (classically a roux) | Provençale, Creole, Portuguese |
| *Hollandaise* | Butter | Egg yolk emulsion | *Béarnaise*, *mousseline*, *maltaise* |

The table is the point of the lesson: a derivative is its mother sauce plus a flavouring or a finish. *Espagnole* and
*demi-glace* are named here and cooked in `CLS-4`.

*The terms.* Into the glossary, in French, Dutch and English, and used from now on: *nappe*, *monter au beurre*, *roux*,
*liaison*, *beurre manié*, *réduction*, *jus*, *sabayon*, *émulsion*.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): build the family table in your own words, with one sauce from your own cooking in
each row. Then take five sauces you have made and name the family of each, and what holds it together.

**Look for:** for any sauce, you can say what holds it together and which family it belongs to.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Lost in hundreds of names | Treating the mother sauces as recipes to learn rather than as a map | Learn the five rows; derive the rest |
| A gravy called a *jus* | Confusing a reduction with a starch-thickened sauce (a *jus* with flour in it) | Ask what thickens it |
| *Béchamel* filed as an emulsion | Calling every creamy sauce an emulsion | Ask what holds it: starch or an emulsifier |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the family table and the five sauces placed in it.

### SAU-2.M2.L1 — Deglazing and pan sauces

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m2-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M3.L3`, `STK-2.M2.L5` · moves TS-SAU→2*

*Objectives:* [S] deglaze the fond, add stock, reduce, adjust; [J] judge consistency by eye and spoon
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the brown crust a sear leaves in the pan is the most concentrated flavour in the kitchen. Five
minutes and a little stock turn it into a sauce, and this is the sauce you will make most often.

**You need:** for 2 portions: 2 chicken thighs or pork chops; a shallot; 150 ml of stock from the freezer (a gelled
one); 100 ml of dry white wine or dry cider for session 2; butter; salt and pepper; the sauté pan; a wooden spatula; a
spoon for the *nappe* photo.

**Explanation:**

*The fond:* the browned juices stuck to the pan after a sear (`HEA-1.M3.L3`). Brown is flavour; black is bitter, and a
black fond is wiped out, not deglazed.

*The pan-sauce sequence:*
1. **Pour off** most of the fat, leaving a film.
2. **Sweat** a finely chopped shallot over medium heat, for a minute.
3. **Deglaze** with wine, vinegar, cider or water, scraping the fond loose.
4. **Reduce** almost dry, until the sharp smell of alcohol has gone.
5. **Add the stock** (`STK-2.M2.L5`).
6. **Reduce** to the consistency you want.
7. **Add the resting juices** of the meat.
8. **Season,** and finish with butter, cream or herbs.

*Judging the consistency.* As the sauce thickens, the bubbles change from small and fast to larger and slower. Dip the
spoon: the sauce coats it, and the line stays clean. Salt goes in only at the end, because reduction concentrates it.

*Gelatin does the work.* A stock that gelled in the fridge gives a pan sauce body without flour. A thin, unset stock
never reaches *nappe* by reduction alone: it becomes too salty or too strong first. If your stock is thin, add a cube of
*glace*.

*Proportions:* about 100–150 ml of stock for two portions, reduced by roughly half.

**Practice:**
1. **A weeknight pan sauce (30 min).** Sear the chicken or pork, rest it on a warm plate, and make the sauce with water
   to deglaze and stock to finish. Reduce to *nappe* and photograph the spoon.
2. **With wine or cider (30 min).** The same sauce, deglazed with wine or cider. Compare the body, colour and acidity
   with the first.

**Look for:** large, slow bubbles as the sauce reaches *nappe*; a clean line on the spoon; a glossy surface.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A bitter sauce | Deglazing a burnt fond | Wipe out a black fond; deglaze only brown |
| Too salty once reduced | Adding salt before reducing | Salt at the end |
| Too strong, still thin | Reducing a thin stock until it is too strong to eat | A gelled stock, or a cube of *glace* |
| A raw, boozy taste | Leaving the alcohol raw by adding stock too soon | Reduce the wine almost dry first |

**Safety:** the pan is hot and the fat spits when the cold liquid goes in: pour away from you, and keep the handle
turned in. The meat is cooked to its §9 figure before it rests (poultry 75 °C, probed).

**Record:** the two spoon photos and a line comparing the sauces.

### SAU-2.M2.L2 — Reductions and *monter au beurre*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m2-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SAU-2.M2.L1`, `SCI-2.M4.L2` · moves TS-SAU→3, SC-EMUL→3*

*Objectives:* [S] reduce to *nappe*; mount with cold butter; [K] why it emulsifies and why it breaks
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a few cubes of cold butter swirled into a reduction turn it glossy, round and restaurant-smooth. It
is also the easiest emulsion to break, so it is the best place to learn how emulsions fail and how to save them.

**You need:** 500 ml of stock; 80 g of cold butter in 1 cm cubes, kept in the fridge until used; a small saucepan; a
whisk; a spoon of cold water in a cup; two spoons and two warm plates for comparing; the phone.

**Explanation:**

*Reducing to nappe.* A stock reduced by about two thirds coats a spoon. Reduced further, it becomes a *glace* that sets
firm when cold (`STK-2.M2.L5`).

*Mounting with butter (monter au beurre):*
1. Take the reduction off the boil.
2. Swirl the pan, or whisk, as you add cold butter a few cubes at a time.

The butter melts into tiny droplets of fat that the gelatin of the reduction and the milk solids of the butter hold in
suspension. This is a warm butter emulsion (`SCI-2.M4.L2`): the sauce turns glossy and slightly paler.

*Why it breaks.* Brought back to a simmer or above, the fat droplets merge and the milk proteins tighten: the emulsion
separates into a greasy layer on a thin liquid. Too much butter for the liquid does the same. Melted butter doesn't
form the emulsion at all; cold butter melts slowly into it.

*The repair:*
1. Take the pan off the heat.
2. Add a spoon of cold water or stock.
3. Whisk hard; or whisk the broken sauce slowly into a fresh spoon of reduction.

*Serve soon.* A mounted sauce is made at the last moment and held warm, never boiled again.

**Practice:**
1. **Mount and hold (40 min).** Reduce the stock to *nappe*. Pour half into a second pan, and mount it with about 40 g
   of cold butter. Compare the two halves on a spoon and on a warm plate: gloss, body, taste.
2. **Break and repair (40 min).** Mount a fresh reduction, then bring it to a boil until it breaks. Photograph it,
   repair it, and photograph the result.

**Look for:** a glossier, slightly paler sauce as the butter goes in; the broken sauce with oil beads that runs off the
spoon; the repaired sauce coating the spoon again.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A greasy sauce from the start | Melted butter in place of cold | Cold cubes, a few at a time |
| Splits as you mount | Mounting over full heat | Off the boil, swirling |
| Oil on a thin sauce | Too much butter for too little reduction | Less butter; reduce more first |
| Breaks at the table | Reheating a mounted sauce to a boil | Hold it warm; never boil again |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the four photos (two halves, broken, repaired), and the cause of the break in a sentence.

## SAU-2.M3 — Starch-thickened sauces

### SAU-2.M3.L1 — Roux

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m3-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-SAU→2, RD-RATO→2*

*Objectives:* [S] white, blond and brown roux; [K] ratios of roux to liquid for thin, medium and thick
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a roux is the most reliable way to thicken a sauce, and it scales from a cup of gravy to a pan of
croquette filling. With three colours and three ratios, you can thicken anything to the exact consistency you want.

**You need:** 150 g of butter and 150 g of flour; 750 ml of stock; three small pans; a flat-ended wooden spatula; a
whisk; the scale; three spoons; the phone.

**Explanation:**

*A roux* is equal weights of fat (usually butter) and flour, cooked together. The fat coats the starch granules so that
they disperse in the liquid without clumping; they swell and thicken as the sauce comes to the boil (`SCI-2.M3.L1`).

*Three colours,* by cooking time over moderate heat, stirring:

| Roux | Cooking | Use | Thickening power |
|---|---|---|---|
| White | 2–3 min, no colour; smells of biscuit rather than raw flour | *Béchamel* | Highest |
| Blond | 5–8 min, pale straw | *Velouté* | Slightly lower |
| Brown | 15–30 min, hazelnut to brown | Brown sauces, gumbo | Clearly lower: browning breaks down some starch |

*Ratios per litre of liquid* (white roux, equal weights of butter and flour):

| Thickness | Roux per litre | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Thin | About 60 g (30 g butter, 30 g flour) | Soups, a pouring sauce |
| Medium | About 100 g (50 g, 50 g) | A coating sauce at *nappe* |
| Thick | About 160 g (80 g, 80 g) | A binding sauce: croquettes, a soufflé base |

A brown roux needs more for the same thickness. Copy both tables onto a ratio card for the kitchen wall.

*Avoiding lumps.* Add the liquid to the roux gradually, whisking. One of the two should be hot and the other cool or
warm: a hot roux with cool liquid, or a cooled roux with hot liquid. Both hot causes lumps.

*Judge at the boil.* The sauce reaches its full thickness only at the boil, so don't adjust it before. Then simmer it
for several minutes, stirring, to cook out the taste of raw flour.

**Practice:**
1. **Three roux (40 min).** Make a white, a blond and a brown roux, each from 25 g of butter and 25 g of flour, and
   photograph them side by side. Thicken 250 ml of stock with each (the medium ratio), bring to the boil, simmer for
   5 minutes, and compare them on a spoon.

**Look for:** the smell turning from raw flour to biscuit, then hazelnut; the sauce thickening sharply at the boil; the
brown-roux sauce visibly thinner.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Black specks, a bitter smell | A burnt roux | Throw it away; start again |
| Lumps | Adding all the liquid at once | Gradually, whisking; strain if needed |
| Too thick once it boils | Judging the thickness before the boil | Bring to the boil, then adjust |
| A floury taste | Not cooking the sauce long enough | Simmer several minutes, stirring |

**Safety:** a roux is very hot and sticks to skin: stir with a long spatula, and add the liquid slowly, away from you,
because it steams hard.

**Record:** the photo of the three roux and a line on the three sauces; the ratio card.

### SAU-2.M3.L2 — *Béchamel* and derivatives

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SAU-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-SAU→3, CK-CLAS→2*

*Objectives:* [S] a smooth *béchamel*; *Mornay* and others; [J] adjust thickness
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** *béchamel* is behind the Dutch kitchen's gratins, lasagne, croquettes and cauliflower with cheese
sauce. Once it is smooth every time, a whole family of sauces is a single step away.

**You need:** for session 1: 500 ml of milk; half an onion; a bay leaf; a clove; 25 g each of butter and flour; salt,
white pepper, nutmeg; a heavy saucepan; a whisk; a fine sieve. For session 2, the *Mornay* inputs of `SAU-2.M3.X1`.

**Explanation:**

*A béchamel:*
1. **Infuse** the milk: warm it with the onion, the bay leaf and the clove, and leave it for 15 minutes; strain.
2. **Make a white roux** at the ratio for its use (the medium ratio coats at *nappe*).
3. **Whisk in** the warm milk gradually, stirring into the corners.
4. **Simmer** for several minutes, stirring.
5. **Season** with salt, white pepper and a grating of nutmeg.

*The derivatives,* each one step from the *béchamel*:
- ***Mornay*:** grated cheese, off the heat, for gratins;
- ***soubise*:** sweated, puréed onion;
- **cream sauce:** finished with cream;
- **parsley sauce:** chopped parsley.

*Adjusting the thickness.* Too thick: whisk in hot milk. Too thin: simmer it down, or whisk in a little *beurre manié*
(`SAU-2.M3.L3`).

*Keeping it smooth:*
- stir to the corners of the pan, where it catches and burns;
- strain it through a fine sieve if lumps form;
- press buttered paper or film on the surface against a skin.

*Cheese* goes in off the boil: boiling makes it stringy and greasy.

*A gratin* needs the vegetables cooked and drained well first. Their water dilutes the sauce in the oven.

**Practice:**
1. **A béchamel (40 min).** Make a *béchamel* at the medium ratio, infused, simmered, adjusted to *nappe*, and
   photographed on a spoon.
2. **A weeknight gratin (45 min).** A *Mornay* for a gratin of a seasonal vegetable: cauliflower, leek, chicory or
   celeriac in winter, courgette in summer. This can be the gratin of `SAU-2.M3.X1`.

**Look for:** a smooth, glossy sauce that coats the spoon; no taste of raw flour; the cheese melted in without strings.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Lumps | Adding the milk too fast | Gradually, whisking; strain |
| Burnt flecks, a scorched taste | A sauce that catches on the bottom | Stir into the corners; a heavy pan; moderate heat |
| A skin on the surface | Left uncovered | Paper or film pressed on the surface |
| A watery gratin | The vegetables were not drained | Drain well, or dry them in the pan |

**Safety:** a thick *béchamel* spits as it bubbles: a long whisk, and a lid ready. A gratin dish comes out of the oven
with oven gloves.

**Record:** the spoon photo and the gratin.

### SAU-2.M3.L3 — *Velouté*, slurries and *beurre manié*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m3-l3 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SAU-2.M3.L1`, `STK-2.M2.L2` · moves TS-SAU→3*

*Objectives:* [S] a stock-based *velouté*; quick thickening with a slurry or *beurre manié*
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m3-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a *velouté* is a *béchamel* made with stock, and it is only as good as the stock. The quick
thickeners are the everyday tools that save a sauce that is already made but too thin.

**You need:** for session 1, the inputs of `SAU-2.M3.X2`, or 500 ml of white stock, 25 g each of butter and flour, and
50 ml of cream. For session 2: 750 ml of thin stock in three pans; cornflour or potato starch; soft butter and flour;
an egg yolk and 50 ml of cream; three spoons.

**Explanation:**

*A velouté:* white stock thickened with a blond roux, simmered gently and skimmed until silky. Its quality is the
quality of the stock (`STK-2.M2.L2`).

*A suprême:* a chicken *velouté* finished with cream: the natural sauce for poached chicken.

*Quick thickeners* for a sauce that is already made:

| Thickener | How | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Slurry | Cornflour or potato starch mixed with twice its volume of cold liquid, whisked into a simmering sauce | Thickens at the boil; glossy and clear (cornflour slightly cloudy); thins again if boiled long or stirred hard |
| *Beurre manié* | Equal weights of soft butter and flour kneaded together, whisked in small pieces into the simmering sauce | Body and gloss; needs a few minutes of simmering to cook the flour |
| *Liaison* | Egg yolk and cream, tempered with hot sauce and stirred in off the boil | Richness and a light body; curdles if boiled |

*How much.* Start small, about a teaspoon of starch for 250 ml, because a slurry or *beurre manié* only thickens fully
at the boil. Add a second teaspoon if it is still thin.

*The liaison* is tempered as in `STK-2.M4.L2`: a ladle of hot sauce whisked into the yolk and cream, then the mixture
stirred into the sauce off the heat, and the sauce warmed gently, without boiling.

**Practice:**
1. **A chicken velouté (60 min).** From frozen white stock, at the medium ratio, skimmed, and finished with cream as a
   *suprême*. This can be the *velouté* of `SAU-2.M3.X2`.
2. **Three rescues (30 min).** Bring the three pans of thin stock to a simmer. Thicken one with a slurry, one with
   *beurre manié* and one with a *liaison*. Compare the gloss, the body and the taste on three spoons.

**Look for:** a *velouté* that tastes of the stock, not of flour; the slurry sauce turning clear and glossy at the boil;
the *liaison* thickening without curdling.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Lumps of starch | Starch added dry, so it forms lumps | Mix it with cold liquid first |
| Grainy flecks of egg | A *liaison* boiled after it goes in | Gentle heat; never boil |
| A sauce that thins again | A slurry sauce boiled hard for a long time | Bring to the boil, then serve |
| A flat, floury *velouté* | A *velouté* from a weak stock | Start from a gelled stock |

**Safety:** a sauce with a *liaison* is eaten hot and not kept warm for hours; leftovers are cooled and kept as

### SAU-2.M4.L1 — Mayonnaise

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m4-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M4.L2` · moves TS-SAU→3, SC-EMUL→3*

*Objectives:* [S] by hand and by blender; [K] oil ratio, temperature, rate; [S] rescue a broken mayonnaise
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a home-made mayonnaise is a different thing from a jar: thick, glossy and tasting of the oil you
chose. Made by hand, it is the clearest lesson in emulsions, because you can watch the moment it forms.

**You need:** per batch, 1 pasteurised yolk (about 20 g), 1 teaspoon of mustard, 1 tablespoon of vinegar or lemon, about
175 ml of neutral oil at room temperature, and salt; a bowl on a damp cloth; a balloon whisk; a jug with a spout; for
session 2, 50 g of pasteurised whole egg (or 2 yolks) for the blender and 1 yolk for the rescue, and the stick blender
with its beaker.

**Explanation:**

*Mayonnaise is oil in water:* tiny droplets of oil suspended in the water of the yolk and the acid, held apart by the
yolk's lecithin and proteins (`SCI-2.M4.L2`).

*The three variables:*
- **ratio:** one yolk holds about 150–200 ml of oil comfortably in a home batch. More is possible, but the sauce becomes
  stiff and needs a little water.
- **temperature:** yolk and oil at room temperature. Cold oil and cold yolk emulsify poorly.
- **rate:** the oil goes in drop by drop until the emulsion forms, then in a thin stream. Too much oil too early floods
  the emulsifier.

*By hand.* Whisk the yolk, mustard, a pinch of salt and half the acid,
then add the oil from the jug a few drops at a time, whisking constantly. After about a quarter of the oil, the sauce
turns from translucent to opaque and thick: the emulsion has formed.

*By blender.* The egg (or yolks), mustard, acid and salt in the narrow beaker, all the oil poured on top. Start the
blender on the bottom and draw it up slowly. It is faster and more forgiving, and
gives a lighter, paler sauce.

*Rescuing a broken mayonnaise.* Start again in a clean bowl with a fresh yolk, or with a teaspoon of mustard or water,
and whisk the broken sauce in slowly, as if it were the oil.

*Seasoning:* salt, the rest of the acid, mustard. A thick mayonnaise is thinned with a few drops of water.

**Practice:**
1. **By hand (40 min).** One yolk, about 175 ml of oil. Photograph the stages: the first drops, the thickening, the
   finished sauce.
2. **By blender, and a rescue (40 min).** A blender mayonnaise. Then a hand mayonnaise broken on purpose by pouring the
   oil too fast at the start, rescued, and photographed at each step.

**Look for:** the sauce turning opaque and thick in the first minutes; a ribbon that holds on the whisk; a broken sauce
turning thin and oily, with a curdled look.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| It never thickens | Cold ingredients straight from the fridge | Room temperature; rescue as below |
| Breaks at the start | Pouring the oil too fast at the start | Drop by drop until it forms |
| A bitter mayonnaise | Strong extra-virgin olive oil as the only oil, which turns bitter when whisked hard | A neutral oil; olive oil only as a part |
| A wasted batch | Giving up on a broken sauce instead of rescuing it | A fresh yolk; whisk the broken sauce in |

**Safety:** mayonnaise is made with pasteurised yolk, for every guest, and kept covered in the fridge at 4 °C for at
most 24 hours (assessment §9, the raw-egg row). It is out of the fridge for no longer than 2 hours in total.

**Record:** the stage photos, and the cause of the break in a sentence.

### SAU-2.M4.L2 — Derivatives and safety

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m4-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAU-2.M4.L1`, `SAF-1.M1.L1` · moves TS-SAU→3*

*Objectives:* [S] *aïoli*, *rémoulade*, tartare; [K] raw-yolk safety and storage
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** one bowl of mayonnaise becomes three sauces in ten minutes. Because it contains raw yolk, it is
also the sauce with the strictest rules in the course, and they are knowledge-check safety items.

**You need:** one batch of mayonnaise (`SAU-2.M4.L1`); for the *aïoli*, 2 cloves of garlic, coarse salt and olive oil;
for the *rémoulade*, a teaspoon of mustard, 2 gherkins, a tablespoon of capers, parsley, chives or tarragon; for the
herb mayonnaise, a handful of soft herbs; a mortar or a knife; three small bowls; a jar with a lid; a label; boiled
potatoes and seasonal vegetables.

**Explanation:**

*The derivatives,* from a framework:
- ***aïoli*:** garlic pounded to a paste with a pinch of coarse salt, the mayonnaise made on it with part olive oil;
- ***rémoulade*:** mustard, chopped gherkins, capers and herbs;
- **tartare sauce:** gherkins, capers, shallot and herbs, sometimes chopped boiled egg;
- **herb mayonnaise:** chopped soft herbs, or a green purée of blanched herbs.

Wet additions thin the sauce: pat herbs dry, and drain and squeeze the gherkins and capers.

*Raw yolk and safety* (assessment §9):
- **Raw eggs can carry *Salmonella*.** A raw-yolk sauce is made with pasteurised egg yolk, for every guest, not only for
  people in risk groups (pregnant people, young children, older people and anyone with weakened immunity). Those are
  the people for whom it matters most.
- **The eggs** in the kitchen are fresh, clean and uncracked, from the fridge, and their shells are kept away from the

- **Storage:** home-made mayonnaise goes into the fridge at 4 °C, covered, and is used within 24 hours, also when it is
  made with pasteurised yolk. It is out of the fridge for no longer than 2 hours in total: a bowl goes on the table for
  the meal and back into the fridge after it.
- **Garlic in oil** needs refrigeration (§9). An *aïoli* is made for the meal; garlic oil is never kept at room
  temperature.
- **Labelling:** the sauce and the date on the jar (`SAF-1`).

**Practice:**
1. **Three derivatives (45 min).** From one batch of mayonnaise, make an *aïoli*, a *rémoulade* and a herb mayonnaise.

   new potatoes). Label and chill what is left before the meal ends.

**Look for:** each derivative recognisable by its flavour; the jar labelled and in the fridge before the meal ends.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A thin, runny derivative | Watery additions, such as wet herbs or undrained gherkins, that thin the sauce | Dry and drain; a thick base |
| A guest put at risk | A raw-yolk sauce made with unpasteurised yolk | Pasteurised yolk, for every guest |
| A bowl warm by dessert | A bowl of mayonnaise left on the table all evening | Back into the fridge after the meal |
| A botulism risk | Garlic oil stored at room temperature | Fridge; make *aïoli* for the meal |

**Safety:** this lesson is the safety rule: pasteurised yolk for every guest; in the fridge at 4 °C, covered, for at
most 24 hours; at most 2 hours out of it; garlic in oil in the fridge (assessment §9).

**Record:** a tasting line per derivative, and a photo of the labelled jar in the fridge.

### SAU-2.M5.L1 — *Sabayon* and *hollandaise*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m5-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SAU-2.M4.L1`, `EGG-1.M4.L1` · moves TS-SAU→3, TS-EGG→4*

*Objectives:* [S] cook yolks over a bain-marie to the ribbon stage; add butter; [J] save it from overheating
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** *hollandaise* is the sauce cooks fear most, because it can scramble, split or set within a minute.
Its secret is a *sabayon*: yolks cooked gently to a stable foam.

**You need:** 5 pasteurised yolks (2 per session, 1 for the rescue); white-wine vinegar, a shallot and peppercorns for
the reduction; 150 g of butter; lemon, salt, cayenne; the heatproof bowl and a saucepan; a balloon whisk; the probe; the
vacuum flask; a small pan; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*A sabayon:* egg yolks whisked with a little liquid (water, a reduction or wine) over a bain-marie until they thicken to
a pale foam.
- **The ribbon stage:** a trail from the whisk holds on the surface for a few seconds.
- **The heat:** with water and acid in the mix, the yolks thicken at a higher temperature than a plain yolk
  (`EGG-1.M2.L1`), and they scramble if the bowl gets too hot. Probe it at the ribbon and note the reading.
- **The bowl** sits over barely simmering water without touching it, and comes off the heat whenever the edges start to
  set.

*Hollandaise:*
1. **The reduction:** the vinegar, water, chopped shallot and crushed peppercorns, reduced to about a tablespoon and
   strained. Or simply water and lemon.
2. **The sabayon:** the yolks and the reduction whisked to the ribbon.
3. **The butter:** warm melted or clarified butter whisked in slowly, roughly 50–75 g per yolk, off and on the heat.
4. **Seasoning:** salt, lemon, a pinch of cayenne.

It is a warm butter emulsion, thickened by the cooked yolk.

*Saving it from overheating.* Lift the bowl off the heat and whisk in a spoon of cold water. If it has split, whisk it
slowly into a fresh yolk with a spoon of water over gentle heat.

*Holding it.* *Hollandaise* is made for the meal, and held either in a bowl over warm water off the heat or in a
pre-warmed vacuum flask, at 57 °C or above (assessment §9), below the point where the yolk scrambles. It is not cooled
and reheated, and what is left after the meal is thrown away.

*Adapting.* The method scales from 1 to 6 yolks; a larger batch takes longer, so time it against the rest of the plate.

**Practice:**
1. ***Sabayon* to ribbon (40 min).** Two yolks with the reduction. Photograph the ribbon; probe it at the ribbon and
   note the reading.
2. ***Hollandaise* and rescue (50 min).** A two-yolk *hollandaise*. Overheat a spoonful in the small pan until it
   splits, and rescue it. Hold the rest in the flask for 30 minutes, probing every 10 minutes.

**Look for:** the volume about tripled; a ribbon that holds; a *hollandaise* that coats the spoon and shines; no grains
of cooked yolk.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Scrambled at the edges | Water boiling under the bowl | Barely simmering; lift off often |
| Thin and oily | Butter added too fast or too hot | Slowly; warm, not sizzling |
| A grainy sauce | Graininess from scrambled yolk | Strain; next time, lower heat |
| Split by serving time | A sauce held on the stove until it splits | Off the heat, in the flask |
| A safety risk | A sauce kept for the next day | Made for the meal; leftovers thrown away |

**Safety:** the yolks in a *sabayon* or *hollandaise* are only lightly cooked, so they are pasteurised, for every guest
(assessment §9, the raw-egg row). Held at 57 °C or above for the meal, then thrown away (`SAU-2.M5.Q4`).

**Record:** the ribbon photo and its reading; the holding log.

### SAU-2.M5.L2 — *Beurre blanc*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m5-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SAU-2.M2.L2` · moves TS-SAU→3*

*Objectives:* [S] reduce, mount with butter, hold warm; [K] why temperature control matters
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** *beurre blanc* is little more than a sharp reduction and a lot of butter, and it is the classic
sauce for fish and leeks. It has no egg; temperature alone holds it together, so it teaches heat control more purely
than any other sauce.

**You need:** for session 1: 2 shallots; 100 ml of dry white wine; 2 tablespoons of white-wine vinegar; 150 g of cold
butter in cubes; salt; a small heavy saucepan; a whisk; a sieve; the vacuum flask. For session 2: red wine or dry cider
in place of the white wine, and a weeknight fish or vegetable dish.

**Explanation:**

*Beurre blanc:* finely chopped shallot with white wine and white-wine vinegar, reduced almost dry, to about a
tablespoon. Then cold butter is whisked in piece by piece, over low heat, until the sauce is pale, creamy and coats a
spoon. It has no yolk: the milk solids of the butter and the reduction hold the emulsion (`SAU-2.M2.L2`). Strain out
the shallot, or leave it in.

*Why temperature control matters:*
- too hot, and the butterfat melts out of the emulsion: the sauce turns oily;
- too cool, and the butter stops melting into it: the sauce thickens and sets.

The working range is warm to the touch, never near a simmer. Move the pan on and off the heat as you whisk.

*A spoon of cream* in the reduction (*beurre nantais*) makes it more forgiving.

*The reduction must be reduced.* Not reduced enough, the sauce is thin and sour, and there is too much water for the
butter.

*Holding.* In a warm bain-marie off the heat, or a pre-warmed vacuum flask, for the length of the meal, and out of the
fridge for no longer than 2 hours (assessment §9). Never next to the burner.

*Variations:*
- ***beurre rouge*:** with red wine;
- **with herbs**, chopped in at the end;
- **with cider:** a reduction of dry cider and cider vinegar, good with pork and with fish.

**Practice:**
1. ***Beurre blanc* (40 min).** The shallot reduction, then the 150 g of cold butter. Strain, or leave the shallot in.
   Hold it in the flask for 20 minutes, and check it.
2. **A variation (40 min).** A *beurre rouge* or a cider version, for a weeknight fish or vegetable dish (leeks in
   winter, asparagus in spring).

**Look for:** a pale, creamy sauce that coats the spoon; the butter melting into a sauce rather than into oil; no
separation in the flask after 20 minutes.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A thin, sour sauce | A reduction that is not reduced enough | Reduce to about a tablespoon |
| Oil from the start | Butter added all at once | Piece by piece, whisking |
| An oily, separated sauce | Too much heat | Off the heat; a cold cube to cool it |
| Split while waiting | Holding it next to the burner | Bain-marie off the heat, or the flask |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`); the 2-hour limit out of the fridge applies to the
sauce (assessment §9).

**Record:** a spoon photo and a line on the flask check.

## SAU-2.M6 — Vinaigrettes and dressings

**Knowledge check:** the 8 items of `SAU-2.M6` in the [course file](../../05-courses/S2/SAU-2.md). Pass mark 80 %.

### SAU-2.M6.L1 — Vinaigrettes

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m6-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M4.L2`, `TST-1.M3.L1` · moves TS-SAU→3, RD-RATO→2*

*Objectives:* [S] the 3:1 ratio and its variants; emulsified versus broken; [J] adjust acid and sweetness to the leaves
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m6-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a vinaigrette is the sauce you make most often and think about least. A ratio, the right order and
tasting on a leaf turn a salad from an afterthought into part of the meal.

**You need:** a neutral oil and a good olive oil; wine vinegar and cider vinegar; a lemon; mustard; honey or apple
syrup (*appelstroop*); salt and pepper; small jars with lids; a timer; two leaves to taste on: a delicate one
(butterhead or lamb's lettuce) and a bitter one (chicory or endive).

**Explanation:**

*The ratio:* about 3 parts oil to 1 part acid by volume, then adjusted by taste.
- **Sharper, 2 : 1:** for bitter or robust leaves such as chicory and endive, or a potato salad that absorbs it.
- **Softer, 4 : 1:** for delicate leaves such as butterhead lettuce or lamb's lettuce.

*The order:*
1. Dissolve the salt in the acid first: salt does not dissolve in oil.
2. Add the mustard.
3. Add the oil.

*Emulsified or broken:*
- **emulsified:** with mustard, shaken or whisked hard, or blended; it clings to leaves and holds for a while;
- **broken:** oil and acid only, or drizzled separately; lighter, for delicate leaves and grilled vegetables.

*Adjusting:*
- **acid and sweetness to the leaves:** bitter leaves take a touch of honey or apple syrup; sweet leaves take more acid;
- **dress late,** just before serving, because salt and acid wilt leaves;
- **taste on a leaf,** not from a spoon (`TST-1.M3.L1`): on a spoon, any vinaigrette tastes too sharp.

*Acids and oils.* Acids: wine vinegar, cider vinegar, lemon. Oils: rapeseed and sunflower (neutral), olive, walnut.
Dutch cold-pressed rapeseed oil has a nutty, green taste of its own.

*A ratio card.* Add the three ratios to the card from `SAU-2.M3.L1`.

**Practice:**
1. **Three ratios (30 min).** Make 2 : 1, 3 : 1 and 4 : 1 with the same oil and vinegar, salted the same. Taste each on
   the same leaf; note which ratio suits which leaf.
2. **Broken and emulsified (30 min).** The same 3 : 1 dressing, shaken with and without mustard. Time how long each
   takes to separate in the jar. Then taste each on the delicate and the bitter leaf.

**Look for:** an emulsified dressing that coats a leaf; a balance where neither the oil nor the acid stands out on the
leaf; leaves still crisp at the table.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Gritty grains of salt | Salt in the oil | Salt into the acid first |
| Delicate leaves wilted and sour | Too much acid for delicate leaves | 4 : 1, or a broken dressing |
| A limp, soggy salad | A salad dressed an hour before the meal | Dress just before serving |
| A dressing that tastes harsh but suits the leaves | Tasting from the spoon | Taste on a leaf |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the three ratios with the leaf each suited, and the separation times.

## SAU-2.M7 — Diagnosing and repairing sauces

**Knowledge check:** the 8 items of `SAU-2.M7` in the [course file](../../05-courses/S2/SAU-2.md). Pass mark 80 %.

### SAU-2.M7.L1 — Sauce clinic

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-sau-2-m7-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SAU-2.M5.L2` · moves JD-TRBL→3, FL-CORR→3*

*Objectives:* [J] broken, lumpy, too thin, too thick, greasy, flat, too salty: cause and fix for each family
<!-- END generated:lesson-sau-2-m7-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** in a real kitchen, sauces go wrong at the last minute, and nobody tells you why. The clinic trains
the habit that saves them: look, taste and name the cause before reaching for anything.

**You need:** a helper; the fault cards of `SAU-2.M7.X1`; the ingredients for three sauces from your own cooking per
session (stock, milk, butter and flour, eggs and oil, vinegar and a shallot); the clinic table; a spoon; the phone; the
fault log; the household's tasting forms.

**Explanation:**

*Diagnosis first.* The same symptom has different causes in different families. A thin sauce can be under-reduced,
under-thickened, a broken emulsion, or a slurry boiled too long. Name the cause in the log before you add anything.

*The clinic table,* cause and fix per family:

| Fault | Reduction or pan sauce | Starch-thickened | Emulsion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broken or greasy | Over-boiled after mounting: cold liquid, whisk | Fat floating from too much roux fat: skim, whisk | Rescue with fresh yolk or water |
| Lumpy | — | Strain, or blend and simmer | Grainy yolk: strain, rescue |
| Too thin | Reduce, or mount with butter | Simmer down, *beurre manié*, slurry | More oil (cold) or a further ribbon (warm) |
| Too thick | Add stock | Add hot liquid | Add water a few drops at a time |
| Flat | Salt, acid, a reduction of wine | Salt, nutmeg, cheese | Salt, acid, mustard |
| Too salty | Dilute with unsalted stock, add cream or butter | Add more unsalted sauce base | Make more unsalted sauce and combine |

*Flavour faults* are fixed with the tools of `FL-CORR` (`TST-1`, `STK-2.M5.L1`): acid lifts a flat sauce; fat or
sweetness softens a harsh one; salt is corrected by dilution, never masked with sugar.

*One change at a time,* tasted after each.

*The fault cards* are in `SAU-2.M7.X1`: the helper draws one per sauce, cards 1–3 for session 1 and 4–6 for
session 2.

*Write it up:* the fault, the cause, the fix and why it worked: the entries of your fault log.

**Practice:**
1. **Structural faults (60 min).** Three sauces from your own cooking, each given one structural fault by the helper,
   unseen. Diagnose, name the cause, repair, and photograph before and after.
2. **Flavour faults (60 min).** Three sauces again, each with one flavour fault. Taste, correct, write up the reason;
   the household tastes before and after.

**Look for:** the cause named before anything is added; one change at a time, tasted after each; a repaired sauce at
*nappe* and balanced.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A broken emulsion turned to paste | Adding flour to a broken emulsion | Rescue an emulsion with yolk or water |
| A salty sauce that is now sweet | Masking salt with sugar | Dilute with unsalted base |
| A sauce nobody can read | Adding several corrections at once | One change, then taste |
| A waste | Throwing away a sauce that could be saved | Check the clinic table first |

**Safety:** the usual rules hold: pasteurised yolk in the egg sauces, and a split *hollandaise* rescued for the meal or
thrown away, never kept (assessment §9).

**Record:** each fault, cause, fix and result in the fault log, with the photos.

### SAU-2.M7.X1 — The sauce clinic

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-sau-2-m7-x1 -->
*brief, brief* — Repair three faulty sauces prepared by a household member, without instructions.
<!-- END generated:exercise-sau-2-m7-x1 -->

**Task:** diagnose and repair faults in sauces, with no warning and no instructions.
- **The goal:** six sauces back to *nappe* and balance, over two sessions on different days.
- **The setup:** for each session, make three sauces from different families for a meal. While you are out of the
  kitchen, a household member applies one fault card from `SAU-2.M7.L1` to each. You are told only that each sauce has
  one fault.
- **The constraints:** no notes beyond the clinic table.
- **Session 1, structure:** diagnose each sauce by look and spoon; name the cause in the log; repair it; photograph
  before and after.
- **Session 2, flavour:** taste each sauce; name the fault and the cause; correct it; the tasters score it before and
  after on the form.
- **The criteria:** the cause named before the fix; the sauce at *nappe* and balanced; all six written up (fault, cause,
  fix, why it worked).

*The fault cards,* for the helper, who draws one per sauce and applies it unseen:

| Card | Fault | What the helper does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Too thick | Whisks an extra tablespoon of roux, or *beurre manié*, into a starch sauce and simmers it |
| 2 | Broken | Brings a mounted sauce to the boil, or warms a *hollandaise* until it splits |
| 3 | Too thin | Adds 50 ml of water to a sauce |
| 4 | Too salty | Adds a pinch (about 1 g) of salt per 100 ml |
| 5 | Too sour | Adds a teaspoon of vinegar per 100 ml |
| 6 | Flat | Makes the sauce without salt, or dilutes it |

Session 1 uses cards 1–3, session 2 cards 4–6.

**You need:** the components of six sauces across two meals (stock; milk, butter and flour; pasteurised yolks and oil;
vinegar and a shallot), served on the household's meals; the clinic table; the fault log; the tasting forms.

**Show:**
- **`JD-TRBL` level 3:** the three structural faults from session 1, in sauces of your own cooking, recovered, with
  photos before and after and the cause named in the log.
- **`FL-CORR` level 3:** the three flavour faults from session 2, named, fixed and written up with the reason, with the
  tasters confirming each fix on the form.

The repair test is run again at `MS-S2`.

**Time:** 2 × 1 h.

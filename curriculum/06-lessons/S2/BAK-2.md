**Before you start.** In baking you can't taste and adjust as you go: a cake is right or wrong when it comes out of the
oven. Three things decide it: the formula (the proportions), the method (how they are combined) and the oven (how it
sets them). This course teaches you to read all three:
- **the fundamentals:** baker's percentages, what each ingredient does, tins, ovens and allergens;
- **quick breads and batters:** muffins, pancakes and scones;
- **shortcrust:** *pâte brisée* and *pâte sucrée*, blind-baked without shrinking;
- **cakes:** creamed and foamed, diagnosed from the crumb, and a finished layer cake;
- **custards and creams:** *crème pâtissière*, *crème anglaise*, baked custards and whipped cream;
- **biscuits:** one variable tested, and a review of your baking log.

*The cross-section is the evidence.* Every bake is cut and photographed: the crumb of a cake, the base and edge of a
tart, the rise of a muffin. You band your own result before the AI review and the tasters do, which trains your eye
(`JD-REFL`).

*Weigh everything*, including eggs and liquids, on the scale; leaveners and salt on the precision scale.

*Two safety threads* ([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)):
- **allergens:** every allergen-free bake is made first, with washed tools, and every label is read;
- **egg creams:** pastry cream and custards are cooled from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours and to 5 °C within 6 hours,
  and kept at most 2 days; egg-thickened desserts and Swiss meringue reach 71 °C in every part.

Plan about seven weeks, the longest course of S2. Most bakes fit a weekend afternoon; pastry and custards need time in
the fridge. Share the bakes with the household, neighbours or colleagues: they are your tasters.

**Have ready:** the scale and the precision scale; the oven thermometer; the probe; a hand or stand mixer; a 1 kg loaf
tin, a 23 cm loose-bottomed tart tin, one or two 20 cm round tins, a muffin tin or paper cases; a rolling pin; a bench
scraper; baking paper and baking beans; ramekins; a palette knife; a wire rack; shallow trays and film for cooling; the
baking log; the allergen checklist (`SAF-1`); the tasting forms.

### BAK-2.M1.L1 — Baker's percentages

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `WRK-1.M3.L3` · moves KM-MATH→3, RD-RATO→2*

*Objectives:* [S] express and scale a formula in baker's %; [K] why baking needs precision
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a few grams of baking powder, or 10 % more liquid, change a bake visibly, and once it is in the oven
nothing can be corrected. Baker's percentages let you see a formula's character at a glance and scale it to any tin.

**You need:** the scale (1 g) and the precision scale (0.1 g); a calculator; the pound-cake and bread formulas of
`BAK-2.M1.X1`; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Why precision.* Everything is weighed: flour, sugar and fat on the scale; leaveners and salt on the precision scale
where the quantity is small (`TST-1`). Liquids are weighed too: 1 ml of water weighs 1 g. A cup of flour varies with how
it is filled; a weight doesn't.

*Baker's percentages:*
- the total flour is 100 %;
- every other ingredient is a percentage of the flour's weight.

A bread of 500 g of flour, 325 g of water, 10 g of salt and 5 g of yeast is 100 %, 65 %, 2 % and 1 %. The percentages
show the character of the formula:
- **hydration:** the water as a percentage of the flour (65 % here);
- **sweetness:** the sugar;
- **richness:** the fat and eggs.

*Scaling:*
1. Choose the flour weight, or work it out from the total weight you need: the total divided by the sum of the
   percentages. For 1 kg of the bread dough above: 1000 ÷ 1.68 = 595 g of flour.
2. Multiply each percentage by the flour weight: 387 g of water, 12 g of salt, 6 g of yeast.

*Ratios as a memory aid* (`RD-RATO`):
- pound cake 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 (flour, butter, sugar, eggs);
- shortcrust 2 : 1 (flour to fat);
- a basic biscuit 3 : 2 : 1 (flour, fat, sugar).

A ratio card gives you a formula from memory; baker's percentages turn it into grams.

*Eggs in formulas.* A medium egg is about 50 g without its shell: the yolk about 18 g, the white about 32 g. Eggs are
weighed, not counted, because sizes vary.

*Tin sizes.* To fit a formula to a different tin, compare the areas of the base: a 23 cm round tin holds about a third
more than a 20 cm one (23² ÷ 20² ≈ 1.3).

**Practice:**
1. **Convert and scale (45 min).** Write the pound cake and the bread formula in a table with grams and percentages;
   scale the pound cake from a 20 cm to a 23 cm round tin, and the bread to a 1 kg dough.

**Look for:** a table with grams and percentages for every ingredient; the percentages adding up to the stated total; a
scaled formula that fits the tin.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dry or dense bake | Measuring flour by volume | Weigh it |
| Wrong amounts after scaling | Percentages of the total rather than of the flour | Flour is 100 % |
| A runny or stiff batter | Counting eggs instead of weighing them | Weigh the eggs |
| A soapy or flat bake | Rounding leaveners to the nearest 5 g | The precision scale |

**Safety:** none specific; the tins and the oven come in `BAK-2.M1.L3`.

**Record:** the two formula tables, with the scaled versions.

### BAK-2.M1.L2 — What each ingredient does

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M3.L2` · moves IN-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [K] flour (protein), sugar, fats, eggs, liquids, chemical leaveners, salt
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** if you know what each ingredient does, you can predict what a change will do, and read a failed bake
backwards to its cause. Without it, a recipe is a set of instructions you can only follow.

**You need:** the muffin formula of `BAK-2.M1.X1`, for the flour comparison; cake flour (*zeeuwse bloem*) and bread
flour (*broodmeel*); the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Flour* gives starch for structure, and protein that forms gluten with water and mixing (`SCI-2.M3.L2`).

| Flour (NL) | Protein | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Cake flour (*zeeuwse bloem*, patent flour) | Low, about 8–10 % | Cakes, tender pastry, biscuits |
| Plain or all-purpose (*tarwebloem*) | About 10–11 % | Most baking |
| Bread flour (*broodmeel*) | High, about 12–14 % | Bread, chewy textures |
| Wholemeal (*volkorenmeel*) | Protein plus bran | Bread, rustic bakes; absorbs more water |

*Sugar* does more than sweeten:
- it holds water, so a bake stays moist;
- it tenderises, because it competes with gluten and starch for water;
- it browns (`SC-BRWN`);
- in creaming, its crystals trap air.

*Fats:*
- **butter** gives flavour, and its water turns to steam;
- **oil** gives a moist, tender crumb;
- **solid fat in flakes** gives flaky layers.

Fat coats the flour and shortens the gluten strands: hence "shortcrust".

*Eggs* give structure (the proteins set), emulsify (the yolk), leaven (a whipped foam), and add colour and richness.

*Liquids* hydrate flour and starch, dissolve sugar and salt, and make steam.

*Chemical leaveners:*
- **baking soda** (sodium bicarbonate) needs an acid in the formula, such as buttermilk, yoghurt, honey or brown sugar,
  and reacts at once;
- **baking powder** is soda with its own acid. Most is double-acting: some gas on mixing, more in the oven.

Soda is much stronger than powder, so they are never swapped one for one. Too much leavener gives a coarse crumb, a
soapy taste and a collapse.

*Salt* gives flavour; in bread it also controls the yeast and tightens the gluten.

**Practice:**
1. **The flour comparison (50 min).** The muffin formula baked twice, once with cake flour and once with bread flour,
   in the same oven at the same time; cut and photographed in cross-section. This is part of `BAK-2.M1.X1`.

Before you bake, write each ingredient's job in the muffin formula beside its line.

**Look for:** the job of every ingredient written before baking; a visible difference in the crumb, explained.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A tough, chewy cake | Bread flour in a tender cake | Cake or plain flour |
| A soapy, collapsed bake or a flat one | Swapping soda and powder one for one | Use the leavener the formula names |
| A dry, pale cake | Cutting the sugar in half "for health" | Change sugar in small steps, and expect drier |

**Safety:** none specific.

**Record:** the formula annotated with each ingredient's job; the cross-section photos and three sentences on the flour.

### BAK-2.M1.L3 — Tins, ovens and allergens

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAF-1.M4.L1` · moves SA-ALLG→3, KM-EQPT→3*

*Objectives:* [S] prepare tins; check the oven; [K] gluten, egg, milk and nuts in baking; cross-contact
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m1-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** an oven that runs 20 °C hot, or a tin that sticks, ruins a bake that was weighed perfectly. And
flour dust travels: an allergen-free cake can be made unsafe by the bake next to it.

**You need:** a round tin and a loaf tin; baking paper; butter and flour; the oven thermometer; a litre of water and the
scale; a 500 g pack of butter; oil and kitchen paper; the cast-iron skillet of `EQP-1`; the calibration log; the
allergen checklist.

**Explanation:**

*Preparing tins:*
- grease and line them with baking paper, base and sides for long bakes;
- butter and flour fluted tins.

The tin size changes the depth, and so the bake time: a smaller tin means a deeper cake and a longer, lower bake.

*Tin care.* Carbon-steel and tinned tins rust if left wet. Dry them in the cooling oven and keep them lightly oiled. The
cast-iron skillet is cared for the same way: seasoned with a thin layer of oil baked on.

*The oven:*
- preheat for at least 15–20 minutes;
- check it with the oven thermometer (`EQP-1`), because a dial can be off by 10–20 °C or more;
- know its hot spots (`EQP-1.M2`), and turn a tray halfway if one side colours first;
- fan ovens bake hotter and faster: set them about 20 °C lower than a conventional oven.

Write the offset on a label on the oven, for example "dial 180 = 165 real".

*Checking the scale.* A litre of water weighs 1000 g, and a 500 g pack of butter about 500 g. Note the results in the
calibration log (`KM-EQPT`).

*Allergens in baking* (`SA-ALLG`):
- **the usual ones:** gluten (wheat, spelt, rye, barley, oats), egg, milk, nuts, peanuts, soy, sesame, and lupin (in
  some flours);
- **hidden sources:** chocolate (milk, soy), marzipan (almonds), some baking powders (wheat starch), sprinkles;
- **reading labels:** the 14 EU allergen groups are printed in bold (`SAF-1.M4.L1`); "may contain" counts for a severe
  allergy.

*Cross-contact.* The allergen-free bake is made first, with washed bowls, tins and utensils, and stored apart, covered
and labelled. Flour dust settles everywhere: don't sift the ordinary flour while the allergen-free batter stands open.

**Practice:**
1. **Tins and oven (40 min).**
   - Line a round tin and a loaf tin.
   - Place the oven thermometer, set the dial to 180 °C, and compare after 20 minutes; write the offset on the oven.
   - Weigh a litre of water.
   - Oil the carbon-steel tins and season the skillet; photograph them.

**Look for:** tins that release cleanly; the oven offset on a label; no rust.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A pale, sunken or flat bake | Baking in a cold oven | Preheat 15–20 minutes |
| Burnt or raw at the stated time | Trusting the dial | The thermometer and the offset label |
| Rust spots on the bake | A rusted tin | Dry in the warm oven; oil lightly |
| An allergic reaction | Flour from an ordinary bake dusting an allergen-free one | Allergen-free first, washed tools, stored apart |

**Safety:** the allergen rules of `SAF-1.M4`: every label read for the named allergen, "may contain" counted for a
severe allergy, the allergen-free bake made first with washed tools and stored apart. Oven gloves for every tin.

**Record:** the calibration log, the offset label, the photos of the tins and skillet.

### BAK-2.M2.L1 — The muffin method

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m2-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M1.L2` · moves TS-BAK→2*

*Objectives:* [S] wet into dry, minimal mixing; [K] soda versus powder and their acids
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the muffin method is the quickest bake there is: two bowls and a dozen strokes. Its one rule, mix as
little as possible, is the first lesson in controlling gluten, and it carries into pancakes, quick breads and cakes.

**You need:** the muffin formula scaled to 12 (`BAK-2.M1.X1`), twice; 240 g of buttermilk and 4 g of baking soda for the
soda batch; two bowls; a whisk and a spatula; paper cases; the oven thermometer; a skewer; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*The method:*
1. Whisk the dry ingredients together: flour, leavener, salt, and the sugar.
2. Whisk the wet ones together: eggs, milk or buttermilk, melted butter or oil.
3. Pour the wet into the dry.
4. Fold with a spatula in about a dozen strokes, until just combined. A few streaks and lumps remain.

*Why minimal mixing.* Mixing develops gluten. A beaten muffin batter bakes tough, with a peaked top and long tunnels
running up through the crumb.

*Soda versus powder:*
- **soda** reacts at once with the acid (here, the buttermilk), so the batter goes into the oven without delay;
- **double-acting powder** gives a second rise in the oven, so the batter can wait a little.

A formula with an acid ingredient and too little soda bakes dense and tastes sour; too much soda tastes soapy and browns
too fast.

*Baking.* A hot start, about 200 °C, sets the edges and pushes the centre up into a dome; then lower, to 180 °C, to bake
through. Fill the cases about three-quarters full. Done when a skewer in the centre comes out clean and the top springs
back.

**Practice:**
1. **Mixed right and mixed wrong (45 min).** Two batches of the same formula: one folded in a dozen strokes, one beaten
   for a minute with the whisk. Bake them on the same tray, cut and photograph them.
2. **Soda and powder (45 min).** One batch with milk and baking powder; one with 240 g of buttermilk for the milk and
   4 g of baking soda instead of the powder. Compare the rise, the colour and the taste.

**Look for:** a lumpy batter that is barely mixed; muffins with rounded tops and an even, tender crumb, without tunnels.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Peaked tops, tunnels, toughness | Overmixing | A dozen strokes |
| A flat, dense muffin | Batter left standing too long with soda | Into the oven at once |
| Pale, flat tops | An oven not up to heat | Preheat; check the thermometer |
| Batter running over, flat "mushroom" tops | Overfilling the cups | Three-quarters full |

**Safety:** a hot tray and the oven door: gloves on both hands.

**Record:** the four cross-section photos and a line on each.

### BAK-2.M2.L2 — Pancakes and scones

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m2-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] pancakes; rubbing in for scones; [J] judge the batter and dough
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a stack of *pannenkoeken* is a Dutch dinner, and a batch of scones is ready in half an hour. Both
are about judging a batter or dough by eye and hand, rather than by the clock.

**You need:** for thick pancakes, 200 g of flour, 10 g of baking powder, 25 g of sugar, a pinch of salt, an egg, 250 ml
of milk, 30 g of melted butter; for *pannenkoeken*, 250 g of flour, 2 eggs, 500 ml of milk, a pinch of salt; toppings
(leek and cheese, or bacon and *stroop*); for scones, the formula of `BAK-2.M2.X1`; a frying pan; a cutter.

**Explanation:**

*Pancakes:*
- **thick pancakes:** the muffin method, with a thicker batter that is rested briefly;
- **Dutch *pannenkoeken*** and *crêpes*: a thin batter whisked smooth and rested for 30 minutes, so the gluten relaxes
  and the starch hydrates. Whisk the eggs and half the milk into the flour first, to a smooth paste, then the rest.

Cook them in a medium-hot pan, lightly greased. The first one tests the heat: too pale and greasy means the pan is too
cold, and dark spots before the top sets mean it is too hot. Turn thick pancakes when bubbles burst on the surface and
stay open.

*Rubbing in (scones):*
1. Cut cold butter into the flour and rub it with your fingertips until it looks like breadcrumbs. The fat coats the
   flour and stays in small pieces, which make steam and flakiness.
2. Add the liquid and bring it together with a knife.
3. Pat it out about 2.5 cm thick, without kneading.
4. Cut straight down, without twisting, so the scones rise evenly.

*Judging the dough and batter:*
- a scone dough is soft and slightly sticky, not dry;
- a pancake batter coats a spoon and flows.

Each is adjusted with a spoon of flour or liquid, not by kneading or more whisking.

**Practice:**
1. **Pancakes (45 min).** A thick pancake batter and a thin *pannenkoek* batter; a stack of each for breakfast or
   dinner, with seasonal toppings: leek and cheese, or bacon and *stroop*.
2. **Scones (45 min).** Rubbed-in scones, cut and baked in a hot oven (about 200 °C); one scone twisted as it is cut,
   to see the uneven rise.

**Look for:** an even golden colour on the pancakes, with bubbles that burst before turning; tall scones with straight
sides and a split middle.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pale, greasy pancakes | A cold pan | Heat it; test with the first one |
| Flat, heavy scones | Butter rubbed in until it melts | Cold butter, cool fingertips, work quickly |
| Tough, squat scones | Kneaded scone dough | Pat it out; bring it together only |
| Lopsided scones | Twisting the cutter | Cut straight down |

**Safety:** hot fat in the pan; a dry pan handle turned away from the edge. Pancakes with bacon are cooked until the
bacon is crisp.

**Record:** photos of the stacks and of the scones, including the twisted one.

### BAK-2.M3.L1 — *Pâte brisée*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 h · builds on `BAK-2.M1.L2` · moves TS-BAK→2*

*Objectives:* [S] rub in, add water sparingly, rest; [K] gluten and fat in pastry
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a short, crisp pastry base is what makes a quiche or a tart. It is three ingredients and a few
minutes' work; what goes wrong is almost always warm butter, too much water or no rest.

**You need:** 200 g of plain flour; 100 g of cold butter in cubes; 2 g of salt; about 40–50 g of iced water; an egg yolk
(optional); a bowl; a knife or fork; film; the scale.

**Explanation:**

*The formula:* about 2 : 1 flour to cold butter by weight, a pinch of salt, and just enough cold water, about 20–25 % of
the flour (40–50 g for 200 g), to bring it together. An egg yolk, in place of some of the water, adds richness and makes
it easier to handle.

*Rubbing in:*
- cut the cold butter into the flour with your fingertips, a pastry cutter or a few pulses of a food processor;
- stop when it looks like coarse crumbs with some pea-sized pieces of butter left;
- work quickly, and lift the crumbs as you rub, so your hands don't warm the butter.

*Water, sparingly.* Add it a spoon at a time and mix with a knife or fork until the dough just holds when you squeeze a
handful. More water develops more gluten, and the pastry turns tough and shrinks in the oven. Note the water you used:
flours vary, and next time you'll know.

*Gluten and fat.* Fat coats the flour and shortens the gluten strands. The more evenly the butter is rubbed in, the more
tender and short the pastry; the bigger the pieces, the flakier, because they melt into steam pockets.

*Resting.* Press the dough into a flat disc, wrap it and chill it for at least an hour. The gluten relaxes and the
butter firms, so the pastry rolls easily and shrinks less. Wrapped well, it keeps in the freezer for later use: thaw it
in the fridge.

**Practice:**
1. ***Pâte brisée* (45 min, plus an hour's rest).** One batch rubbed in by hand; the water used noted; rested, then kept
   or frozen for `BAK-2.M3.L2`.

**Look for:** coarse crumbs with visible butter; a dough that just holds together; no greasy, sticky patches.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A greasy, hard pastry | Warm hands melting the butter | Fingertips only; chill the bowl if needed |
| A tough pastry that shrinks | Too much water | A spoon at a time; squeeze-test |
| A tough, elastic dough | Kneading | Bring it together, no more |
| Pastry that springs back and tears | Skipping the rest | At least an hour in the fridge |

**Safety:** none specific; a food processor blade is lifted out before you scrape the bowl.

### BAK-2.M3.L2 — Rolling, lining and blind baking

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] roll evenly, line a tin, blind bake; [J] prevent shrinking and soggy bases
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a tart lives or dies by its base. Shrunken sides spill the filling, and a soggy base makes the whole
slice limp. Both are prevented before the filling goes in.

**You need:** the *brisée* of `BAK-2.M3.L1`, and a second batch; two 23 cm loose-bottomed tart tins (or one, used
twice); a rolling pin; flour for dusting; baking paper; baking beans, dried beans or rice; an egg for the wash; a sheet
pan; the oven thermometer.

**Explanation:**

*Rolling:*
- on a lightly floured surface, from the centre outwards;
- turn the dough a quarter after every few rolls, so it stays round and doesn't stick;
- to an even 3–4 mm, and a circle about 5 cm wider than the tin.

*Lining:*
1. Roll the pastry over the pin and drape it over the tin.
2. Ease it into the corners without stretching it: lift the edge and let it drop in. Stretched pastry shrinks back.
3. Leave a little overhang.
4. Chill the lined tin for 20–30 minutes.

*Blind baking:*
1. Preheat the oven with a sheet pan on the shelf.
2. Line the pastry with crumpled baking paper and fill it to the top with baking beans, dried beans or rice.
3. Bake on the hot pan at about 180–190 °C until the edges are set and pale gold.
4. Lift out the paper and beans, and bake again until the base is dry and golden.
5. Trim the overhang with a sharp knife after baking, for a clean edge.

*Preventing shrinking and soggy bases:*
- **against shrinking:** a rested, unstretched dough, chilled in the tin, and the beans filled to the top so the sides
  are supported until they set;
- **against a soggy base:** a fully blind-baked base, brushed with egg wash in the last minutes to seal it, a hot oven
  shelf or pan, and the filling added just before baking.

**Practice:**
1. **Line and blind bake (60 min).** Roll and line a 23 cm tin; chill; blind bake fully; photograph the base and edge.
2. **Fix a fault (45 min).** Line a second tin, stretching the pastry and skipping the chill on purpose; blind bake;
   photograph and compare the shrinking.

**Look for:** an even thickness; the edges standing up straight after baking; a dry, golden base without cracks.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Sides shrunk down the tin | Stretching the pastry into the tin | Ease it in; lift and drop |
| Pale patches and dark patches | Rolling unevenly, so it bakes unevenly | Quarter turns; 3–4 mm |
| Slumped sides | Removing the beans too early, so the sides slump | Edges set and pale gold first |
| A soft, pale base | An underbaked, pale base | Bake on until dry and golden; egg wash |

**Safety:** baking beans hold heat for a long time: lift them out in the paper, with gloves, into a heatproof bowl.

**Record:** the photos of both shells side by side.

### BAK-2.M3.L3 — *Pâte sucrée*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l3 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 h · builds on `BAK-2.M3.L2` · moves TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] the creaming method for sweet pastry; handle a soft dough
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m3-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a sweet tart needs a sweet, crisp shell that tastes like a fine biscuit. *Pâte sucrée* is made the
other way round from *brisée*: the butter is creamed, not rubbed in, and the dough is soft, so it needs different
handling.

**You need:** 200 g of plain flour; 100 g of soft butter; 70 g of icing sugar; 1 egg (about 50 g); a pinch of salt; a
bowl and a spatula or the mixer; two sheets of baking paper; a rolling pin; a 23 cm tart tin; film.

**Explanation:**

*Pâte sucrée* is a sweet, crisp, biscuit-like pastry, made by the creaming method:
1. Cream the soft butter with the icing sugar and salt until smooth.
2. Beat in the egg.
3. Mix in the flour just until combined. Stop as soon as no dry flour shows.

The sugar and the creaming give a fine, crisp crumb. Overmixing after the flour develops gluten and makes it tough and
prone to shrinking.

*A soft dough:*
- press it into a flat disc, wrap it and chill it well, at least an hour;
- roll it between two sheets of baking paper, so it doesn't stick or need extra flour;
- if it softens while you work, slide it, still in the paper, onto a tray and chill it for 10 minutes;
- patch any cracks with off-cuts, pressed in: *sucrée* forgives patching, unlike *brisée*.

*Lining and baking.* Line the tin as for *brisée*, and chill it. Bake at a slightly lower heat than *brisée*, about
170–180 °C, because the sugar browns quickly. Blind bake it the same way, with paper and beans, then without, until the
shell is an even golden brown.

*Uses:* fruit tarts, the cream tart of `BAK-2.M3.X2`, and small tartlets. A lined, unbaked shell freezes well, and can
go into the oven from frozen.

**Practice:**
1. ***Pâte sucrée* (45 min, plus an hour's rest).** Creamed, chilled, rolled between paper, lined, and frozen for
   `BAK-2.M3.X2`.

**Look for:** a smooth, soft dough that firms in the fridge; rolled without tearing between the papers; a lined shell
with even sides.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Sticky, tearing dough | Rolling the dough warm | Chill it; roll between paper |
| A tough shell that shrinks | Over-mixing after the flour | Stop when no dry flour shows |
| A burnt edge, a pale base | Baking too hot, so the edge burns | About 170–180 °C; check the thermometer |

**Safety:** none specific.

**Record:** a photo of the lined shell, and a note of how long the dough needed in the fridge.

### BAK-2.M4.L1 — The creaming method

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M1.L2` · moves TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] cream butter and sugar; add eggs without splitting; fold in flour
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** most home cakes, from a Dutch *cake* to a Victoria sponge, are made by creaming. The rise starts in
the bowl, as air beaten into butter and sugar; if it isn't there before the oven, no baking powder fully makes up for
it.

**You need:** for the pound cake: 200 g each of butter, sugar, eggs and flour, 4 g of baking powder, a pinch of salt;
two small loaf tins for the creaming test; for the sponge: the same formula again, in two 20 cm round tins; the mixer;
a spatula; a skewer; a rack.

**Explanation:**

*Creaming:*
1. Beat soft butter (cool to the touch, about 18–20 °C) with the sugar for 3–5 minutes, until pale and fluffy. The sugar
   crystals cut air pockets into the fat, and that air is the start of the rise.
2. Add the eggs at room temperature, one at a time or in a thin stream, beating well after each. The yolk's
   emulsifiers hold the water of the egg in the fat. Cold eggs, or eggs added too fast, split the mixture, which looks
   curdled. A spoon of the flour rescues it.
3. Fold in the flour (sifted with the baking powder and salt), and any liquid, gently, just until combined, so the air
   stays in and little gluten develops.

*Butter cakes* made this way: pound cake (*cake* in Dutch), *boterkoek*-style cakes, a Victoria sponge. The baking
powder supports the creamed air; it doesn't replace it.

*Baking.* Bake in the middle of the oven at about 170–180 °C, and don't open the door until the cake has set, at least
two-thirds of the way through: a draught of cold air can make a rising cake sink.

*Done* when a skewer in the centre comes out clean, the top springs back when pressed lightly, and the cake shrinks just
away from the tin. Cool it in the tin for 10 minutes, then turn it out onto a rack.

**Practice:**
1. **A pound cake, two creaming times (60 min, plus baking).** One batch of batter, split: half creamed for 1 minute,
   half for 5 minutes (cream the butter and sugar in two bowls), in two small loaf tins. Bake side by side, cut and
   photograph.
2. **A Victoria-style sponge (60 min, plus baking).** Creamed, in two 20 cm tins, for the layer cake of `BAK-2.M4.L4`.
   Cool, wrap and freeze or keep them for a day.

**Look for:** butter and sugar pale and fluffy; a smooth, uncurdled mixture after the eggs; a flat or gently domed top
with an even crumb.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Lumpy, airless creaming | Cold butter that won't cream | Butter cool to the touch, about 18–20 °C |
| A greasy, flat cake | Melted butter | Soft, not melted |
| A curdled mixture, a dense cake | Eggs from the fridge | Room-temperature eggs; a spoon of flour |
| Tunnels, a tough crumb | Overmixing after the flour | Fold just to combine |
| A sunken centre | Opening the oven early | Wait until it has set |

**Safety:** none specific beyond the hot tins: gloves, and the rack ready before the cake comes out.

**Record:** the creaming-test photo, the oven reading and the bake times in the baking log.

### BAK-2.M4.L2 — Sponge (*génoise*)

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M4.L1` · moves TS-BAK→3, SC-EMUL→3*

*Objectives:* [S] whisk eggs to the ribbon; fold without losing air; [K] foams
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a *génoise* has no baking powder at all: it rises on the air in an egg foam. Learning to whisk to
the ribbon and fold without losing that air is the skill behind sponges, rolled cakes and many desserts.

**You need:** 4 eggs, 125 g of sugar, 125 g of flour, 30 g of butter; a heatproof bowl over a pan of simmering water;
the mixer; a large spatula; a sieve; a lined 20 cm tin; a second set of eggs and sugar for the whisking test.

**Explanation:**

*A foam does the rising:*
1. Whisk whole eggs and sugar over a bain-marie until just warm to the touch, about body temperature, then off the heat,
   until they triple in volume and reach the ribbon stage (`SAU-2.M5.L1`): batter falling from the whisk lies on the
   surface for a few seconds before sinking.
2. Sift and fold in the flour, in two or three parts.
3. Fold in the melted, cooled butter.

The air in the egg foam expands in the oven, and the egg proteins set around it.

*Foams* (`SC-EMUL`). A foam is gas dispersed in a liquid, stabilised by proteins. Sugar stabilises an egg foam; fat and
rough folding collapse it. Warming helps the sugar dissolve and the eggs whisk higher.

*Folding:*
- use a large spatula: cut down through the middle, sweep along the bottom and turn the batter over, while turning the
  bowl;
- add the flour in two or three parts, sifted over the surface;
- mix the melted butter first with a spoonful of batter, then fold that in, so the heavy fat doesn't sink and knock out
  the air.

*Baking.* Straight into the lined tin and into the oven at once, at about 180 °C, because the foam starts to collapse as
it waits. Done when it springs back when pressed and shrinks from the sides.

**Practice:**
1. **Whisk to the ribbon (60 min).** Two bowls of 2 eggs and 60 g of sugar: one warmed over the bain-marie, one cold.
   Whisk each for the same time; photograph the volume and the ribbon. Use both foams in an omelette or a batch of
   biscuits rather than throwing them away.
2. **A *génoise* (60 min, plus baking).** Folded, baked, cooled, cut through and photographed.

**Look for:** a pale, thick foam that holds a ribbon for a few seconds; a batter that stays light after folding; an
even, fine, springy crumb.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A flat, dense sponge | Under-whipped eggs | Whisk to a ribbon that holds |
| Flour pockets or a heavy batter | Stirring rather than folding | Cut, sweep and turn |
| A greasy, dense layer at the base | Butter poured in hot or all at once | Cooled butter, loosened with batter |
| A sunken, close crumb | A batter left waiting | Straight into the oven |

**Safety:** steam from the bain-marie scalds: an oven glove on the hand that holds the bowl.

**Record:** the ribbon photos, the cross-section and the log entry.

### BAK-2.M4.L3 — Diagnosing cakes

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l3 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `BAK-2.M4.L2` · moves JD-TRBL→3, FL-TEXT→2*

*Objectives:* [J] sunken, domed, dense, dry, tunnelled: causes and fixes
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a cake that went wrong tells you why, if you cut it and read the crumb. Diagnosing from evidence,
and changing one thing next time, is how a baker improves, rather than blaming the oven or the recipe.

**You need:** the course's five photos of faulty cakes; your own bakes from `BAK-2.M4.L1` and `BAK-2.M4.L2` and their
log entries; the baking log.

**Explanation:**

*Reading the crumb:*

| Fault | Likely causes | Fix next time |
|---|---|---|
| Sunken centre | Too much leavener or sugar; oven door opened early; underbaked | Weigh the leavener; check the oven; bake to the skewer |
| High dome, cracked top | Oven too hot; too much flour; too much mixing | Lower the heat (check with the thermometer); weigh the flour; fold less |
| Dense, heavy | Under-creamed; a collapsed foam; too little leavener; curdled batter | Cream longer; fold gently; weigh |
| Dry | Overbaked; too little fat or sugar; too much flour | Bake to the skewer; weigh |
| Tunnels | Overmixing after the flour | Fold just to combine |
| Sticky or sinking edges | Underbaked | Bake longer; check the oven |

*Cut it to look.* Cut through the centre with a serrated knife, top to bottom, and photograph the face in good light.
Look at the top (flat, domed, cracked, sunken), the crumb (even, coarse, tunnelled, dense), the base (a dense wet
streak?) and the edges.

*One fault, one change.* The cause is diagnosed from the crumb and the log: the time, the oven reading and the weights.
A dense cake from a log that shows cold eggs points at the eggs, not the oven. The next bake changes one thing, so its
effect can be seen. Change three things and you won't know which one worked.

*The baking log* records every bake: the formula, the weights, the oven reading, the time, a photo, your band, and the
AI or taster band. It is the evidence for `BAK-2.M6.L2`.

**Practice:**
1. **Diagnose five crumbs (40 min).** The course's five photos of faulty cakes, plus your own bakes so far: name the
   most likely cause and the one change for each, in the log.

**Look for:** a diagnosis that names the most likely cause from evidence, and a single change.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No idea which change helped | Changing three things at once | One change per bake |
| The same fault again | Blaming the oven without checking it | The thermometer first |
| A diagnosis from the top alone | Not cutting the cake to look | Cut and photograph |

**Safety:** none specific.

**Record:** the five diagnoses in the log.

### BAK-2.M4.L4 — Buttercreams, fillings and finishing a cake

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l4 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `BAK-2.M4.L2` · moves TS-BAK→3, JD-TRBL→3*

*Objectives:* [S] American and Swiss meringue buttercream; [S] split, fill, crumb-coat and finish a layer cake; [K] why buttercream splits and how to rescue it
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m4-l4 -->

**Why it matters:** a birthday cake is the bake people remember. Filling and finishing it neatly takes patience and
cold, not talent; and a buttercream that splits can nearly always be rescued.

**You need:** the two sponges of `BAK-2.M4.L1`; for the buttercream, the formula of `BAK-2.M4.X3`; a bain-marie; the
probe; the mixer; a serrated knife; a palette knife; a cake board or plate; a compote (optional).

**Explanation:**

*American buttercream:* soft butter beaten with icing sugar and a little milk. It is quick, sweet and stable.

*Swiss meringue buttercream:*
1. Whisk egg whites and sugar over a bain-marie until the sugar has dissolved and every part reaches at least 71 °C on
   the probe (the egg-thickened row of assessment §9, which names Swiss meringue).
2. Whip it to a stiff, glossy meringue, and keep whipping until it has cooled to room temperature.
3. Beat in soft butter piece by piece. It may look curdled halfway: keep beating.

It is less sweet and silky.

*Why it splits, and the rescue.* Buttercream is an emulsion of fat and the water in the meringue.
- **Too cold** (the butter or the meringue): it curdles and looks lumpy. Warm the bowl briefly over the bain-marie or
  with a warm towel, and beat.
- **Too warm:** it turns soupy. Chill it for 15 minutes, and beat again.

Never throw a split buttercream away: one of these two fixes nearly always works.

*Finishing a layer cake:*
1. Level the cooled cakes with a serrated knife, and split them if needed.
2. Fill them: buttercream, a compote, or a cream.
3. Apply a thin crumb coat, which seals in the crumbs, and chill it until firm.
4. Apply a final coat, smoothed with the palette knife while you turn the plate.

Chilling between steps makes each easier. A finished buttercream cake is kept in the fridge, and taken out a while
before serving so the buttercream softens.

**Practice:**
1. **Buttercream and a cake (75 min, over two days).** A Swiss meringue buttercream, deliberately split by adding butter
   that is too cold, then rescued; the sponges of `BAK-2.M4.L1` filled, crumb-coated, chilled overnight and finished.

**Look for:** a silky buttercream that holds a peak; a level cake with straight sides; no crumbs in the final coat.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Wasted butter and sugar | Throwing away a split buttercream | Warm it or chill it, and beat |
| A sliding, melting filling | Filling warm cakes | Cakes fully cooled |
| Crumbs through the coat | Skipping the crumb coat | A thin coat, chilled |
| Torn sponge, a ragged coat | Buttercream too warm to spread | Chill 15 minutes; beat |

**Safety:** the Swiss meringue reaches 71 °C in every part, probed (assessment §9). The finished cake is kept in the
fridge and eaten within 2 days.

**Record:** photos of the split and the rescue, and the finished cake.

### BAK-2.M5.L1 — *Crème pâtissière*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `EGG-1.M4.L1`, `SCI-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-EGG→4, TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] temper, boil to set the starch, cool safely; [K] why it must boil and a *crème anglaise* mustn't
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** pastry cream fills tarts, choux and cakes. Its rule is the opposite of a *crème anglaise*: it must
boil. Knowing why each does what it does is the key to both, and to keeping them safely.

**You need:** for the pastry cream, the quantities of `BAK-2.M5.X1`; for the *crème anglaise*, 250 ml of milk, 3 yolks,
40 g of sugar, vanilla; a heavy pan; a whisk; a sieve; the probe; a shallow tray and film; a bowl in an ice bath.

**Explanation:**

*Crème pâtissière* (pastry cream): milk, yolks, sugar and a starch (cornflour or flour), boiled to a thick, smooth
cream.

*The method:*
1. Heat the milk with the vanilla.
2. Whisk the yolks, sugar and starch together.
3. Temper them: whisk in a third of the hot milk.
4. Return everything to the pan, and whisk constantly, into the corners, as it comes to the boil.
5. Boil for 1–2 minutes, still whisking.

*Why it must boil.* The starch must gelatinise fully to thicken and lose its raw taste. The boil also destroys an enzyme
in the yolk (amylase) that would otherwise digest the starch and thin the cream overnight. The starch protects the yolk
from curdling, so boiling is safe here.

*Why a crème anglaise mustn't boil.* With no starch, the yolks set and curdle above about 84 °C. It is stirred over
gentle heat to *nappe*, about 80–84 °C on the probe (`EGG-1.M4.L1`), when it coats the spoon and a finger drawn through
it leaves a clean line. Then it is strained at once into a cold bowl.

*Cooling safely* (assessment §9):
1. Spread the pastry cream in a shallow tray.
2. Cover the surface with film, against a skin.
3. Cool it from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C within 6 hours in total, probing and logging the times.
4. Keep it covered in the fridge, at most 2 days.

The *crème anglaise* is cooled the same way, stirred in its bowl over the ice bath.

**Practice:**
1. ***Crème pâtissière* (45 min).** Boiled for the full time; cooled in a tray with the temperature logged.
2. ***Crème anglaise* (40 min).** Stirred to *nappe*, the temperature probed, strained and cooled in an ice bath;
   served with a baked fruit or the cake of `BAK-2.M4`.

**Look for:** a pastry cream thick, glossy and smooth after the boil; a *crème anglaise* that coats the spoon with a
clean line through it, without grains.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A cream that is runny next day | Pastry cream taken off before it boils, so it thins overnight | Boil 1–2 minutes |
| Lumps | Lumps from not whisking into the corners | Whisk into the corners; sieve |
| A grainy, split *anglaise* | A *crème anglaise* boiled until it curdles | Probe; stop at 80–84 °C |
| Slow cooling, a safety risk | A deep bowl of hot cream in the fridge | A shallow tray; the ice bath |

**Safety:** the cooling rule of assessment §9: from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours and to 5 °C within 6 hours, then at
most 2 days in the fridge. Hot pastry cream spits as it boils: a long whisk, and a sleeve over your wrist.

**Record:** the cooling log for both creams.

### BAK-2.M5.L2 — Baked custards

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 h · builds on `EGG-1.M4.L1` · moves TS-EGG→4*

*Objectives:* [S] *crème caramel* and a baked custard; water bath; [J] judge the wobble
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a *crème caramel* is eggs, milk and sugar, set in the oven. Silky or grainy depends on gentle heat
and on knowing when to take it out, which you judge by a wobble rather than a timer.

**You need:** for four ramekins and one larger dish: 150 g of sugar for the caramel; 500 ml of milk; 3 eggs and 2 yolks;
60 g of sugar; vanilla; a deep roasting tin; a kettle; the probe; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*A baked custard:* eggs, milk or cream and sugar, set in the oven without starch. Examples are *crème caramel*, *crème
brûlée* and *pots de crème*.

*The water bath.* The ramekins stand in a deep tin of hot water, which comes halfway up their sides, in a moderate oven
(about 150–160 °C). The water never goes above 100 °C, so the custard sets gently and evenly, without bubbles or
curdling. Pour the hot water in with the tin already on the oven shelf, so you don't carry it full.

*Crème caramel:*
1. Make a caramel: sugar heated dry, or with a little water, until deep amber (`SAU-2`). Pour it at once into the moulds
   and tilt to coat the bases.
2. Warm the milk with the vanilla; whisk it gently into the eggs, yolks and sugar without making foam; strain it.
3. Pour it over the set caramel, and bake in the water bath.
4. Chill for at least 4 hours: the custard firms, and the caramel dissolves into a sauce.
5. Run a thin knife round the edge, and turn it out onto a plate.

*Judging the wobble.* Done when the edges are set and the centre wobbles like a jelly, not a liquid, when the ramekin is
nudged. It sets further as it cools. A knife in the centre comes out with a thin coat. Probe one: every part at least
71 °C (the egg-thickened row of assessment §9).

*Adapting.* A larger dish takes longer than ramekins at the same heat, because the heat has further to travel to the
centre. Start checking the ramekins early, and the dish later; judge both by the wobble and log the times.

**Practice:**
1. ***Crème caramel* (60 min, plus 4 hours' chilling).** Caramel, custard and water bath; four ramekins and one larger
   dish; the wobble judged and the times logged; unmoulded.

**Look for:** a smooth, glossy custard with no bubbles; a clean unmoulding with a pool of caramel; an even texture right
through when cut.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Bubbles, a curdled texture | No water bath, so the custard bubbles and curdles | A water bath |
| A grainy, weeping custard | Baking until the centre is firm, so it turns grainy and weeps | Out at the jelly wobble |
| A bitter sauce | A burnt caramel | Off the heat at deep amber |

**Safety:** caramel is far hotter than boiling water and sticks to skin: pour it slowly, keep a bowl of cold water
nearby for your fingers, and keep children away. The custards are chilled within 2 hours, and kept at most 2 days.

**Record:** the times for the ramekins and the dish; the photos of the unmoulded custards and a cut one.

### BAK-2.M5.L3 — Whipped cream

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M4.L1` · moves IN-DAI→3*

*Objectives:* [S] soft, medium and stiff peaks; *chantilly*; [J] rescue over-whipped cream
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m5-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** whipped cream finishes half the desserts of this course, and the difference between soft and stiff
peaks decides whether it folds, fills or pipes. Over-whipped cream is a common mistake, and it can be rescued.

**You need:** 500 ml of whipping cream (*slagroom*), cold; a cold bowl; a whisk or the mixer; icing sugar and vanilla;
a few spoons of extra liquid cream; *mascarpone* or *kwark* (optional).

**Explanation:**

*Whipping.* Cream of at least 30 % fat (Dutch *slagroom* is about 35 %), cold, in a cold bowl. As you whip, the fat
droplets, partly crystallised, join into a network around the air bubbles (`SCI-2.M4.L1`). Warm cream has liquid fat,
which can't form that network, so it won't whip. Light cooking cream (*kookroom*) has too little fat.

*The peaks.* Lift the whisk and look:
- **soft:** the peak flops over; for folding into mousses and serving with cake;
- **medium:** the tip bends; for filling;
- **stiff:** it stands up; for piping and stabilising.

The last stage goes fast. With an electric whisk, switch to a hand whisk near the end, so you can stop exactly.

*Chantilly:* cream whipped with a little icing sugar and vanilla, added when it starts to thicken.

*Rescuing over-whipped cream.* When it turns grainy, fold in a few spoons of liquid cream by hand, until it is smooth
again. Taken much further, it becomes butter and buttermilk, which can both be used: the butter for cooking, the
buttermilk for the muffins of `BAK-2.M2.L1`.

*Stabilising* for a filling that must hold for hours: a spoon of *mascarpone*, or *kwark*, folded in.

*Keeping.* Whipped cream is kept covered in the fridge and used the same day or the next.

**Practice:**
1. **Peaks and a rescue (30 min).** Whip cream to soft, medium and stiff peaks, photographing each; over-whip a portion
   until grainy, and rescue it with liquid cream.

**Look for:** peaks that match the target; a smooth, glossy surface; the rescued cream smooth again.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Cream that won't thicken | Warm cream that won't whip | Cold cream, cold bowl |
| Cream that won't hold a peak | Low-fat cream | *Slagroom*, at least 30 % fat |
| Grainy cream | An electric whisk left running until it turns grainy | Finish by hand; fold in liquid cream |

**Safety:** none specific.

**Record:** the photos of the three peaks and the rescue.

### BAK-2.M6.L1 — Biscuit texture

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m6-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M1.L1` · moves RD-TEST→2*

*Objectives:* [S] change one variable (sugar, fat, resting, flour) to make chewy or crisp
<!-- END generated:lesson-bak-2-m6-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** chewy or crisp, thick or thin: every biscuit comes from a few variables you can control. Changing
one at a time, and seeing exactly what it does, is the method of every test you will run as a cook.

**You need:** for each batch, about 250 g of flour, 125 g of butter, 125 g of sugar (white and brown), an egg, 2 g of
baking powder, a pinch of salt; two sheet pans and baking paper; a scoop or spoon and the scale, so every biscuit
weighs the same; a ruler.

**Explanation:**

*The variables of a biscuit:*

| Variable | Chewier, thicker | Crisper, thinner, more spread |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | More brown sugar (holds water) | More white sugar |
| Fat | Melted butter; less fat | Creamed butter; more fat |
| Flour | Bread flour (more gluten) | Cake flour |
| Egg | An extra yolk | Less egg, or white only |
| Resting | Chilled dough, rested overnight (less spread, deeper flavour) | Baked at once |
| Baking | Pulled while the centre is soft | Baked until golden throughout |

*One variable at a time* (`RD-TEST`, `SCI-2.M1`): one control dough and one changed dough, otherwise identical. Weigh
each ball of dough (for example 30 g), bake them on the same tray, at about 180 °C, and taste them blind. Measure the
spread with a ruler.

*Dutch biscuits* to try the framework on:
- ***boterkoekjes*:** rich, short butter biscuits;
- ***speculaas*** in winter: spiced, thin and crisp;
- ***kletskoppen*:** very thin and crisp, from a high-sugar, high-butter dough that spreads.

*Reading the results.* Spread comes from fat melting before the structure sets, and from sugar dissolving; chew comes
from moisture held by brown sugar and egg yolk, and from gluten. If the result surprised you, the table usually shows
why.

**Practice:**
1. **A chewy and a crisp biscuit (50 min).** The same base formula changed in sugar and fat: brown sugar and melted
   butter for one, white sugar and creamed butter for the other. Photograph them side by side.
2. **The rested dough (50 min, the next day).** The same dough baked straight, and after a night in the fridge; the
   spread measured with a ruler.

**Look for:** spread and texture that follow the variable as predicted; biscuits of equal size on one tray.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No clear conclusion | Changing two variables | One change only |
| Uneven baking, an unfair test | Different sizes on one tray | Weigh each ball |
| Hard "chewy" biscuits | Overbaking chewy biscuits | Pull them while the centre is soft |

**Safety:** none specific; the trays come out with gloves.

**Record:** the photos, the spread measurements and the predictions.

### BAK-2.M6.L2 — The baking log

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-bak-2-m6-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** after seven weeks the baking log holds a dozen or more bakes. Read together, they show what single

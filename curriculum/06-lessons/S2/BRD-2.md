**Before you start.** Bread is four ingredients (flour, water, salt and yeast) and a great deal of time. What turns them
into a loaf with a crackling crust and an open, chewy crumb is control of two living processes:
- **fermentation:** yeast producing gas and flavour;
- **gluten development:** the protein network that holds the gas.

Home bread mostly fails because the baker follows the clock instead of the dough, or bakes without the steam and heat
that make a crust. This course takes a lean dough through every stage: mixing to a target temperature, bulk
fermentation read by rise and feel, shaping with tension, proofing with the poke test, and baking with steam in a home
oven. It ends with a soft enriched dough and a weekday bake that fits around work.

*The log is the main tool.* Every bake records:
- the formula in baker's percentages (`BAK-2`);
- the dough temperature;
- the times and the room temperature;
- your estimates against the readings;
- photos of each stage and the crumb.

From it you'll see how temperature and time shape your bread, in your kitchen.

*Safety* ([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment) and `HEA-2`): a preheated pot or
stone is about 250 °C, and steam burns. Dry oven gloves on both hands, the loaf lowered in on baking paper, and water
never near hot glass.

Plan about four weeks, mostly weekend baking, with short weekday tasks once the overnight retard is learned. A lean loaf
takes about 5 hours from mixing to baking, mostly waiting. The loaves are the household's bread.

**Have ready:** the scale and the precision scale; bread flour (*broodmeel*) and instant yeast; a straight-sided dough
container with tape to mark the rise; a bench scraper; a proofing basket or a bowl with a floured tea towel; rice flour
for dusting; a razor blade or lame; the casserole of `HEA-2` with a lid; a baking stone or steel (optional) and a sheet
pan for water; two dry, thick oven gloves; the probe; the oven thermometer; a wire rack; the bread log.

### BRD-2.M1.L1 — Yeast and fermentation

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 h · builds on `SCI-2.M6.L1` · moves IN-BAK→3, SC-MICR→3*

*Objectives:* [K] fresh, dry and instant yeast; temperature and time; flavour from fermentation
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** yeast is alive, and the dough's temperature sets its pace. Once you understand that, the times in a
recipe stop being rules and become a guess for one kitchen, and you can read your own dough instead.

**You need:** 150 g of bread flour, 100 g of water, 3 g of salt and 1 g of instant yeast; three identical straight-sided
containers or jars, marked with tape; a warm spot (near, not on, a radiator); the fridge; the phone.

**Explanation:**

*Yeast* is a living fungus that eats sugars from the flour and produces carbon dioxide (the rise), and alcohol and acids
(the flavour) (`SCI-2.M6.L1`).

*The forms:*

| Yeast (NL) | Use | Relative amount |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh yeast (*verse gist*) | Crumbled into the water | About 3 |
| Active dried yeast | Dissolved in warm water first | About 1.5 |
| Instant yeast (*gedroogde gist*, sachets) | Mixed straight into the flour | 1 |

A formula with 1 % instant yeast needs about 3 % fresh. This course uses instant yeast.

*Temperature and time:*
- yeast works fastest at about 30–35 °C, slowly in the fridge, and dies at around 55–60 °C;
- a dough at 24–26 °C ferments steadily;
- the cooler and longer the fermentation, the more flavour, because acids and aromatic compounds build up while the gas
  does.

That is why bakers use less yeast and more time, or an overnight rest in the fridge.

*Salt* at about 2 % slows the yeast and tightens the gluten. A dough without salt rises fast and tastes flat. Weighed
into the flour, not poured straight onto the yeast in a small bowl.

*Sugar* in small amounts feeds the yeast, but large amounts (enriched doughs, `BRD-2.M4`) slow it, because sugar draws
water away from the yeast.

**Practice:**
1. **Yeast at three temperatures (40 min, then 2 hours of watching).** Mix the small dough, divide it into three equal
   pieces and press each into a marked container: one in the fridge, one at room temperature, one somewhere warm.
   Photograph all three every 30 minutes, and note the room temperature.

**Look for:** the warm dough doubles fastest and the fridge dough barely moves, and you can explain why.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No rise at all | Water hot enough to kill the yeast | Lukewarm water; the probe if unsure |
| A slow, patchy rise | Salt poured onto the yeast in a small bowl | Both mixed into the flour |
| An underrisen dough at the stated time | Following the clock in a cold kitchen | Read the dough; log the room temperature |

**Safety:** none specific. The fridge dough is kept covered and away from ready-to-eat food.

**Record:** the photo series and two sentences explaining it, for `BRD-2.M1.X1`.

### BRD-2.M1.L2 — Developing gluten

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m1-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M3.L2` · moves SC-STRC→3*

*Objectives:* [S] kneading, *autolyse*, stretch and fold; [K] the windowpane test
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** gluten is the net that holds the gas. Too little, and the loaf is flat and dense; developed well, it
rises tall with an open crumb. There are three ways to build it, and the windowpane test tells you when it is done.

**You need:** 250 g of bread flour, 170 g of water, 5 g of salt, 2 g of instant yeast; a clean bench; a bench scraper;
a timer; the phone.

**Explanation:**

*Gluten* forms when the proteins of wheat flour (glutenin and gliadin) take up water and are worked (`SCI-2.M3.L2`):
- **glutenin** gives strength and elasticity: the dough springs back;
- **gliadin** gives extensibility: the dough stretches.

Together they make a net that holds the gas of fermentation. Rye forms almost no such net, and spelt's is weaker and
more fragile than wheat's: those flours need other handling (`BRD-3`).

*Three ways to develop it:*
- **kneading,** by hand for about 10 minutes, or by mixer: fast and thorough. Push the dough away with the heel of your
  hand, fold it back, turn it a quarter, and repeat. It turns from shaggy to smooth and elastic;
- ***autolyse*:** flour and water mixed and rested for 20–60 minutes before the salt and yeast go in. The gluten starts
  to form without work, and the dough becomes easier to handle;
- **stretch and fold:** every 30 minutes during bulk fermentation, lift one side of the dough with a wet hand, stretch
  it up, and fold it over the rest; turn the container and repeat from four sides. Time does most of the work, and
  wetter doughs can be handled.

*Sticky dough.* A dough is sticky at first. Don't add flour while kneading until it is stiff: use the scraper, and wet
or lightly oiled hands. As the gluten develops, it stops sticking.

*The windowpane test:* tear off a small piece of dough and stretch it slowly between your fingers, turning it. A
well-developed dough stretches into a thin, translucent sheet without tearing. A dough that tears at once needs more
work, or more time.

**Practice:**
1. **Knead and test (40 min).** Knead the small dough by hand; do the windowpane test after 3, 6 and 10 minutes, and
   photograph each against the light. Then let the dough rise and bake it as a small loaf or rolls, or use it in
   `BRD-2.M1.X1`.

**Look for:** the dough turning from shaggy to smooth and elastic; a windowpane without tearing.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A stiff, dry dough and a tight crumb | Adding flour while kneading until the dough is stiff | Scraper and wet hands |
| A flat, dense loaf | Stopping at a smooth surface without testing | The windowpane test |
| A dough that tears, whatever you do | Expecting gluten from rye or spelt to behave like wheat | Bread flour here; rye and spelt in `BRD-3` |

**Safety:** none specific.

**Record:** the three windowpane photos.

**Recipe:** the lean dough in two halves.

### BRD-2.M2.L1 — Mixing a lean dough

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 3 h · builds on `BAK-2.M1.L1` · moves TS-BRD→1*

*Objectives:* [S] weigh, mix, check dough temperature; [K] hydration
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a dough mixed at the right temperature ferments in a predictable time, so your notes from one bake
help the next. Weighing and probing take two minutes, and they make every bake after them comparable.

**You need:** the bread formula you scaled in `BAK-2.M1.X1`, or 500 g of bread flour, 340 g of water, 10 g of salt and
4 g of instant yeast; the scale and the precision scale; the probe; a straight-sided container; a bench scraper; the
bread log.

**Explanation:**

*The lean formula* in baker's percentages (`BAK-2.M1.L1`):
- bread flour 100 %;
- water 65–70 %;
- salt 2 %;
- instant yeast 0.5–1 %.

The 500 g above is 100 %, 68 %, 2 % and 0.8 %.

*Hydration:*
- **lower (60–65 %):** a firmer dough that is easier to shape, with a tighter crumb;
- **higher (70–75 %):** a slacker, stickier dough, with a more open crumb.

Wholemeal flour takes more water, because the bran absorbs it. Start at 68 % with white bread flour, and change it by
one variable at a time in `BRD-2.M2.X1`.

*The target dough temperature:* about 24–26 °C after mixing. You control it with the water: warmer water in a cold
kitchen, cooler in a warm one. Probe the dough after mixing and log the water, the room and the dough temperatures.
After two or three bakes you'll know which water gives 24–26 °C in your kitchen in each season, and the bulk
fermentation times become comparable.

*Mixing:*
1. Flour and water first, until no dry flour remains. Optionally rest it for 20–60 minutes (*autolyse*,
   `BRD-2.M1.L2`).
2. Add the yeast and salt, sprinkled over, and squeeze and fold them through with a wet hand until evenly mixed.
3. Develop the gluten by kneading or by folds during the bulk.

*Weighing.* Flour is weighed, never scooped: a scooped cup packs a different amount every time. Yeast and salt go on
the precision scale.

*Sticky dough.* A 68 % dough is sticky. Work it with a wet hand and the scraper, rather than adding flour, which changes
the hydration you set.

**Practice:**
1. **Mix and log (40 min, then 3 hours' fermentation).** Weigh the formula; choose the water temperature from the room
   temperature; mix; probe the dough; record everything. Carry the dough on through `BRD-2.M2.L2`.

**Look for:** a dough at the target temperature; all the weights in the log; a dough that feels as you expected for its
hydration.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dry or tight dough | Scooping flour | Weigh it |
| Times that never match the last bake | Ignoring the dough temperature | Probe and log it |
| A stiff dough and a tight crumb | Adding flour to a sticky dough instead of using wet hands and a scraper | Wet hands, the scraper |

**Safety:** none specific.

**Record:** the first full entry in the bread log.

### BRD-2.M2.L2 — Bulk fermentation

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m2-l2 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 3 h · builds on `BRD-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-BRD→2, JD-CUES→3*

*Objectives:* [S] folds; [J] read the rise, bubbles and feel instead of the clock
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** most of the flavour and strength of a loaf develop in the first rise. Shape it too early and the
loaf is dense; too late and it spreads and tastes sour. The dough shows you when, if you learn to read it.

**You need:** the dough of `BRD-2.M2.L1` in a straight-sided container, marked with tape at its starting level; a
bowl of water for your hand; the probe; a timer; the bread log; for session 2, two more doughs of the same formula.

**Explanation:**

*Bulk fermentation* is the first rise, from mixing to shaping.

*Folds.* Two to four sets of stretch and fold in the first half of the bulk, about every 30 minutes, build strength.
After the last set, leave the dough alone.

*Reading readiness instead of the clock:*
- **the rise:** about 50–75 % for a lean dough with folds, read against the tape in a straight-sided container (mark
  +50 % and +75 % before you start);
- **bubbles** on the surface and at the sides;
- **the feel:** a domed top, airy and jiggly when the container is shaken, pulling away from the sides.

At 24–26 °C this takes about 2–3 hours with 1 % yeast. A cooler dough takes longer; a warmer one, shorter.

*Estimate, then read* (`JD-CUES`). Every time you check:
1. estimate the rise from the look and feel, and write it down;
2. then read the tape and write the reading;
3. before probing the dough, estimate its temperature by touch, then probe.

The log shows your estimates growing more accurate. Within ±10 % for the rise and ±2 °C for the temperature counts as
within tolerance.

**Practice:**
1. **Folds and a marked container (60 min over 3 hours).** Four sets of folds, photographed; the rise estimated, then
   read, every 30 minutes.
2. **Under and over (45 min over a day).** Two more doughs: one shaped at about 25 % rise, one at about 100 %. Bake both
   with a dough shaped at the right point; compare the three crumbs.

**Look for:** a dough that has grown by half to three quarters; a domed top; bubbles at the sides; a jiggle when shaken.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dense loaf, a few big holes | Shaping by the clock | Shape at 50–75 % rise |
| No idea how far it has risen | A bowl with sloping sides, where the rise can't be read | A straight-sided, marked container |
| A sour, slack dough that spreads | A dough left to triple and collapse | Check it every 30 minutes |

**Safety:** none specific.

**Record:** the estimate-then-reading entries and the three crumb photos.

### BRD-2.M2.L3 — Shaping

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m2-l3 -->
*2.5 h · 3 practical sessions · builds on `BRD-2.M2.L2` · moves TS-BRD→2*

*Objectives:* [S] pre-shape, rest, shape a round (*boule*) and an oval (*bâtard*); build tension
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m2-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** shaping gives the loaf a taut skin that holds the gas, so it rises up instead of spreading out.
It takes practice in the hands, which is why this lesson has three sessions.

**You need:** fermented dough (for two loaves per session); a bench scraper; flour and rice flour; two proofing baskets,
or bowls lined with floured tea towels; a shower cap or a bag.

**Explanation:**

*Pre-shaping:*
1. Tip the dough onto a lightly floured surface, floured side down.
2. Divide it with the scraper if needed, by weight.
3. Round each piece loosely: fold the edges in and turn it seam side down.
4. Rest it for 20–30 minutes, uncovered or loosely covered (the bench rest), so the gluten relaxes and it can be
   shaped without tearing.

*Shaping a round (boule):*
1. Flip it floured side down, and fold the edges to the centre.
2. Flip it seam side down.
3. With both hands cupped behind it, drag it towards you in short pulls on an unfloured patch of the bench, turning it
   a little each time. The friction tightens the surface into a smooth skin.

*Shaping an oval (bâtard):*
1. Flatten it gently into a rough rectangle.
2. Fold the top third down and the bottom third up.
3. Roll it tightly from the top, pressing the edge in with your thumbs as you go, and seal the seam with the heel of
   your hand.

*Tension* is the aim: a taut surface holds the gas and helps the loaf rise up rather than out. Too little, and the loaf
spreads; too much, and the skin tears. Use only enough flour to stop sticking, and handle the dough firmly but gently,
without pressing out the gas.

*Into the basket:* seam side up, in a basket or bowl dusted with flour (rice flour sticks least). Cover it, so the skin
doesn't dry.

**Practice:**
1. **Pre-shape and boule (45 min).** Pre-shape and shape two rounds.
2. **Bâtard (45 min).** Pre-shape and shape two ovals.
3. **Tension (40 min).** Shape one loaf loosely and one tightly; proof and bake them side by side; compare the spread.

**Look for:** a smooth, taut skin; a loaf that holds its shape on the bench; a sealed seam.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The dough slides and won't tighten | Too much flour on the bench, so the dough slides and won't tighten | An unfloured patch for the final pulls |
| A dense loaf | Degassing the dough by pressing too hard | Firm but gentle |
| A torn, ragged surface | Torn skin | Less pulling; a longer bench rest |
| Dough that fights back and tears | Skipping the bench rest | 20–30 minutes |

**Safety:** none specific.

**Record:** photos of the shaped loaves on the bench, and of the loose and tight loaves after baking.

### BRD-2.M2.L4 — Proofing

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m2-l4 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BRD-2.M2.L3` · moves TS-BRD→2*

*Objectives:* [S] proof in a basket or bowl; the poke test; retarding in the fridge
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m2-l4 -->

**Why it matters:** the final rise decides whether the loaf springs in the oven or spreads. The poke test reads it in
seconds; the fridge lets you choose when to bake, which is what makes bread fit a working week.

**You need:** two shaped loaves in baskets; a shower cap or bag for each; flour for your finger; the fridge; the oven,
pot and gloves of `BRD-2.M3.L1`; a timer; the bread log.

**Explanation:**

*Proofing* is the final rise of the shaped loaf: at room temperature, about 1–2 hours; or in the fridge, 8–16 hours
(retarding).

*The poke test.* Flour a fingertip and press it about 1 cm into the side of the loaf:
- **springs back at once:** underproofed. Wait, and test again in 15–20 minutes;
- **springs back slowly and partly**, leaving a small dent: ready. Bake;
- **doesn't spring back:** overproofed. Bake at once; it will spread, and the score won't open much.

Press gently: a hard poke reads every dough as overproofed. Before each test, write your prediction (under, ready or
over), then compare it with the result and, after baking, with the crumb.

*Retarding* in the fridge, covered:
- slows the yeast and develops flavour;
- firms the dough, so it scores more cleanly;
- lets the bake fit the day.

The loaf is baked straight from the fridge, after a poke test. A cold dough springs back more slowly, so judge it a
little more generously than a warm one.

*Covered:* a shower cap or a bag over the basket, so the skin doesn't dry, harden and crack.

**Practice:**
1. **Room-temperature proof (40 min over 2 hours).** Poke-test every 20 minutes with a prediction; bake at "ready";
   log the times and the predictions.
2. **A retarded proof (40 min over two days).** Shape in the evening, proof overnight in the fridge; poke-test in the
   morning; bake straight from the fridge.

**Look for:** a slow, partial spring-back, matched against the bake.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dry, cracked skin | An uncovered dough that skins | Cover it |
| Every test reads "over" | Poke-testing too hard | About 1 cm, gently |
| A flat, spread loaf | Waiting for a dough that is already overproofed | Bake at once; test earlier next time |

**Safety:** the raw dough is kept covered in the fridge, apart from ready-to-eat food; the oven safety of
`BRD-2.M3.L1` applies to every bake.

**Record:** the poke-test predictions and results against the two bakes.

### BRD-2.M2.X1 — A lean white loaf, three times

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-brd-2-m2-x1 -->
*guided, recipe* — A lean white loaf, three times over three weeks.
<!-- END generated:exercise-brd-2-m2-x1 -->

**Task:** carry a lean dough through every stage with a guiding recipe, three times over three weeks, reading the dough
instead of the clock. Change one thing per bake, written in advance: the hydration, the shape (*boule* or *bâtard*), or
room proof against retard. Log the times and every estimate, then its reading.

**You need:** per bake: 500 g of bread flour, about 340 g of water, 10 g of salt, 4 g of instant yeast; the container
and tape; the probe; a basket; the casserole; gloves; a blade; the bread log. The loaves are the household's bread.

**Recipe:** a lean loaf (100 % flour, 68 % water, 2 % salt, 0.8 % yeast).
1. **Weigh, mix and probe:** water chosen for a dough of 24–26 °C; estimate, then probe.
2. **Bulk:** four sets of folds in the first two hours; estimate, then read the rise every 30 minutes; shape at 50–75 %.
3. **Pre-shape**, rest 20–30 minutes, and **shape**; into the basket, covered.
4. **Proof** at room temperature, or overnight in the fridge; poke-test with a prediction.
5. **Preheat** the casserole with the lid in the oven at about 250 °C for 45 minutes; check the thermometer.
6. **Score** the loaf on a sheet of baking paper; lower it into the pot by the paper, with dry gloves; lid on.
7. **Bake** 20 minutes with the lid on; then lid off, at about 230 °C, until deep brown, about 20–25 minutes more.
8. **Predict** doneness by colour and sound, then probe the core through the base: at least 93 °C.
9. **Cool** on a rack for at least an hour; cut and photograph the dough at each stage, the crust and the crumb.

**Show:**
- **`TS-BRD` level 2:** a lean loaf from a formula, risen, baked through with the core at least 93 °C, and an even
  crumb, three times.
- **`JD-CUES` level 3, the dough part:** at least ten estimate-then-reading entries over the three bakes, at least eight
  within tolerance (±2 °C for temperatures, ±10 % for the rise).

AI review scores the crumb against the reference images: *competent* is an even crumb without dense patches, risen, with
an "ear" at the score; *not yet* is a dense, gummy crumb or a large hole under the crust, a flat, spread loaf, or a pale
crust. The rubric: risen, even crumb without gummy patches, crust deep golden (each yes or no).

**Time:** 3 × about 1.25 h active over 5 hours.

### BRD-2.M3.L1 — Steam and heat at home

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m3-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `EQP-1.M2.L3` · moves TS-BRD→2, KM-EQPT→3*

*Objectives:* [S] Dutch oven, baking stone, a tray of water; [K] oven spring and crust
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a home oven is dry, and a loaf baked dry stays small and dull. With steam, the same dough springs
and bakes a glossy crust. At these heats, loading the oven safely is part of the skill.

**You need:** two shaped loaves; the casserole of `HEA-2` with a lid and a metal or ovenproof knob; a baking stone or
steel, or a sheet pan; a second sheet pan or tray for water; a kettle; baking paper; two dry, thick oven gloves; the
oven thermometer; the calibration log.

**Explanation:**

*Oven spring.* In the first 10–15 minutes of baking, the gas in the loaf expands and the yeast makes a last burst, until
the crust sets. A loaf can grow by a third.

*Why steam.* Moisture on the surface keeps the crust soft and stretchy, so the loaf can spring fully. It also
gelatinises the surface starch, for a glossy, crackling crust. Without steam, the crust sets early and the loaf stays
small, with a dull, thick crust.

*Three ways at home:*
- **a Dutch oven** (the casserole), preheated with the oven, lid on for the first 20 minutes: the loaf's own moisture
  is the steam. It is the simplest and the most reliable;
- **a baking stone or steel,** preheated for 45 minutes, with a tray of boiling water on the shelf below for the first
  15 minutes;
- **a tray of water** in a plain oven, with the loaf on a preheated sheet pan: the weakest of the three.

*Heat.* A lean loaf bakes at about 230–250 °C, checked with the oven thermometer (`EQP-1`), then lower once the steam is
gone. Log the real temperature and the preheat time of the pot or stone (`KM-EQPT`).

*Loading safely:*
- a preheated pot or stone is about 250 °C: dry oven gloves go on both hands, because a damp glove passes heat straight
  through as steam;
- the loaf goes in on a sheet of baking paper, lowered by its corners, so your hands stay out of the pot;
- lift the lid away from you, so the steam rises on the far side;
- pour boiling water into the preheated tray with your arm clear of the steam;
- water never touches a hot stone, the oven glass or the door, which can crack.

**Practice:**
1. **Dutch oven (50 min over a bake).** Preheat the pot; load the loaf safely; bake lid on, then lid off; photograph the
   spring at the moment the lid comes off and at the end.
2. **Stone or tray (50 min over a bake).** The second loaf with the other method; compare the spring and the crust.

**Look for:** a loaf that grows and opens at the score; a glossy, deep crust; the oven temperature logged against the
dial.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A flat loaf, a pale base | Loading a cold pot | Preheat it with the oven, 45 minutes |
| A cracked oven door or stone | Water poured onto glass | Water only into the preheated tray |
| A small loaf, a closed score | Removing the lid too early | 20 minutes with the lid |
| A burn through the glove | Damp oven gloves | Dry, thick gloves on both hands |

**Safety:** the module's safety items are the loading rules above: dry gloves, paper, the lid away from you, and water
never on hot glass or stone.

**Record:** the oven reading and preheat times in the calibration log; the photos of both loaves.

### BRD-2.M3.L2 — Scoring and baking to colour

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m3-l2 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BRD-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-BRD→2*

*Objectives:* [S] score with a blade; bake to a deep crust
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a cut decides where the loaf opens as it springs, instead of bursting at a weak point; and the
colour of the crust is most of its flavour. Most home bakers score too timidly and pull their loaves too pale.

**You need:** shaped loaves, ideally retarded in the fridge; a razor blade or lame, or a sharp serrated knife; baking
paper; the casserole or stone; dry gloves; the phone.

**Explanation:**

*Why score.* As the loaf springs in the oven, the skin has to give somewhere. A cut controls where it opens, so the loaf
expands evenly and keeps its shape.

*How to score:*
- for an oval, hold the blade at a shallow angle, about 30° to the surface, and make one long cut along the length,
  slightly off-centre: the flap lifts into an "ear";
- for a round, cut straight down: a cross, or a square;
- make each cut one quick, confident stroke, about 0.5–1 cm deep;
- a cold, retarded dough scores most cleanly, because it is firm.

Score just before loading, on the baking paper, and load at once.

*Baking to colour.* Bake the crust to a deep golden brown, with darker edges at the score, past where a beginner would
stop. The colour is flavour (`SC-BRWN`), and a pale crust softens as the loaf cools, because there is still moisture in
it. If the top colours too fast, lower the heat rather than taking the loaf out early.

*Listen.* A well-baked lean loaf crackles as it cools on the rack: the crust is contracting.

**Practice:**
1. **Score (40 min).** On shaped dough, practise single slashes, a cross and a square; bake them and photograph how each
   opened.
2. **Colour (40 min).** Bake two loaves: pull one at light golden, the other at deep brown. Compare the crust after an
   hour, by touch, sound and taste.

**Look for:** a clean, open score with an ear; a deep, even brown crust that crackles as it cools.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A torn, ragged cut that doesn't open | A hesitant, ragged score | One quick stroke |
| A loaf that bursts at the side, or a gaping, flat cut | Scoring too deep or too shallow | About 0.5–1 cm |
| A soft, bland crust | Pulling a pale loaf | Bake to deep brown |

**Safety:** the blade is stored with its cover on. Scoring happens before loading, never over the hot pot.

**Record:** the score photos and the two crusts side by side.

### BRD-2.M3.L3 — Doneness and crumb

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m3-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `BRD-2.M3.L2` · moves JD-CUES→3*

*Objectives:* [J] colour, hollow sound, core temperature; read a crumb for faults
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m3-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a loaf can look done and still be gummy inside. Colour, sound and weight give you a good guess; the
probe confirms it. And once it is cut, the crumb tells you what happened in the bulk and the proof.

**You need:** two loaves at the end of baking; the probe; a wire rack; a bread knife; the bread log with the bakes'
times; the phone.

**Explanation:**

*The cues of a done loaf:*
- a deep crust colour;
- a hollow sound when the base is tapped;
- a loaf that feels light for its size.

Write your prediction (done or not), then confirm it with the probe: the core of a lean loaf is at least 93 °C (the
`TS-BRD` level 2 standard). Probe through the base or the side into the centre, not through the score, where the crust
is thin and the reading is misleading. If it is short, it goes back in the oven for 5 minutes, directly on the shelf.

*Cooling.* The crumb is still setting as steam escapes, so a loaf cools on a rack for at least an hour before cutting. A
loaf cut hot is gummy, however well it was baked.

*Reading a crumb.* Cut through the centre and photograph the face:

| Crumb | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Dense, tight, a few big holes under the crust | Underfermented or underproofed |
| Large irregular holes, a flat loaf that spread | Overproofed, or too little tension |
| Gummy, wet | Underbaked, or cut while hot |
| Even, with a mix of small and medium holes | As intended for a lean white loaf |

*Check the log before you blame anything.* A dense crumb from a log that shows a cold dough and a short bulk points at
the fermentation, not the flour. As in `BAK-2.M4.L3`: one fault, one change next time.

**Practice:**
1. **Predict and probe (30 min).** For two loaves, predict doneness by colour and sound, then probe; cool, cut, and read
   each crumb against the table and the log.

**Look for:** predictions that match the probe; a crumb diagnosis that matches the log.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A misleading reading | Probing through the score | Through the base, into the centre |
| A gummy crumb in a done loaf | Cutting a hot loaf | Cool at least an hour |
| The same fault next time | Blaming the flour without reading the log | Read the times and temperatures |

**Safety:** the loaf is lifted out of the pot with dry gloves; the probe is cleaned after use.

**Record:** the predictions and probe readings (doneness entries for `JD-CUES`), and the crumb diagnoses.

### BRD-2.M4.L1 — A soft enriched dough

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m4-l1 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 h · builds on `BRD-2.M2.L4` · moves TS-BRD→2*

*Objectives:* [S] milk bread or soft rolls; [K] fat and sugar slow fermentation and tenderise the crumb
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** soft rolls and milk bread are everyday Dutch bread (*zachte bolletjes*, *krentenbollen*), and they
behave quite differently from a lean dough. Knowing why lets you adjust the timing and the oven instead of being
surprised by them.

**You need:** the soft-roll formula of `BRD-2.M4.X1`; the mixer (optional); a sheet pan or the loaf tin of `BAK-2`;
baking paper; an egg for the wash and a pastry brush; the probe; the oven thermometer.

**Explanation:**

*Enriched doughs* add fat (butter), sugar, milk and often egg. Examples are milk bread, soft rolls, *krentenbollen* and,
richer still, brioche (in `BRD-3`).

*What the enrichments do:*
- **fat** coats the gluten and tenderises the crumb, giving a soft, fine texture and a soft crust. It gets in the way of
  gluten forming, so it is added after some gluten has developed: knead the dough first, then work in the soft butter a
  piece at a time;
- **sugar** slows the yeast (it draws water away) and browns the crust faster;
- **milk** softens the crumb and colours the crust;
- **egg** adds richness, colour and structure.

*The consequences:*
- a slower rise: more yeast, or more time, and the dough is read by its rise, not the lean dough's timing;
- a lower baking temperature, about 180–190 °C, so the crust doesn't burn before the inside is done;
- a lower core temperature at done: about 88–90 °C.

*Tangzhong* (optional): a little of the flour cooked with some of the milk into a thick paste, cooled and added to the
dough. It holds more water, and keeps the bread soft for days.

*Shaping rolls.* Divide the dough by weight, so every roll bakes in the same time. Round each piece under a cupped hand
on an unfloured surface, moving your hand in small circles until it forms a tight ball. Set them close together on the
pan, so they rise into each other and bake with soft sides. Proof them covered, then egg-wash for a shine.

**Practice:**
1. **Soft rolls (75 min active, about 4 hours in total).** Mix; knead; add the butter once the gluten has started;
   ferment; divide by weight; shape; proof; egg-wash and bake; probe; cool and photograph the crumb.

**Look for:** a smooth, soft, slightly tacky dough; rolls of equal size; a shiny, golden crust; a soft, fine crumb.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dough that stays greasy and won't develop | Butter added at the start, so the dough won't develop | Knead first; butter after |
| Dense, small rolls | A dough fermented on the lean timing | Read the rise; allow more time |
| A dark crust, a doughy inside | A dark crust from a lean bread's oven heat | About 180–190 °C |
| Some rolls dark, some pale | Rolls of uneven weight | Divide by weight |

**Safety:** the egg wash is raw egg: the brush and bowl are washed after use, and the rolls are baked through (core
about 88–90 °C).

**Record:** the dough temperature and times, for comparison with the lean dough.

### BRD-2.M4.X1 — Soft rolls or a milk loaf

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-brd-2-m4-x1 -->
*guided, recipe* — Soft rolls or a milk loaf.
<!-- END generated:exercise-brd-2-m4-x1 -->

**Task:** make a soft enriched bread with a guiding recipe, and compare its behaviour with the lean dough. Choose rolls
(weighed pieces) or a milk loaf in the loaf tin of `BAK-2`. Log the dough temperature and the times, for comparison with
your lean bakes.

**You need:** 500 g of bread flour; 270 g of milk; 50 g of soft butter; 40 g of sugar; an egg, and another for the wash;
7 g of instant yeast; 9 g of salt; a sheet pan or the loaf tin; the probe; the scale.

**Recipe:** soft rolls (12) or a milk loaf. In baker's percentages: flour 100 %, milk 54 %, egg 10 %, butter 10 %,
sugar 8 %, yeast 1.4 %, salt 1.8 %.
1. **Mix** the flour, sugar, yeast and salt; add the lukewarm milk and the egg; mix until no dry flour remains.
2. **Develop:** knead for about 8 minutes, until smooth.
3. **Add the butter** a piece at a time, kneading until each disappears and the dough is smooth and slightly tacky; a
   windowpane should stretch thin. Probe and log the dough.
4. **Ferment** covered until about doubled, logging the rise against the tape; expect it to be slower than the lean
   dough.
5. **Divide** by weight into 12 pieces (about 75 g each), and round them; or shape one loaf as a *bâtard* for the tin.
6. **Proof** covered, close together on the pan or in the tin, until puffy and the poke test is slow.
7. **Egg-wash** and bake at about 180–190 °C until golden: rolls about 15–20 minutes, a loaf longer.
8. **Probe** the core: about 88–90 °C.
9. **Cool** on a rack; cut and photograph the crumb.
10. **Write** three differences from the lean dough, with the reason for each.

**Show:** **`TS-BRD` level 2:** the enriched bread risen and baked through (about 88–90 °C at the core); even rolls or
an even loaf; a soft, fine crumb in cross-section; and the differences from the lean dough explained.

AI review scores the crumb and tray photos: *competent* is even, golden rolls with a soft, fine crumb; *not yet* is
uneven sizes, a dark crust, or a dense or doughy crumb.

**Time:** 1.25 h active, about 4 hours in total.

### BRD-2.M5.L1 — A bread schedule and log

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-2-m5-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 2 wk · builds on `BRD-2.M3.L3` · moves KM-PLAN→3, JD-REFL→3*

*Objectives:* [S] plan mixing, bulk and baking around work using the fridge; [J] review the log and set targets
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-2-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a loaf takes five hours, but only about one of them is work. With the fridge as a pause button,
fresh bread fits around a working day, if you plan backwards and check the dough at the right points.

**You need:** your diary for the coming week; the bread log of the course; the backwards-schedule template
(`WRK-1.M2.L3`); the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The fridge as a pause button.* A dough can be slowed at two points:
- **after mixing:** a cold bulk fermentation;
- **after shaping:** a retarded proof.

So a loaf can be mixed in the evening, shaped the next evening and baked the morning after. Or it can be mixed in the
morning, shaped after work, retarded overnight and baked before work.

*A backwards schedule* (`KM-PLAN`, `WRK-1.M2.L3`):
1. Start from when the bread should come out of the oven.
2. Work back through cooling, baking, preheating, proofing, shaping, bulk and mixing, with a time for each.
3. Mark the control points: the dough temperature after mixing, the rise before shaping, the poke test before baking.
4. Mark the parallel tasks around the working day: dinner cooked during the bulk, for example.

An example for a weekday: mix at 18:00 and cook dinner during the folds; shape at 21:00 at 50–75 % rise; into the
fridge; preheat at 06:15; bake at 07:00 from the fridge; cool while you get ready; bread on the table that evening.

*The plan against the actual.* Each stage gets a planned and an actual time. Where the dough wasn't ready, write the
change and the reason: "bulk 45 minutes longer: dough below 24 °C, kitchen cold". A schedule that ignores the kitchen
temperature will be wrong in winter.

*The bread log review,* as in `BAK-2.M6.L2`. Read the course's bakes for patterns:
- fermentation times against dough temperature;
- crumb faults against proofing;
- your own banding against the AI review and the tasters.

Then set two or three specific targets for `BRD-3`.

**Practice:**
1. **The schedule (45 min).** Write a two-day schedule for a weekday bake, with control points and parallel tasks.
2. **The review (45 min, after the bake).** Set plan against actual; read the log; mark the agreements; write the
   targets.

**Look for:** a schedule that fits around work; every control point with a reading; a review that cites entries and
photos.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The dough isn't ready on time | A schedule that ignores the kitchen temperature | Plan from the logged times at that temperature |
| Surprises at shaping | No control points | Temperature, rise, poke test |
| A review that changes nothing | A review without evidence | Cite entries and photos |

**Safety:** none specific; the oven safety of `BRD-2.M3.L1` applies to an early-morning bake too.

**Record:** the schedule, the plan-against-actual log and the review (in `BRD-2.M5.X1`).

**Before you start.** `HEA-1` taught the basic methods one at a time. This course puts them in a **matrix** (dry, moist
and combination, by medium and temperature) and adds the methods that need more control:
- **roasting** a large piece at two temperatures, with the rest planned;
- **grilling** with radiant heat and two heat zones;
- **deep-frying**, safely and at the right temperature;
- **braising**, to make a tough cut tender.

It ends with a lab that cooks one protein three ways.

*Frying safety comes first.* Deep-frying is the most dangerous thing a home cook does with heat. Module 4 treats the
station as the first skill: the oil level, the thermometer, the lid within reach, and never leaving hot oil. It is
assessed on video, and an unsafe station is *not yet* however good the chips are.

*Choosing a method.* From here, each framework and brief asks you to say why a method suits the ingredient and the goal.
Write the reason and a prediction before you cook; the result then confirms or corrects you.

*The instruments.* Before the roast, the probe is checked in ice water and the oven at three settings; before frying,
the frying thermometer is checked in boiling water. All three go into the calibration log (`EQP-1`).

Plan about four weeks. The roast, the braise and the fried dinners need a weekend slot; the method plan of module 1
runs over two weeks of normal cooking. The roast chicken's carcass goes into stock for `STK-2`.

**Have ready:** the probe and the oven thermometer, and the calibration log (`EQP-1`); a clip-on frying thermometer

### HEA-2.M1.L1 — Classifying methods

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M5.L5` · moves CK-METH→3*

*Objectives:* [K] dry, moist and combination methods by medium, temperature and outcome
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** recipes name dozens of methods, but they reduce to three families with predictable results. Place a
dish in the matrix, and you know what it will taste like, how long it takes and what can go wrong, before you read the
recipe.

**You need:** this lesson; a cookbook or a list of 20 dishes you know; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The matrix:*

| Family | Medium | Methods | Typical outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dry heat | Air, fat, metal or radiation | Roast, bake, grill, sauté, pan-fry, deep-fry | A browned crust; a gradient from surface to core |
| Moist heat | Water or steam | Poach, simmer, boil, steam | No crust; even cooking; gentle at the surface |
| Combination | Fat first, then water | Braise, stew, pot-roast | A browned start, then tenderising in liquid |

Deep-frying belongs to dry heat: the medium is fat, not water, and it browns.

*For each method,* name three things:
- **the medium:** what carries the heat;
- **the temperature range:** a simmer is far below an oven or a frying pot (`HEA-1`);
- **the outcome:** a crust or none; even or a gradient; tenderising or not.

*Surface against core.* The surface of a food in a hot oven or pan gets far hotter than the core, which is why a steak
can be crusted outside and pink inside. In moist heat the surface can't go above the boil, so there is no crust and
little gradient. A grey steak throughout is one cooked in its own juices: moist heat by accident.

*Words that mislead.* A pot roast is not roasting: it is braised in a covered pot. A stew and a braise are cousins:
small pieces fully covered in a stew, large pieces partly covered in a braise (`HEA-2.M5`).

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): place 20 dishes in the matrix (for example *hachee*, *kibbeling*, roast chicken,
poached egg, *stamppot*, grilled mackerel, chips, steamed mussels) and write the medium, the temperature range and the
outcome of each, with the reason in one line.

**Look for:** every dish placed, with the reason in one line.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A pot roast in the dry-heat column | Calling a pot roast "roasting" | Ask: is there liquid, and a lid? |
| Stew and braise swapped | Confusing a stew and a braise | Piece size and liquid level decide |
| Chips under moist heat | Forgetting deep-frying is dry heat | The medium is fat |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the matrix of 20 dishes in the journal.

### HEA-2.M1.L2 — Choosing a method

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m1-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-2.M1.L1` · moves JD-CHSE→2, CK-METH→3*

*Objectives:* [J] choose from tenderness, size, fat and the result wanted; [K] when to combine methods
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a recipe chooses the method for you; a cook chooses it from the ingredient and the result wanted.
Four questions settle most choices, and a written prediction turns every dinner into a test of your reasoning.

**You need:** an ingredient you have not cooked before (in winter Jerusalem artichoke, salsify or kohlrabi; in summer
fennel or broad beans); the pans for two methods; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The analysis questions:*
1. **How tender is it?** A little connective tissue or a lot? Tender cuts and young vegetables suit fast dry heat; tough
   cuts need moist or combination heat.
2. **How large and thick is it?** A thick piece needs gentle heat to cook through before the outside burns; a thin one
   needs high heat to brown before it overcooks.
3. **How much fat does it have?** Fatty pieces (pork belly, mackerel, chicken thighs) survive high heat and long
   cooking; lean ones (breast, white fish) dry out.
4. **What result do I want?** A crust, juiciness, tenderness, a sauce?

*Combining methods* when one alone can't do it:
- sear, then roast, for a thick cut;
- blanch, then grill, for a dense vegetable;
- brown, then braise, for a tough cut.

*Size changes the answer.* A whole celeriac roasts for hours, or is boiled; celeriac in small dice sautés in minutes.

*Write a prediction* before cooking: "roasted in wedges, the artichoke will be crisp outside and creamy inside in about
35 minutes". The result confirms or corrects your reasoning.

**Practice:**
1. **Analyse and cook (75 min).** Answer the four questions for the unfamiliar ingredient, choose between two methods
   (for example roast or sauté Jerusalem artichokes; braise or grill fennel), and write the prediction. Cook it by the
   method you chose, and a small portion by the other. Compare both with the prediction.

**Look for:** a choice reasoned from the ingredient, not from a recipe; a prediction the result confirms.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Everything sautéed | Choosing by habit | Answer the four questions first |
| A burnt outside, hard centre | Ignoring size: a whole celeriac and a celeriac dice need different methods | Ask how thick it is, and choose for that |
| Nothing learned from the dinner | No prediction written | One line before cooking |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the four answers, the prediction and the result (in `HEA-2.M1.X1`).

### HEA-2.M2.L1 — Oven control for roasting

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m2-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `EQP-1.M2.L3`, `HEA-1.M4.L2` · moves TS-DRY→3, KM-EQPT→3*

*Objectives:* [S] high versus low temperature; convection; rack and pan choice
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the oven temperature you choose for a roast decides the inside as much as the outside. A hot oven
gives a fine crust and a grey band under it; a low oven gives an even, pink or juicy interior from edge to edge. Knowing
when to use which is the core of roasting.

**You need:** two equal pieces of pork loin of about 500 g each (or two chicken legs); salt; oil; two sheet pans or
low-sided tins with racks (or a bed of sliced onion and carrot); the probe and the oven thermometer; the oven table
(`EQP-1.M2.X1`); a board and a sharp knife.

**Explanation:**

*Two ways to roast:*

| | High temperature | Low temperature |
|---|---|---|
| Oven | 200–230 °C | Around 120–150 °C |
| Crust | Deep, fast | Little: added at the end with a hot blast or a sear (the "reverse sear") |
| Inside | A steep gradient: a band of overcooked meat under the crust | Even colour edge to edge |
| Suits | Small birds, quick roasts | Large joints |

*Convection* (the fan) moves hot air over the surface: it browns faster and dries the surface. Most oven tables need a
lower setting with the fan.

*Rack and pan.*
- **A rack** (or a bed of vegetables) lets hot air reach the underside, so it doesn't sit in its juice.
- **A low-sided pan** keeps the sides from steaming; a deep one traps steam.
- **The oven table** of `EQP-1` sets the real temperature; the dial may be off by 20 degrees or more.

*Check the oven* with the oven thermometer before a long roast, and enter it in the calibration log (`KM-EQPT`).

*Opening the door* lets the heat out, and the oven takes minutes to recover. Use the probe's cable, or check quickly.

**Practice:**
1. **Two ovens (75 min).** Salt both pieces of pork. Roast one hot, at 230 °C by your oven table, and the other low, at
   120 °C, finishing the low one with 10 minutes at 230 °C or a sear in a hot pan. Take each out when the core is a few
   degrees below 63 °C, rest it, and confirm 63 °C. Slice both across the middle and photograph the cut surfaces side
   by side.

**Look for:** a grey band under the crust on the hot roast; an even colour edge to edge on the low roast.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Over- or undercooked at the planned time | Trusting the dial instead of the oven table | Set the oven from your table |
| A pale, steamed roast | A deep pan that steams the roast | A low-sided pan |
| A soggy underside | No rack | A rack, or a bed of vegetables |
| A roast that takes far longer | Opening the oven door often | Probe with the cable, or check quickly |

**Safety:** pork and other intact meat at 63 °C at the core (assessment §9), confirmed after the rest. Lift the tins out
with dry oven gloves; the fat in them spits.

**Record:** the oven settings, the core readings and the photo of the two cross-sections.

### HEA-2.M2.L2 — Resting and carryover

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m2-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M1.L3` · moves TS-DRY→3, SC-HEAT→3*

*Objectives:* [S] plan resting time; [K] juices, carryover and why resting works
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a roast keeps cooking after it leaves the oven. Plan for it, and you take the meat out at the right
moment and serve it juicy and done; ignore it, and a perfect roast is overcooked on the board, or a chicken that looked
done is not.

**You need:** for session 1, the roast of `HEA-2.M2.X1` and the probe; for session 2, 4 boneless chicken thighs (or two
steaks), salt, oil, the sauté pan, two boards with a rim or two plates, the scale; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Carryover* (`HEA-1.M1.L3`): the hot outer layers keep pushing heat inwards after the oven, so the core rises. How far
depends on:
- **size:** a large roast rises more than a small one;
- **oven temperature:** from a hot oven, 5 °C or more; from a low oven, hardly at all;
- **shape:** a round joint more than a flat one.

*Planning the rest:*
1. Take the roast out below the target and let carryover finish it.
2. Rest it uncovered, or loosely tented with foil; a tight cover steams the crust.
3. Always confirm the safe core temperature after the rest
   ([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)): 75 °C for poultry, 63 °C for intact
   meat such as a pork loin.

If it hasn't reached the figure, it goes back into the oven.

*How long to rest.* About 10 minutes for a chicken or a pork loin, 5 for a steak or thighs. A thin piece rested too long
goes cold; serve it on warm plates.

*Why resting works.* The gradient evens out, and the juices thicken slightly as the meat cools from its peak, so less
runs out on slicing.

**Practice:**
1. **Predict the rise (45 min, within a roast).** During `HEA-2.M2.X1`, write your predicted carryover before the roast
   comes out, then log the core every 5 minutes while it rests until it stops rising.
2. **Rest or no rest (45 min).** Cook the thighs together to 75 °C. Slice two at once on one board, and rest two for 5
   minutes before slicing them on the other. Weigh the juice on each board (weigh the board before and after).

**Look for:** the predicted rise within a few degrees; less juice on the board after resting.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Undercooked poultry served | Taking poultry out early and never checking it after the rest | Probe again after the rest: 75 °C |
| A soft, steamed crust | Covering tightly, which steams the crust | Uncovered, or foil loosely over the top |
| A cold steak | Resting a thin piece for so long that it goes cold | About 5 minutes; warm plates |

**Safety:** the safe core temperature is checked after the rest, not assumed from the carryover (assessment §9).

**Record:** the carryover log with your prediction, and the two juice weights.

### HEA-2.M3.L1 — Radiant heat

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m3-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M1.L1` · moves TS-DRY→3, SC-HEAT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] grill pan, oven grill and barbecue; distance and heat zones; [S] set up two heat zones
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** an oven grill or a barbecue cooks by radiation, and radiation behaves unlike a pan: move the food a
few centimetres and the heat changes completely. Once you can map your grill's zones, you can grill a thin fillet and a
thick slice of celeriac in the same session without burning either.

**You need:** the oven grill (or a barbecue); a sheet pan or the oven rack; 6 slices of bread, or in summer 2 courgettes
in 1 cm slices; oil; long tongs; oven gloves; a timer; a ruler; a phone.

**Explanation:**

*Radiant heat* travels in straight lines from a hot source (the grill element, glowing coals) and falls off sharply with
distance: close to the source is much hotter. Distance is the main control on an oven grill (*grill*) and on a
barbecue.

*A grill pan* works differently: the hot ridges cook by conduction and give the marks; between them, the food cooks by
radiation from the pan.

*Two heat zones:*

| | Hot zone | Cooler zone |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Marks and crust | Finishing thick pieces without burning them |
| Oven grill | The top rack, close to the element | A rack lower down |
| Barbecue | Coals heaped on one side | The side without coals (`FIR-3` goes deeper) |

*Preheating.* An oven grill needs about 5–10 minutes to reach full heat, a grill pan longer, and a barbecue until the
coals are covered in grey ash. A cold grill steams the food.

*Fat and flare-ups.* Fat dripping onto coals or an element flares up and leaves soot on the food. Trim excess fat, and
move the food to the cooler zone until the flames die down.

**Practice:**
1. **Map the grill (60 min).** Preheat the oven grill. Put a slice of bread (or courgette) on the top rack, one in the
   middle and one at the bottom, and time how long each takes to brown, measuring the distance from the element with the
   ruler. Photograph them side by side. Repeat once with the door open if your oven grills that way.

**Look for:** a predictable difference between the zones; you know how far from the element to grill a thick and a thin
piece.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Thin pieces burnt, thick ones raw | Everything at one distance | Hot zone for thin, cooler zone to finish thick |
| Pale, steamed food | The grill not preheated | 5–10 minutes first; coals ashed over |
| Soot and a bitter taste | Ignoring a flare-up | Trim fat; move to the cooler zone |

**Safety:** an oven grill heats the door, the rack and the tray: oven gloves and long tongs. A flare-up is moved away
from, not doused with water (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the distances and times for each zone, in the journal.

### HEA-2.M3.L2 — Grilling vegetables and fish

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-VEG→4, IN-VEG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] oiling, preheating, marks, turning; [J] which vegetables and fish suit the grill
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** grilling gives vegetables and oily fish a smoky, charred flavour that no pan can, but it punishes
mistakes: food that sticks and tears, a raw centre under black marks. A short sequence, followed every time, prevents
most of them.

**You need:** for session 1, three seasonal vegetables: in winter chicory, leek and celeriac; in summer courgette, a
pepper and fennel; a pan of salted water for blanching; for session 2, 2 whole mackerel or 4 fillets with skin; oil;
salt; lemon; the grill or a grill pan; tongs; a fish spatula; the probe.

**Explanation:**

*The sequence:*
1. **Oil the food, not the grill:** a thin coat, so it doesn't stick or smoke.
2. **Preheat thoroughly.**
3. **Lay it down and leave it** until it releases; it sticks until a crust has formed.
4. **Turn once** for marks.
5. **Finish in the cooler zone** if it is thick.

*Vegetables that suit the grill:*
- **firm and juicy:** courgette, peppers, leeks, spring onions, fennel, chicory, aubergine in summer;
- **dense roots** (celeriac, beetroot, carrot, salsify) are blanched first, because the outside would char before the
  inside is done;
- pat everything dry: wet vegetables steam and don't mark.

What heat does to colour and texture (`VEG-1.M3`) decides the choice: a green vegetable grilled long turns olive.

*Fish that suits the grill:* oily and firm (mackerel, herring, salmon), or a whole fish with the skin on. The skin
protects the flesh and crisps. Delicate white fillets need a grill basket or the pan. Probe the thickest part to 63 °C.

*Leeks and chicory.* Leeks are blanched for a few minutes, then grilled until marked; chicory is halved lengthwise and
grilled cut side first. Both get a little oil, salt and lemon.

**Practice:**
1. **Grilled vegetables (60 min).** Grill the three vegetables; blanch the densest (celeriac, or fennel in summer) in
   slices for about 4 minutes first, and grill one slice unblanched beside it. Photograph the marks and the
   cross-sections.
2. **Grilled fish (60 min).** Oil and salt the mackerel, grill it skin side to the heat first, turn once, and probe it
   to 63 °C.

**Look for:** clean dark marks without black soot; the fish lifting off cleanly; the vegetables tender inside.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pale, steaming vegetables | Wet vegetables | Pat dry, then oil |
| Torn food stuck to the grill | Turning too early, which tears the food | Leave it until it releases |
| Black outside, raw inside | A raw centre in a thick slice of celeriac | Blanch dense roots first |
| A fillet in pieces | Delicate fish falling through the bars | A grill basket, or the pan |

**Safety:** fish at 63 °C at the thickest part (assessment §9). Keep the raw fish's plate apart from the cooked fish
(`SAF-1.M2`).

**Record:** the photos, and a line on the blanched against the raw-grilled slice.

## HEA-2.M4 — Frying

### HEA-2.M4.L1 — Oil, temperature and safety

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m4-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAF-1.M4.L2`, `SCI-2.M4.L1` · moves SA-PHYS→4, KM-EQPT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] oils for frying, smoke point, reuse; [S] set up a safe frying station; never leave hot oil; fat-fire response
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a pot of hot oil is the most dangerous thing in a home kitchen. Fat fires and burns from spilt oil
happen in seconds, almost always because of one missing step. A safe station, set up the same way every time, makes
deep-frying routine.

**You need:** a deep, heavy pot (the stockpot); 1.5–2 litres of refined oil (rapeseed or sunflower); the frying
thermometer; a lid that fits the pot; the fire blanket; a spider or slotted spoon; a wire rack over a sheet pan; a slice
of bread; a phone on a stand.

**Explanation:**

*Oils for frying:* refined, neutral oils with a high smoke point: rapeseed, sunflower, or groundnut where nobody has a
peanut allergy. Butter and extra virgin olive oil smoke too early (`SCI-2.M4.L1`). Used oil darkens, foams and smokes
earlier each time. Cool it, strain it, keep it covered, reuse it once or twice for the same kind of food, and discard it
when it is dark or smells. Cooled used oil goes back into its bottle and to a collection point (many supermarkets and
the *milieustraat* take it), never down the sink.

*The safe frying station:*

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| A deep, heavy pot on a back burner, handle turned in | Stable, out of reach, hard to knock |
| Oil no more than a third of the pot | Room for the oil to rise and bubble when food goes in |
| The frying thermometer clipped on | The temperature is known, not guessed |
| A lid and the fire blanket within reach | For a fire |
| A spider or slotted spoon; a rack over a tray | Food in and out without splashing |
| Dry food, lowered away from the body | Water makes oil spit and foam |
| Nobody and no water nearby | No knocks, no splashes |
| Never leave hot oil; heat off before it smokes | Smoke comes before fire |

*Fat-fire response* (`SAF-1.M4.L2`): turn the heat off; slide the lid or the fire blanket over the pot; leave it until
it is completely cold. Never water, never move the pot.

*The frying thermometer* is checked in boiling water, where it reads about 100 °C, and logged in the calibration log.

**Practice:**
1. **Set up and film (45 min).** Set up the station and film it, reading the checklist aloud and pointing to each item.
   Heat the oil to 170 °C, watching the thermometer, and fry a cube of bread as a test.

**Look for:** every checklist item visible on the video; the oil holding 170 °C; the bread browning in about a minute.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Oil foaming over the rim | A pot more than half full | A third full at most |
| A pot within reach of a sleeve or a child | A front burner with the handle out | A back burner, handle turned in |
| Violent spitting | Wet chips into hot oil | Dry them well first |
| A fire with nothing to smother it | No lid at hand | Lid and blanket beside the pot before heating |

**Safety:** this lesson is the safety rule: the station checklist, never leaving hot oil, and a lid or the fire blanket
for a fat fire, never water (`SAF-1.M4.L2`).

**Record:** the video, and the frying thermometer's check in the calibration log.

### HEA-2.M4.L2 — Batters and breadings

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m4-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-2.M4.L1` · moves TS-DRY→3, SC-BRWN→3*

*Objectives:* [S] the standard breading (flour, egg, crumbs); a light batter; [K] why cold batter and hot oil give crispness
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a crisp, golden coating keeps fried food juicy inside and makes it a pleasure to eat: think of
*kibbeling* or a good schnitzel. A greasy, pale or patchy coating comes from a few avoidable mistakes in the coating and
in the oil temperature.

**You need:** 400 g of white fish (saithe, *koolvis*, or pollock) or thick slices of celeriac; for the breading, 50 g of
flour, 2 eggs, 100 g of breadcrumbs (*paneermeel*) or panko; for the batter, 100 g of flour, 25 g of corn starch, about
150 ml of very cold sparkling water or beer, salt; three shallow dishes; a whisk; the frying station; kitchen paper.

**Explanation:**

*The standard breading* (*paneren*):
1. **Flour** (dry): a thin, even coat, tapped off.
2. **Egg** (glue): beaten, the excess dripped off.
3. **Crumbs** (crust): pressed on.

Use one hand for the dry dishes and the other for the egg, so your fingers don't clump into "club hands". Rest the
breaded pieces for 10 minutes on a rack so the coating sets.

*A light batter:* flour, a little starch, and cold sparkling water or beer, barely mixed, with lumps left. Cold batter
in hot oil sets instantly, with little gluten (`SCI-2.M3.L2`), so it is crisp and light. Warm, overmixed batter is heavy
and greasy. Mix it just before frying, and keep it cold.

*Temperature and colour:*

| Oil | Result |
|---|---|
| 170–180 °C | The coating browns as the inside cooks: golden and crisp |
| Too cool (150 °C) | The coating soaks up oil before it sets: pale and greasy |
| Too hot | The outside burns before the inside is done |

*Small batches.* Every piece you add cools the oil. Fry a few at a time, and let the oil come back to temperature before
the next batch.

**Practice:**
1. **Breaded and battered (75 min).** Bread half the fish or celeriac and batter the rest. Fry in small batches at
   175 °C until golden, and drain on the rack. Fry one battered piece at 150 °C for comparison. Probe a piece of fish to
   63 °C. Photograph the crusts and a cut piece.

**Look for:** an even golden crust that stays crisp; the low-temperature batch pale and greasy.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Patchy breading, crumb-caked fingers | Clumped "club hands" | One dry hand, one wet hand |
| A heavy, bready coating | Batter mixed smooth like a cake | Barely mix; lumps are fine |
| Greasy, pale pieces | Overloading the pot so the oil temperature crashes | Small batches; wait for the oil to recover |

**Safety:** lower each piece into the oil away from you with the spider; the frying station rules hold throughout
(`HEA-2.M4.L1`). Fish at 63 °C at the thickest part (assessment §9).

**Record:** the oil temperatures and the photos.

### HEA-2.M4.L3 — Frying vegetables and potatoes

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m4-l3 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-2.M4.L1` · moves TS-VEG→4, TS-DRY→3*

*Objectives:* [S] double-fried chips, vegetable fritters; [J] drain, season, hold crisp
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m4-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** proper Dutch *friet* is fried twice, and once you understand why, you can make chips at home that
are crisp outside and fluffy inside. The same logic, of cooking the inside and crisping the outside, gives vegetable
fritters that are golden and cooked through.

**You need:** for session 1, the inputs of `HEA-2.M4.X1`; for session 2, 400 g of vegetables (in winter celeriac and
onion, grated; in summer courgette, grated and squeezed), the batter of `HEA-2.M4.L2`, salt; the frying station; a
clean tea towel.

**Explanation:**

*Double-fried chips* (*friet*):
1. **Floury potatoes** (*kruimig*, `VEG-1.M5.L1`), peeled and cut into even sticks about 1 cm thick.
2. **Rinsed** in cold water to remove surface starch, then dried very well in a tea towel.
3. **First fry** at about 150 °C, in batches, until soft but still pale: about 5–6 minutes.
4. **Rest** on the rack, at least 30 minutes; they can wait for hours, covered, in the fridge.
5. **Second fry** at about 180 °C, in small batches, until crisp and golden: about 2–3 minutes.

The first fry cooks the inside; the second makes the crust.

*Vegetable fritters.* Grated or thinly sliced vegetables in a light batter, dropped by the spoonful: small and flat, so
they cook through before they brown. Squeeze watery vegetables (courgette) dry first. Fry at 170–180 °C.

*Holding crisp:*
- drain on a rack, not stacked on paper, where steam softens them;
- salt straight away, while the surface is still oily;
- serve quickly, or hold briefly in a low oven on a rack.

**Practice:**
1. **Chips (75 min).** Make the double-fried chips of `HEA-2.M4.X1`, with the oil temperature logged.
2. **Fritters (60 min).** Mix the grated vegetables with just enough batter to bind them, fry spoonfuls at 175 °C,
   turning once, and drain on the rack. Salt and taste.

**Look for:** chips crisp outside and fluffy inside; fritters golden and cooked through.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Burnt outside, raw inside | One fry at high heat | Two fries: low, then hot |
| Spitting oil, soft chips | Wet potatoes | Dry them well in a tea towel |
| Soggy chips | Chips piled on paper until soggy | A rack, in one layer |

**Safety:** potatoes go into the oil dry and in small batches, lowered with the spider; the pot never more than a third
full (`HEA-2.M4.L1`). When you finish, turn the heat off and leave the oil to cool completely in place.

**Record:** the oil log and photos (in `HEA-2.M4.X1`), and a photo of the fritters.

### HEA-2.M5.L1 — Braise versus stew

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m5-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-2.M1.L1` · moves TS-CMB→2, CK-METH→3*

*Objectives:* [K] cut size, liquid level, lid, low oven; why tough cuts need time
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the cheapest, toughest cuts make the best braises and stews, and the Dutch kitchen is full of them:
*hachee*, *draadjesvlees*, *stoofvlees*. Their secret is not a recipe but a principle: long, gentle, moist heat that
turns connective tissue into gelatin.

**You need:** this lesson; a list of six cuts and vegetables (for example beef shin, chicken legs, pork shoulder,
chicken breast, leeks, red cabbage); the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Braise and stew:*

| | Braise | Stew (*stoofpot*) |
|---|---|---|
| Pieces | Large: legs, a shank, a whole joint, halved leeks | Small: cubes |
| Browning | Yes, well | Usually yes |
| Liquid | Partway up the pieces | Fully covering |
| Lid | Yes | Yes |
| Heat | Gentle: a low oven (about 150 °C) or the lowest hob setting | The same |

*Why tough cuts need time.* Collagen turns to gelatin only after long cooking at a gentle simmer (`SCI-2.M2.L1`). A boil
tightens the muscle fibres and squeezes out their juice, so the meat turns stringy and dry even in liquid. The liquid
should barely tremble: a bubble now and then. A low oven does this most reliably, because it heats the pot from all
sides.

*Which cuts.* Cuts that work hard are rich in collagen: shin, cheek, shoulder, chicken legs. They suit braising. Lean,
tender cuts (breast, loin, tenderloin) have little collagen: braised for hours, they only dry out.

*The Dutch oven* (*braadpan*, tier 1): heavy cast iron or enamelled cast iron holds heat evenly, and its tight lid traps
steam, so little liquid is lost.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): for six cuts and vegetables, decide braise, stew or neither, and write the piece
size, the liquid level and the oven temperature for each, with the reason. Plan the braise of `HEA-2.M5.L2` with the
same table.

**Look for:** the choice and the liquid level reasoned from cut size and toughness.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stringy, dry meat in liquid | Boiling a stew | A bare tremble; a low oven |
| Boiled, not braised | Too much liquid in a braise, which then boils | Partway up the pieces |
| Dry, chalky breast | Lean, tender cuts braised for hours until dry | Collagen-rich cuts only |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the table of six, and the braise plan.

### HEA-2.M5.L2 — A first braise

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-2-m5-l2 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 3 h · builds on `HEA-2.M5.L1` · moves TS-CMB→2*

*Objectives:* [S] brown, deglaze, braise chicken legs and vegetables; [J] judge tenderness
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-2-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a braise brings together browning, deglazing and slow moist heat in one pot, and gives a dish with
tender meat and its own sauce. Chicken legs are the ideal first braise: forgiving, cheap, and ready in under two hours.

**You need:** the inputs of `HEA-2.M5.X1`: 4 chicken legs, 3 leeks, stock or white wine, butter, thyme; salt and pepper;
the *braadpan*; tongs; a wooden spoon; a ladle and a small bowl for skimming; the probe; a fork.

**Explanation:**

*The steps:*
1. **Dry and season** the meat: salt it an hour ahead, or the day before (`SCI-2.M2.L2`).
2. **Brown it well in batches,** skin side first, in a little fat over medium-high heat, until deep golden. A crowded
   pot steams.
3. **Soften the aromatics** (the leeks) in the fat left behind.
4. **Deglaze the fond** with the wine or stock, scraping the base (`HEA-1.M3`).
5. **Add liquid partway up** the legs, skin side up and above the liquid.
6. **Cover and cook gently** in the low oven until tender.
7. **Reduce the sauce:** lift out the meat, skim the fat, and boil the liquid until it coats a spoon and shines.

*Judging tenderness:* a fork slides in and the meat gives with almost no resistance; it holds together and is not
stringy. With chicken legs, the meat starts to pull from the bone. Poultry passes 75 °C long before it is tender:
tenderness, not the core figure, says when a braise is done.

*The sauce.* Skim the fat with a ladle or spoon. Reduce until it coats a spoon and shines; finish it with a knob of cold
butter off the heat, and taste it for salt and acid.

**Practice:**
1. **A braise (3 h active, spread over the cooking).** Braise the chicken legs with leeks in the *braadpan* at about
   150 °C. Test the meat for tenderness with a fork every 15 minutes from 45 minutes, and note when it is done.

**Look for:** deep browning before the liquid goes in; a liquid that barely trembles; tender meat and a glossy, reduced
sauce.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A pale, flat braise | Pale meat | Brown it deep golden first |
| Grey meat in its own juice | A crowded pan that steams | Batches |
| A thin, dull sauce | Fond left in the pan | Deglaze and scrape |
| A greasy, watery sauce | A thin, greasy sauce | Skim, then reduce |

**Safety:** the *braadpan* and its lid are heavy and hot from the oven: oven gloves, and a cloth on the lid handle as a
reminder. Lift the lid away from you. Raw chicken has its own board; wash hands and tongs after (`SAF-1.M2`).

**Record:** the times, the tenderness tests and the photos (in `HEA-2.M5.X1`).

**Why it matters:** "what is the best way to cook chicken thighs?" has no answer. "What is the best way for crisp skin

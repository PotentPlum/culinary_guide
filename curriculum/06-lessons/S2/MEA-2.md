**Before you start.** Meat rewards understanding more than any other ingredient. A cut's place on the animal says
almost everything about how to cook it:
- **muscles that worked hard** (shoulder, leg, neck) have more connective tissue and flavour, and need long, moist heat;
- **muscles that rested** (loin, fillet) are tender and lean, and need a short, hot cook and a precise doneness.

Most failures at home are a mismatch between cut and method, or a doneness missed by a few degrees. The course builds
the understanding first (meat science; the cuts of beef, pork and lamb with their Dutch names; buying), then applies it
to the four methods a home cook uses most: steaks and chops, roast joints, minced meat, and a first stew.

*Doneness is chosen, not guessed.* You state a target before cooking and are judged against it after resting. The safe
figures come from [assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment):

| Meat | Safe figure |
|---|---|
| Pork, lamb and other intact meat | 63 °C for 15 seconds at the core |
| Minced meat | 75 °C at the core |
| Whole roasts | The time-temperature table of §9 |
| Intact beef steaks | May be served rare when seared on both faces, with the colour changed on every outside surface |

Nothing rare or pink below those figures is served to a guest in a risk group (pregnant people, young children, older
people, people with weakened immunity). A reading below the figure after the rest means the meat goes back to cook
further.

*Raw meat* uses the station of `POU-2.M1.L2`: a separate board, raw protein last in the work order, hands washed after
every raw touch, and a full clean-down. Mince gets extra care (module 5).

*Cost.* Meat is the most expensive ingredient of S2. Good options are given everywhere (*kogelbiefstuk* rather than
*entrecote*, chuck rather than cheek), and every exercise is a household meal.

Plan about five weeks. Steaks, chops and burgers fit weeknights. The joints and the stews are weekend cooking, and the
stews freeze.

**Have ready:** the cast-iron or carbon-steel skillet; the roasting tin; the heavy lidded casserole (*braadpan*); the
probe, checked in boiling water; the chef's knife or a slicing knife and a carving fork; a large board on a damp cloth
and the raw board; kitchen string; shallow containers with lids; the scale; kitchen paper; the phone and phone stand;
the tasting form and the comparison template; the prediction log from `POU-2.M6.L2`; the journal.

### MEA-2.M1.L1 — Muscle, collagen and fat

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M2.L1` · moves IN-MEA→2, SC-PROT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] fibres, connective tissue and marbling; working versus resting muscles; tenderness
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** once you know why a shin is tough and a fillet tender, you never need a chart again: you can look at
any cut, say how it will behave, and choose its method.

**You need:** this lesson; photos of twelve cuts (from a butcher's or a supermarket's website, or your own from the
shop): fillet, ribeye, rump, topside, chuck, brisket, shin, pork loin, pork shoulder, pork belly, lamb leg, lamb neck;
the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Muscle fibres* are bundles of protein wrapped in connective tissue. Tenderness depends on three things:
- **fibre thickness:** fine-grained cuts are more tender;
- **the amount and kind of connective tissue:** collagen softens to gelatin with long, moist heat; elastin (the thin,
  shiny silverskin) never softens and is trimmed;
- **how far the fibres contract** with heat (`SCI-2.M2.L1`).

*Working and resting muscles:*
- **resting muscles** along the back (loin, fillet, ribeye) are tender: quick, dry heat;
- **working muscles** (shoulder, leg, shank, neck, brisket) have more collagen and more flavour: long, moist heat, or
  mincing, or thin slicing across the grain.

*Fat:*
- **marbling** (fat within the muscle) adds juiciness and flavour;
- **the fat cover** on the outside protects a roast;
- **inter-muscular fat** runs between the muscles of shoulder and neck cuts, and melts in a stew.

*The grain* is the direction of the fibres, visible as fine lines on the cut surface. Slicing across it shortens them,
so a firm cut eats more tenderly.

*Predicting a cut.* From its place on the animal, its grain, its marbling and its connective tissue, say how it will
behave and which family of method suits it (`CK-METH`):

| Look | Tells you | Method family |
|---|---|---|
| Fine grain, little connective tissue | A resting muscle | Dry heat, short |
| Coarse grain, visible sinew and collagen | A working muscle | Moist heat, long |
| Fine marbling | Juicy at medium-rare | Dry heat |
| Lean, firm, fine | Tender only if thin and sliced across | Quick sear, or roast and slice thin |

**Practice:** theory only (2 h): sort the photos of the twelve cuts by tenderness and method, with a line of reasoning
for each.

**Look for:** for any cut on the counter, you can say whether it worked or rested, what will happen to it in a hot pan,
and what will happen in a stew.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A tough "lean" steak | Treating every lean cut as tender | Ask whether it worked; slice thin |
| A shoe-leather shin | Searing a shin as a steak | Shin is for braising |
| A dry, grainy stew | Stewing a fillet | Fillet is for a quick sear |
| Chewy slices | Slicing with the grain | Find the lines; cut across them |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the sorted cuts and their reasons.

### MEA-2.M1.L2 — Colour and doneness

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m1-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `MEA-2.M1.L1` · moves SC-PROT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] myoglobin, colour by temperature, the doneness scale; [S] read a cross-section
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** the colour of a cross-section is a thermometer reading you can see. Learning to read it, and
knowing where the safety line is, lets you cook to a doneness you chose, not one you ended up with.

**You need:** the five pieces of `MEA-2.M1.X1`, cooked; a board by a window in daylight; the phone; the readings of
`MEA-2.M1.X1`.

**Explanation:**

*Myoglobin* makes meat red. It is more plentiful in working muscles and in older animals. With heat it changes from red
to pink to grey-brown, so the colour of a cross-section shows the temperature it reached.

*The doneness scale for beef* (the core after resting; a culinary target, not a safety figure):

| Doneness | Core | Cross-section |
|---|---|---|
| Rare | About 50–52 °C | Deep red, cool to warm centre |
| Medium-rare | About 55–57 °C | Red-pink, warm |
| Medium | About 60–63 °C | Pink |
| Medium-well | About 65–68 °C | Faint pink |
| Well done | 70 °C or more | Grey-brown throughout |

*Safety and doneness* (assessment §9):
- an intact, whole-muscle beef steak may be served rare once it is seared on top and bottom with the colour changed on
  every outside surface, because the bacteria are on the outside;
- pork, lamb and other intact meat reach 63 °C for 15 seconds;
- minced meat reaches 75 °C;
- nothing rare is served to risk groups.

*Juiciness against doneness.* The hotter the core, the more the fibres contract and the more juice is lost. You will
weigh it in `MEA-2.M1.X1` (`SCI-2.M2.L1`).

*Reading a cross-section.* Look for an even colour from the edge to the centre, against a grey band under the crust
(`HEA-2.M1`). Cut through the thickest part and photograph it in daylight: kitchen light is yellow and makes pink look
grey.

**Practice:**
1. **Read the cross-sections (45 min).** Photograph the five pieces of `MEA-2.M1.X1` side by side; match each to its
   reading, and name it on the scale. Then have a helper show you the photos in a random order, and name each.

**Look for:** you name the doneness of a cross-section photo within one step of the scale.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Medium-rare read as medium | Judging colour under yellow kitchen light | Daylight, by a window |
| A misleading section | Cutting at the thin end | Cut through the thickest part |
| An overcooked edge praised | Calling a grey band "medium" | Judge the centre and the band separately |

**Safety:** a rare beef target is for intact steaks only, seared on every outside surface, and never for a guest in a
risk group (assessment §9).

**Record:** the row of five photos, labelled with the readings: your reference set for `MEA-2.M3`.

### MEA-2.M1.X1 — One steak at five temperatures

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-mea-2-m1-x1 -->
*comparison, recipe* — Thin slices of the same steak cooked to five temperatures; photograph and taste.
<!-- END generated:exercise-mea-2-m1-x1 -->

**Task:** see, taste and weigh what doneness does to the same piece of meat: one thick steak cut into five equal pieces,
each cooked to a different core, weighed before and after, sliced thin and tasted by the household on the form.

**You need:** for 4, as a tasting with a salad and bread: one thick steak of 500–600 g (rump, *kogelbiefstuk*, or a
sirloin of about 3 cm); salt; a little high-smoke-point oil; the skillet; a baking tray with a rack; the probe; the
scale; five labelled plates; the tasting form.

**Recipe:** the five pieces.
1. **Cut** the steak into five equal pieces, label them 50, 55, 60, 65 and 70, and weigh each. Pat dry and salt.
2. **Heat the oven** to a moderate setting (the moderate setting of your oven table).
3. **Sear** all five in the very hot skillet, a minute or two on each face, then briefly on every cut edge, so every
   outside surface changes colour (assessment §9).
4. **Finish** them on the rack in the oven. Probe each from the side into the centre, and take each out a degree or
   two below its label: the 50 piece just under 50 °C, and so on to the 70 piece just under 70 °C. Carryover finishes
   it.
5. **Rest** each piece for 5 minutes on its labelled plate, probe again, and weigh it.
6. **Slice** each thinly across the grain, and photograph the five cross-sections in a row in daylight.
7. **Taste** them with the household on the form: juiciness, tenderness, flavour.
8. **Calculate** the cooking loss of each: (weight before − weight after) ÷ weight before × 100.
9. **Explain** the trend in three sentences.

The rare and medium-rare pieces are for people outside the risk groups.

**Show:**
- **`SC-PROT` level 3, the doneness part:** five pieces weighed before and after cooking, with the loss in % against the
  core, the trend explained, and this check at 80 %. The brining comparison was shown in `SCI-2.M2.X1` and
  `POU-2.M6.X1`.
- **The reference set:** the five cross-section photos become your reference for `MEA-2.M3`.

**Time:** 1 h.

### MEA-2.M2.L1 — Beef cuts

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M1.L1` · moves IN-MEA→3*

*Objectives:* [K] the primal areas and the main cuts; tender versus tough; cut to method; Dutch names
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a Dutch butcher's counter has its own names, and they don't translate neatly into English or French
recipes. Knowing which cut is which, and what each wants, saves money and failures.

**You need:** this lesson; an index card or a page of the journal for the cut-to-method card; a diagram of a beef
carcass (any butcher's chart).

**Explanation:**

*The primal areas:* neck and chuck, rib, loin, round (the hind leg), brisket and plate, shank.

*The cuts a Dutch shop sells,* by method:

| Cut (NL) | Area | Character | Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Ossenhaas* | Fillet | The most tender, lean, mild | Sear and roast, short |
| *Entrecote*, *ribeye* | Loin, rib | Tender, marbled, a fat edge | Sear, grill |
| *Rosbief* | Loin or round | Lean, fine-grained | Roast, then slice thin |
| *Kogelbiefstuk*, *biefstuk* | Round | Lean, firmer | Sear quickly, slice across the grain |
| *Sukadelappen*, *runderlappen* | Chuck, shoulder | Collagen-rich | Stew, braise (*stoofvlees*) |
| *Klapstuk*, *riblappen* | Brisket, plate | Fatty, collagen-rich | Braise, stew |
| *Schenkel* | Shank | Collagen and marrow bone | Braise, stock |
| *Rundergehakt* | Trim | Mixed | `MEA-2.M5` |

*Tender against tough* is a scale, not two boxes. A *kogelbiefstuk* is tender enough for a quick sear only if it is cut
thin and sliced across the grain. An *ossenhaas* is tender at any thickness but has little flavour of its own and dries
quickly past medium: it suits a sauce.

*Cut to method.* For an unfamiliar cut, ask the questions of `MEA-2.M1.L1`: where on the animal? Worked or rested?
Grain, marbling, sinew? The answers predict the method. A *bavette* (flank) is a working muscle with a coarse grain but
little collagen: a quick sear to medium-rare, sliced thin across the grain.

*Making the card.* Build it in your own words, one line per cut, with the Dutch name, the area, the character, a method
and a reason. You will extend it to pork and lamb in `MEA-2.M2.L2`, and it is used from now on whenever you buy meat.

**Practice:** theory only (2 h): build the cut-to-method card for beef, with the Dutch names, and add two cuts you have
seen in your own shop that are not in the table, with their predicted methods.

**Look for:** for each cut on the card, you can give its area, its character and a method, with a reason.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Tough, chewy "steaks" | *Sukadelappen* fried as steaks | Stew them |
| A dry, expensive fillet | *Ossenhaas* cooked past medium | Pull it early; rest it |
| Chewy slices of a lean roast | *Rosbief* sliced thick and with the grain | Thin, across the grain |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the beef card.

### MEA-2.M2.L2 — Pork and lamb cuts

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M1.L1` · moves IN-MEA→3*

*Objectives:* [K] the main cuts and their methods; Dutch names
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** pork is cheap, and most of it is overcooked out of habit. Lamb is seasonal and expensive. Knowing
their cuts and their safe figures makes both better and cheaper.

**You need:** the cut-to-method card from `MEA-2.M2.L1`; this lesson; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Pork:*

| Cut (NL) | Area | Method |
|---|---|---|
| *Varkenshaas* | Tenderloin | Sear and roast to 63 °C |
| *Karbonade*, *haaskarbonade* | Loin chop | Sear, pan-roast |
| *Fricandeau* | Leg | Roast, slice thin |
| *Procureur*, *schouder* | Shoulder and neck | Roast slowly, stew, pull |
| *Buikspek*, *speklap* | Belly | Roast slowly, braise, crisp |

*Lamb,* mostly Dutch spring and summer lamb, from the Texel or salt-marsh flocks:

| Cut (NL) | Area | Method |
|---|---|---|
| *Lamsrack*, *lamskoteletten* | Rib | Sear, roast to 63 °C |
| *Lamsbout* | Leg | Roast |
| *Lamsschouder*, *lamsnek* | Shoulder and neck | Braise, stew |

*The shoulders share a method.* Beef chuck, pork *procureur* and lamb shoulder are all working muscles rich in collagen
and fat between the muscles: all three want long, moist heat, or slow roasting.

*Pork today is lean.* A loin chop overcooked past about 70 °C dries out. It is cooked to the safe figure (63 °C for 15
seconds, assessment §9) and rested; a trace of pink is normal at that temperature.

*Lamb* is cooked to the same figure, and served pink, not red.

*Extend the card.* Add the pork and lamb rows to your cut-to-method card, and mark the three shoulder cuts.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): extend the cut-to-method card to pork and lamb, and write two lines on why the
shoulders of all three animals share a method.

**Look for:** you name the shoulder cuts of all three animals and say why they share a method.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dry, stringy pork | Cooking pork until grey "to be safe" | 63 °C after the rest, probed |
| Chewy lamb | Roasting lamb neck fast | Neck is for braising |
| A dry "chop" from the shoulder | Confusing *procureur* with *karbonade* | Shoulder slow; loin quick |

**Safety:** pork and lamb reach 63 °C for 15 seconds at the core, probed after the rest (assessment §9).

**Record:** the extended card.

### MEA-2.M2.L3 — Buying meat

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `MEA-2.M2.L1` · moves KM-PURC→2, IN-SEL→3*

*Objectives:* [J] butcher versus supermarket, labels, grading, ageing, price per portion (Good/Best)
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m2-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** the same money buys a dull supermarket steak or a stew and a weeknight dinner from a butcher who
tells you how to cook them. Buying meat well means asking, reading labels, and comparing the price per portion.

**You need:** the cut-to-method card; the questions of `MEA-2.M2.X1`, written; a nearby butcher (*slager*); the phone.

**Explanation:**

*Butcher or supermarket:*
- **a butcher** cuts to order, trims, ties, gives advice, and sells the less common cuts (cheek, *klapstuk*, bones);
- **the supermarket** is cheaper per kilo for standard cuts and reliable for mince and chops.

*Labels:*
- ***Beter Leven*** stars;
- **organic** (*biologisch*);
- **origin:** the country of birth, rearing and slaughter;
- ***weidevlees*** (pasture-raised);
- **the use-by date** (*te gebruiken tot*), a safety date.

*Quality signs:*
- colour: bright, not grey or brown at the edges;
- fine marbling in the cuts that need it;
- firm, dry surfaces;
- a clean smell;
- no pooling of liquid in the pack.

*Ageing:*
- **dry-ageing** (weeks, unwrapped, in a chilled room) concentrates the flavour and tenderises; it is expensive;
- **wet-ageing** (in the vacuum pack) tenderises without concentrating.

*Price per portion,* not per kilo, with bone and trim counted. A bone-in chop at €12 a kilo with a 25 % bone costs more
per portion of meat than a boneless one at €14. Work it out: price per kilo ÷ the share that is meat × the portion.

*Good and Best:*
- **Good:** supermarket chuck for stews, pork shoulder, a *kogelbiefstuk* sliced thin;
- **Best:** a dry-aged *entrecote* or a pasture-raised joint from a butcher, once, for a guest dinner.

*Ask the butcher.* Butchers are glad to explain a cut, to trim and tie a joint, or to suggest a cheaper cut for the same
dish. Ask before you buy.

**Practice:**
1. **A first visit (45 min).** To a butcher, with the questions of `MEA-2.M2.X1` written beforehand.

**Look for:** you can explain each purchase: why this cut, this quality and this price per portion.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A cheap cut that costs more | Buying by the price per kilo alone | Price per portion |
| A missed cheaper option | Not asking the butcher | Ask what they would use |
| An expensive, dry stew | Buying a steak for a stew | Chuck or *klapstuk* |

**Safety:** meat goes home cool and into the fridge at 4 °C at once, raw on the bottom shelf; it is used by its use-by
date or frozen (assessment §9).

**Record:** the answers and the price-per-portion sums (in `MEA-2.M2.X1`).

### MEA-2.M3.L1 — Steak

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-2.M2.L2`, `MEA-2.M1.L2` · moves TS-PRO→3, TS-DRY→3*

*Objectives:* [S] temper, dry, sear, baste, rest, slice against the grain
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a steak is the simplest meat dish and the one with the least room for error: a few minutes and a
few degrees separate a deep crust over an even pink from a grey, steamed slab. The sequence below is the same for every
steak.

**You need:** for session 1, a steak of about 2 cm (*kogelbiefstuk*, rump or *entrecote*); for session 2, one of about
4 cm; salt; a high-smoke-point oil; 30 g of butter, a clove of garlic, thyme; the skillet; tongs; a spoon; the probe; a
board; a sharp knife.

**Explanation:**

*The sequence:*
1. **Temper:** out of the fridge for 20–30 minutes, so the core starts less cold.
2. **Dry and salt:** pat dry; salt well ahead (dry-brine, `SCI-2.M2.L2`) or just before cooking, not in between, when
   the surface is wet with drawn-out juice.
3. **Sear:** a very hot, heavy pan with a little oil; the steak is not moved until it releases with a crust. Turn it,
   then sear the edges and the fat cap too, holding it with tongs, so every outside surface changes colour (assessment
   §9).
4. **Baste:** butter, garlic and thyme for the last minutes, spooned over as the butter foams.
5. **Rest:** 5 minutes for a steak of 2–3 cm (`HEA-2.M2.L2`).
6. **Slice** across the grain.

*Thickness decides the method:*
- **under about 2 cm:** in the pan only, turning every minute for an even interior once the crust has formed;
- **thicker:** sear, then finish in a moderate oven (your oven table's moderate setting), or turn it often over medium
  heat.

*The stated doneness.* State the target from the scale of `MEA-2.M1.L2`, choose a pull temperature a few degrees below
it, and check the core after the rest. Compare the cross-section with your reference set.

**Practice:**
1. **A pan-only steak (45 min).** About 2 cm, to medium-rare. Probe at the pull and after the rest; cut and photograph
   it.
2. **A thick steak (45 min).** About 4 cm, seared and finished in the oven, to medium. Probe at the pull and after the
   rest; cut and photograph it.

**Look for:** a deep brown crust; fat edges rendered and golden; an even pink from edge to centre, with a thin grey
band; the probe within 3 °C of the target after the rest.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A grey, steamed steak | A wet steak in a cold pan, so it greys and steams | Dry it; a very hot pan |
| A torn, patchy crust | Moving it too early | Wait until it releases |
| Pale, raw-looking sides | Edges left raw-looking | Sear the edges with tongs |
| Juice on the board | Slicing at once | Rest 5 minutes |
| Chewy slices | Slicing with the grain | Across the grain |

**Safety:** a rare or medium-rare target only for an intact beef steak, seared on every outside surface, and not for a
guest in a risk group (assessment §9). A smoking pan of oil is close to its limit: the extractor on, a lid at hand.

**Record:** the targets, the pulls, the readings after the rest, and the photos against the reference set.

### MEA-2.M3.L2 — Pork and lamb chops

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l2 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-PRO→3*

*Objectives:* [S] render the fat edge, sear, finish; [K] safe pork temperatures
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a pork chop is cheap, quick and usually dry. Rendered fat, a crust and a pull a degree or two below
63 °C make it juicy and safe, and the same method works for lamb.

**You need:** for session 1, 2 pork loin chops of about 2 cm, one of them dry-brined overnight; for session 2, 4 lamb
chops or a pork chop on the bone; salt; oil; a shallot, stock or cider and butter for a pan sauce; the skillet; tongs;
scissors or a knife; the probe.

**Explanation:**

*The fat edge.* Stand the chop on its fat edge in the pan first, holding it with tongs, so the fat renders and crisps.
Then sear the faces, and finish the chop over lower heat or in the oven.

*Snip the fat edge* every few centimetres, through the fat and the thin membrane under it, so the chop doesn't curl.

*Safe pork and lamb temperatures:* 63 °C for 15 seconds at the core (assessment §9), reached after the rest. Pull a chop
a degree or two below, because its carryover is small. A trace of pink at the bone is normal at that temperature. Lamb
follows the same figure, served pink, not red.

*Juiciness.* Dry-brine or brine a lean pork chop (`POU-2.M6.L1`), and don't cook it much past the figure: every degree
above it costs juice.

*A quick pan sauce* on the fond, as in `SAU-2.M2.L1`: shallot, cider or stock, reduced, and mounted with cold butter.

**Practice:**
1. **Pork chops (45 min).** Render, sear and finish one dry-brined and one plain chop. Probe each after the rest, cut
   and taste them side by side.
2. **Lamb chops, or a pork chop on the bone (45 min).** The same sequence, with a quick pan sauce.

**Look for:** a crisp, golden fat edge; a chop that lies flat; the probe at or above 63 °C after the rest, and not far
above it.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Chewy, white fat | A pale, chewy fat edge | Render it first, on its edge |
| A chop that cups | A chop that curls | Snip the fat edge |
| Dry pork | Pork cooked to 75 °C and dry | Pull a degree or two below 63 °C |
| Red, rubbery lamb | Lamb served red | 63 °C, pink |

**Safety:** pork and lamb at 63 °C or more after the rest, probed; below it, back into the pan (assessment §9).
Rendering fat spits: stand back, and use tongs.

**Record:** the readings and a tasting line on the brined and plain chops.

### MEA-2.M3.L3 — Doneness by touch

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l3 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M3.L1` · moves JD-CUES→3, TS-PRO→3*

*Objectives:* [J] touch, colour of juices and spring, calibrated against the thermometer
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m3-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a cook who can feel doneness probes less often and pulls at the right moment. Like the poultry cues
of `POU-2.M6.L2`, touch is learned by predicting and checking, until your finger and the probe agree.

**You need:** for session 1, three steaks or chops of the same thickness; for session 2, a weeknight steak or chop; the
probe; the prediction log; the skillet.

**Explanation:**

*The touch test.* Press the centre of the steak with a fingertip:
- rare yields softly, like the base of the thumb with the hand relaxed;
- medium springs back;
- well done is firm.

The hand comparison is a guide only. Each cook calibrates it against the thermometer, and each cut feels different.

*Other cues:*
- **beads of juice** on the top surface: pink juice appears around medium-rare to medium, and clear juice later;
- **the spring** after a press: how fast the dent fills;
- **the time** for the thickness, from your own log.

*The cue-then-reading entry* (`POU-2.M6.L2`):
1. Predict the core from the cues.
2. Write the prediction down.
3. Probe, and log the difference.

The aim is at least eight of ten within ±3 °C (`JD-CUES` level 3). These entries add to the poultry log of
`POU-2.M6.X1`.

*When the cue is not enough.* A cut you have never cooked, or a very thick one, gets the probe first. The probe also has
the last word on every pork, lamb and mince figure.

**Practice:**
1. **Calibrate the finger (45 min).** Cook three steaks or chops to three donenesses. Press, predict, probe, log.
2. **Predict a meal (45 min).** A weeknight steak or chop: predict and probe at the pull and after the rest.

**Look for:** predictions that converge on the readings over the sessions.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A steak that feels done but isn't | Pressing at the edge | Press the centre |
| Juice lost, no feel | Pressing with a fork | A fingertip |
| A log that proves nothing | Probing before predicting | Write first |
| A surprise on a new cut | Trusting the hand test on a cut never tried before | Probe first on a new cut |

**Safety:** the probe confirms every pork, lamb and mince figure, whatever the finger says (assessment §9).

**Record:** the prediction entries.

### MEA-2.M4.L1 — Roasting a joint

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m4-l1 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 3 h · builds on `HEA-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-DRY→3, SC-BRWN→3*

*Objectives:* [S] beef or pork joint: sear-first versus reverse sear; [K] edge-to-centre gradient
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a joint is the centrepiece of a family or guest meal, and an expensive one to get wrong. The
difference between an even pink and a thick grey ring comes down to how the heat goes in, which you will see side by
side.

**You need:** two small joints of the same cut, about 600 g each (pork loin or beef topside, *rosbief*), tied; salt;
oil; the skillet; the roasting tin with a rack; the probe; a timer; the board; the phone.

**Explanation:**

*The edge-to-centre gradient.* In a hot oven, the outer layers overshoot long before the centre arrives. The result is a
thick grey band (`HEA-2.M1`). A lower oven narrows the gradient, but browns less.

*Two answers:*
- **sear-first:** brown the joint in a hot pan, then roast at a moderate heat;
- **reverse sear:** roast in a low oven (the lowest setting your oven table holds steadily) until the core is a few
  degrees below the target, rest, then sear hard in a very hot pan.

The reverse sear gives a more even interior and a crust at the last moment. The sear-first is quicker, and gives more
fond for a gravy.

*Safe roasting* (assessment §9). A whole roast reaches its core figure from the time-temperature table of §9, for
example:
- 60 °C held for 12 minutes;
- 62.8 °C held for 4 minutes.

The holding time may include the rise after the oven, so log the core every 5 minutes after the joint comes out, until
it peaks and begins to fall. Pork and lamb joints reach at least 63 °C. A rare roast below the table is not taught at
this stage.

*Browning* (`SC-BRWN`):
- a dry surface and a hot pan;
- the joint not crowded;
- the fond brown, not black.

Onions caramelised in the roasting fat go into the gravy.

**Practice:**
1. **The comparison (60 min active, about 3 hours in total).** Sear one joint first and roast it; reverse-sear the
   other; both to the same target. Log the core of each every 5 minutes after the oven. Rest, cut, and photograph the
   two cross-sections side by side.

**Look for:** an even, deep brown crust; a thin grey band on the reverse-seared joint; the core log meeting the table.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A pale, steamed crust | A wet joint going into the pan | Pat dry; salt ahead |
| Grey sides, no crust | Searing in too small a pan | A pan with space around the joint |
| Juice all over the board | Carving straight from the oven | Rest it; log the rise |
| A joint past its target | Pulling a joint at the target, so it overshoots | Pull a few degrees below |

**Safety:** the core log meets the §9 table, and pork and lamb reach 63 °C (assessment §9). Searing a joint in hot fat
spits: turn it with tongs, away from you.

**Record:** the two core logs and the side-by-side photo.

### MEA-2.M4.L2 — Carving and serving

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m4-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `MEA-2.M4.L1` · moves TS-PLT→2, TS-FAB→2*

*Objectives:* [S] carve against the grain; portion; plate with sauce
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a perfectly roasted joint can still be served as a heap of torn, grey slices. Trimming and tying

**Explanation:**

*Before roasting:*

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Ragged slices | Sawing | Long strokes, a long knife |
| Chewy slices | Carving with the grain | Find the grain; cut across |

## MEA-2.M5 — Minced meat

### MEA-2.M5.L1 — Mince safety

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m5-l1 -->
*1 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SAF-1.M3.L2` · moves SA-TEMP→4, SA-HYG→4*

*Objectives:* [K] why mince carries more risk than whole cuts; [S] storage, handling and core temperature
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a rare steak is safe and a pink burger is not, although they can come from the same animal. The
reason is the whole lesson, and it decides how you store, handle and cook every mince dish.

**You need:** this lesson; the work-order template (`KNF-2.M2`); the plan for `MEA-2.M5.X1`; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Why mince carries more risk.* Bacteria live on the surface of meat. In a steak they stay there and are killed by the
sear. Mincing spreads the surface through the whole mass, so the centre of a burger is as contaminated as the outside.

*The figure:* minced meat reaches 75 °C at the core (assessment §9), the Voedingscentrum's figure. The Food Code also
allows 68 °C for 17 seconds, 66 °C for 1 minute or 63 °C for 3 minutes, but the program uses 75 °C because it needs no
timing. A pink centre in a burger is never "medium".

*Storage:*
- mince in the fridge at 4 °C, on the bottom shelf;
- used by its use-by date, or frozen on the day it is bought, flat in a bag so it thaws evenly;
- thawed in the fridge, not on the counter.

*Handling:*
- mince is mixed and shaped by hand, so the work order puts it last (`KNF-2.M2`);
- hands are washed after shaping and before touching anything else;
- the salt, spices and breadcrumbs are measured into bowls before you touch the meat;
- the mixing bowl, board and tray go straight into the hot wash.

Gloves don't replace hand washing: a gloved hand on a tap spreads bacteria as well as a bare one.

*Cooked mince dishes* (*ragù*, meatballs in sauce) are cooled and stored like any batch: from 57 °C to 21 °C within
2 hours, and to 5 °C within 6 hours; reheated to 74 °C (assessment §9).

*Probing mince.* The probe goes into the centre of the thickest piece, from the side for a burger. In a batch of
meatballs, probe the largest.

**Practice:** theory only (1 h): write the work order and the safety plan for the meatball comparison of `MEA-2.M5.X1`:
mince last, the hand washes marked at each change, the cooling of the leftovers planned.

**Look for:** a plan with mince last, hand washing marked at each change, and the cooling of the leftovers planned.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A food-poisoning risk | Serving a pink burger | 75 °C at the centre |
| Warm outside, frozen inside | Thawing mince on the counter | In the fridge, overnight |
| A contaminated jar | Shaping meatballs and then touching the salt jar | Measure everything first |
| An underdone centre | Probing only the edge of a burger | From the side, into the centre |

**Safety:** this lesson is the safety rule: 75 °C at the core of every mince dish, mince last in the work order, and
cooked mince cooled and reheated by the standard (assessment §9).

**Record:** the work order and the safety plan.

### MEA-2.M5.L2 — Burgers and meatballs

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m5-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M5.L1` · moves TS-PRO→3, FL-TEXT→2*

*Objectives:* [S] fat content, salt timing, *panade*, gentle mixing; [J] texture choices
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a burger and a meatball start from the same mince but want opposite textures: one loose and juicy,
the other bound and tender. Fat, salt timing and a *panade* decide which you get.

**You need:** for session 1, 500 g of beef mince with about 20 % fat, salt, the skillet; for session 2, 500 g of
*half-om-half* mince, a slice of white bread and 50 ml of milk, an egg, a small onion, a tin of tomatoes; the probe; a
bowl of cold water for your hands; the raw station.

**Explanation:**

*Fat content:*
- lean beef mince (about 10 % fat) dries out in a burger;
- about 20 % fat, or half beef and half pork (*half-om-half*), stays juicy.

*Salt timing:*
- salt mixed into mince dissolves proteins that bind it, which gives a springy, sausage-like texture: right for a
  meatball that has to hold together, wrong for a tender burger;
- burgers are salted on the surface just before cooking.

*A panade:* bread soaked in milk, mashed and mixed into the meat. It holds moisture and interrupts the protein network,
so meatballs stay tender and juicy.

*Gentle mixing:* just until combined; shaped lightly with cold, wet hands. Overworking makes mince tough.

*Texture choices:*
- a loose, coarse burger;
- a tender meatball with a *panade*;
- a firm, springy one for a soup (`STK-2.M3.L1`).

*Doneness:*
- the probe goes into the centre of the thickest piece: 75 °C;
- a dimple pressed into the centre of a burger stops it doming.

*Meatballs in sauce.* Brown them in batches, then simmer them in the sauce until the largest reads 75 °C at the centre,
about 15–20 minutes.

**Practice:**
1. **Burgers (60 min).** Two burgers of about 150 g from 20 % fat mince: one salted in the mix, one on the surface just
   before cooking. Probe both to 75 °C; cut and compare.
2. **Meatballs (60 min).** A batch with a *panade*, browned and finished in a tomato sauce; the largest probed to 75 °C.

**Look for:** a juicy, loosely textured burger; meatballs that hold their shape but are tender inside; 75 °C at the
centre.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dry burger | Lean mince for burgers | About 20 % fat |
| A springy, sausage-like burger | Salt mixed into burger mince hours ahead | Salt the surface, just before |
| Dense, tough meatballs | Overworked, dense meatballs | Mix just until combined; a *panade* |
| A domed, uneven burger | A burger that domes and cooks unevenly | A dimple in the centre |

**Safety:** 75 °C at the centre of every burger and of the largest meatball, probed (assessment §9). The raw station of
`POU-2.M1.L2`, with mince last.

**Record:** the two burger cross-sections, and the probe readings.

### MEA-2.M6.L1 — Stewing beef

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m6-l1 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 3 h · builds on `HEA-2.M5.L1` · moves TS-CMB→2*

*Objectives:* [S] cut choice, browning in batches, liquid, low heat; [J] judge tenderness
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m6-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the cheapest beef makes the richest dish, if it is given time. A stew is where collagen, browning
and a barely moving liquid come together, and it is the first step towards the braises of `SLW-3`.

**You need:** for 6: 1.2 kg of chuck (*sukadelappen*, *runderlappen*), *klapstuk* or cheek; 2 onions, a carrot, a stick
of celery; a tablespoon of tomato purée; 330 ml of beer or 300 ml of red wine; about 500 ml of stock; a bay leaf and
thyme; oil; the casserole with a lid; tongs; a wooden spatula; a fork.

**Explanation:**

*The cut:* collagen-rich chuck, *klapstuk* or cheek. Lean cuts dry out and turn stringy in a stew. Large pieces of 4–5
cm hold together.

*Browning in batches:*
- pat the meat dry;
- brown it in a hot casserole, a few pieces at a time, with space between them;
- each batch leaves fond.

A crowded pot steams the meat grey: the liquid it releases can't evaporate, so the surface never gets hot enough to
brown.

*Building the pot.* With the meat out, sweat the vegetables in the fat and fond, fry the tomato purée until it darkens,
deglaze with the beer or wine, and scrape. The meat goes back with the stock and herbs (`MEA-2.M6.L2` covers the
layers).

*The liquid* comes about two thirds of the way up the meat. A covered pot in a low oven (about 150–160 °C) or on the
lowest flame barely bubbles. A rolling boil toughens the meat before the collagen softens (`HEA-2.M5.L1`).

*Judging tenderness:*
- a piece yields to a fork with little pressure and pulls apart along its fibres;
- it is not falling to threads.

This usually takes 2.5–3.5 hours. The time is a guide; the fork decides. Test from the second hour.

*Better the next day.* The stew is cooled within the limits of assessment §9, chilled, and the fat is lifted off the
top.

**Practice:**
1. **A stew (60 min active, about 3 hours in total).** Brown in batches, build the pot, deglaze, braise in the oven, and
   test with a fork every 30 minutes from the second hour.

**Look for:** a deep brown sear on every batch; a liquid that barely moves; meat that yields to a fork.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Grey, steamed meat | Crowding the pot | Small batches, with space |
| Dry, stringy meat | Lean meat | A collagen-rich cut |
| Tough meat after hours | A boil | Barely bubbling; a low oven |
| Tough, or falling apart | Stopping on the clock rather than the fork | Test with the fork |
| A thin, greasy sauce | Not reduced, not skimmed | Reduce at the end; lift the fat |

**Safety:** a full casserole is heavy and very hot: lift it with both hands and oven gloves, and open the lid away from
you. The stew is cooled by the standard before the fridge (assessment §9).

**Record:** the fork tests with their times, and a photo of a piece pulled apart.

### MEA-2.M6.L2 — Building flavour in a stew

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mea-2-m6-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `STK-2.M1.L1` · moves FL-ARCH→2, FL-SEAS→3*

*Objectives:* [S] aromatics, fond, wine or beer, herbs, finishing acid; [J] season at the end
<!-- END generated:lesson-mea-2-m6-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** two stews with the same meat can taste flat or deep, depending on what went into the pot and when.
Flavour is built in layers, and the last layer, seasoning and acid at the end, is the one most often skipped.

**You need:** the stew of `MEA-2.M6.L1`, braised; fine salt; the scale; vinegar or mustard; a little *beurre manié* if
needed; the tasting form.

**Explanation:**

*The layers:*
1. **The fond** of the browned meat.
2. **An aromatic base** (`STK-2.M1.L1`): onions, carrot and celery, sweated or browned in the same pot.
3. **Tomato purée,** fried until it darkens.
4. **The deglazing and cooking liquid:** beer for a Flemish *stoofvlees*; red wine for a *bœuf bourguignon*; stock.
5. **Herbs and spices:** bay, thyme and cloves; for *stoofvlees*, a slice of bread spread with mustard, laid on top,
   and a little syrup (*appelstroop*) or brown sugar.
6. **A finishing acid:** a splash of vinegar or mustard at the end, to lift the richness.

*Season at the end* (`FL-SEAS`). The liquid reduces, so most of the salt goes in during the last half hour, weighed and
noted, and tasted with the calibrated form. Add it in steps of a gram or two for the pot, stir, wait a minute, taste.

*The sauce.* Reduced uncovered at the end, or thickened by the bread or a little *beurre manié*, until it coats a spoon.
Lift the meat out while you reduce, so it doesn't fall apart.

*Taste the balance:* the richness of the meat and the fat; the sweetness of onions and syrup; the bitterness of beer or
the tannin of wine; the lift of acid. If it tastes heavy, it is usually acid that is missing, not salt.

**Practice:**
1. **Finish and season (45 min).** Reduce the sauce of the stew from `MEA-2.M6.L1`. Season it in steps, weighing the
   salt. Add the finishing acid. Taste on the form.

**Look for:** a glossy sauce that coats the meat; a balance where the richness is lifted by acid; the salt noted in
grams.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too salty after reducing | Salting at the start | Most of the salt in the last half hour |
| A flat, heavy stew | A flat, heavy stew without acid | A splash of vinegar or mustard |
| A bitter, acrid edge | Burnt tomato purée | Fry it until rust, not black |
| A watery sauce | Unreduced, watery liquid | Reduce uncovered at the end |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the salt in grams at each step, and the tasting form.

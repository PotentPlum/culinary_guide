**Before you start.** Every cooking method is a way of getting heat into food: at a certain speed, through a certain
medium, to a certain temperature. Once you see a method that way, you can predict what it will do and fix it when it
goes wrong. This course teaches that idea first, then the methods built on it:
- how heat moves through pans, ovens and liquids, and why it keeps moving after the heat is off;
- why a dry, hot surface browns and a wet one steams;
- the dry methods in a pan and in the oven: sautéing, pan-frying, searing and roasting;
- the moist methods: boiling, simmering, poaching, steaming and blanching;
- your first fair experiment, comparing methods on one ingredient.

Two habits start here and run through the whole program:
- **Reading the pan.** Sound, smoke, colour and smell tell you what is happening before any thermometer does.
- **Cue first, then reading.** You write down what the cue says ("the fish is done: opaque, flaking"), then check it
  with the probe or by cutting, and note whether they agreed. This is the **cue log**, and it trains `JD-CUES`.

*The cue log* (a page in the journal):

| Moment | Cue (seen, heard, smelt) | Verdict | Action | Check (probe or cut) | Agreed? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Plan about four to five weeks. The protein lessons are dinners; the comparisons and the temperature drill fit weekday
evenings. Buy fish on the day you cook it.

### HEA-1.M1.L1 — Conduction, convection and radiation

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `EQP-1.M1.L1` · moves SC-HEAT→2*

*Objectives:* [K] the three modes in pans, ovens and grills; why a fan oven cooks faster
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** when a dish cooks too fast outside and too slowly inside, or browns on top and stays pale
underneath, the reason is almost always the way heat is reaching it. Naming the main mode of heat transfer tells you
what to change: the pan, the shelf, the fan, the distance or the thickness.

**You need:** this lesson and the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Conduction* is heat passing by contact: from the burner into the pan, from the pan into the food, and from the surface
of the food to its centre. Metal conducts well, which is why a heavy pan spreads heat evenly. Food conducts poorly.
Inside food, conduction is slow, and that is why **thickness**, not weight, decides cooking time: a piece twice as thick
takes much more than twice as long.

*Convection* is heat carried by a moving fluid: boiling water, hot oil, the air in an oven. Around any food in an oven
there is a thin layer of cooler, still air that slows the heat down. A fan moves the air faster and strips that layer
away, so a **fan oven cooks faster at the same setting**. Its air isn't hotter; it just delivers heat faster.

*Radiation* is heat from a glowing or very hot source: an oven grill, charcoal, the walls of a hot oven. It travels in
straight lines and browns the surfaces that face it, and it weakens quickly with distance. A gratin under the grill
browns on top because the top faces the element; the base, facing away, gets little.

*In practice, every method mixes all three.* Roasting is convection from the air, radiation from the walls and
conduction from the tray. Naming the **main** mode at each step explains what to adjust:
- a pan that browns unevenly: conduction, so a heavier pan or a lower setting;
- a roast that cooks slowly: convection, so the fan, or space around the food;
- a gratin too pale: radiation, so a higher shelf, closer to the grill.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): for six familiar dishes, write the steps and name the main mode of heat transfer at
each: pancakes in a pan; boiled potatoes; a chicken roasted in a fan oven; a cauliflower gratin finished under the
grill; toast under the grill; vegetables sautéed in a wok or pan. For each dish, write one change that would speed it up
and the mode it works through.

**Look for:** you can explain why the top of a gratin browns under the grill but its base doesn't.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Food in the oven expected to colour as fast as in a pan | Thinking the oven's air is as hot as the pan, in effect | Air carries heat poorly; expect longer times, or brown in the pan first |
| A gratin burnt on top, or still pale | The distance from the grill ignored | Choose the shelf by the colour you want, and watch it |
| A thick piece raw in the middle | Time set by weight, not thickness | Cut thinner, or lower the heat and cook longer |

**Safety:** the grill browns in a minute or two and burns soon after: stay at the oven while it is on, and use oven
gloves for the tray (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the six dishes with the main mode at each step, and your one change for each.

### HEA-1.M1.L2 — Water, fat and air

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m1-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M1.L1` · moves SC-HEAT→2, CK-METH→1*

*Objectives:* [K] cooking media and their maximum temperatures; why boiled food never browns
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** the medium around the food sets a limit on its surface temperature, and the surface temperature
decides whether food browns. That one fact sorts every method into a family, and it explains why boiled meat is grey,
why steaming is quick, and why an oven needs time.

**You need:** 1 large potato; a saucepan of boiling water; a frying pan with 1 tablespoon of oil; a sheet pan; the oven
heated to 200 °C; a knife; a timer; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Water* cannot get hotter than its boiling point, 100 °C at sea level, however high the flame. More flame only makes
more steam. Food surrounded by water never browns, because browning needs a surface well above that. **Steam** that
condenses on food releases a lot of heat at once, so steaming is fast, though it browns no more than boiling does.

*Fat* can reach far higher temperatures before it starts to smoke. A surface in hot fat dries and goes well past
100 °C, so it browns and crisps.

*Air* can be very hot, but it carries heat poorly: there is little of it, and it touches the food lightly. An oven at
200 °C cooks a potato more slowly than water at 100 °C, but the oven browns it and the water doesn't.

*The three method families* follow from the medium:

| Family | Medium | Examples | Browns? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dry heat | Air, fat, metal | Roasting, sautéing, pan-frying, grilling | Yes |
| Moist heat | Water, steam | Boiling, simmering, poaching, steaming | No |
| Combination | First dry, then moist | Braising, pot-roasting | Yes, at the start |

**Practice:**
1. **Hand test of the media (30 min).** Cut three slices of the potato, each 5 mm thick. Drop one into the boiling
   water; lay one in the frying pan over medium heat; put one on the sheet pan in the oven. Test each with the knife
   every 2 minutes and note the time at which it meets no resistance, and what the surface looks like. Before you start,
   write down which you expect to cook fastest and which to brown.

**Look for:** your prediction written before you start, and checked against the times and the surfaces.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A pan of water boiling hard, and the food no faster | The flame turned up under boiling water "to cook faster" | Water stays at 100 °C; turn it back to a steady boil and save the gas |
| Disappointment at pale poached fish | Expecting colour from poaching | Moist heat doesn't brown; brown in a pan first, or choose a dry method |
| The slice in the pan spits | A wet slice in hot oil | Pat it dry first |

**Safety:** pat the slice dry and lay it in the pan away from you, so the oil doesn't splash towards you; use oven
gloves for the sheet pan (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** your prediction, the three times, and a line on each surface.

### HEA-1.M1.L3 — Carryover and resting

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m1-l3 -->
*1 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M1.L1` · moves SC-HEAT→2*

*Objectives:* [K] the heat gradient and carryover; [S] measure carryover on a roast
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m1-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** food doesn't stop cooking when it leaves the heat. A roast taken out at exactly the temperature you
want will be past it ten minutes later. Knowing how much it rises lets you take it out at the right moment. But for
safety you never assume the rise: you measure it.

**You need:** a pork tenderloin of about 500 g (or another thick roast you are cooking this week); salt; 1 tablespoon of
oil; a sheet pan; the oven at 200 °C; the probe; a timer; a board; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The heat gradient.* During cooking the outside of a piece of meat is much hotter than its centre. When the meat leaves
the oven, the heat stored in those hotter outer layers keeps flowing inwards, and the core temperature goes on rising
for several minutes. That is **carryover**.

*How large it is.* Carryover is larger for big, dense items cooked at high heat, because they store more heat in a
steeper gradient, and small for thin items. A pork tenderloin often rises about 3 °C; a thin fish fillet hardly rises at
all.

*Resting.* While the meat rests, loosely covered, its temperature evens out and the juices settle. Slicing at once loses
more juice onto the board.

*Using carryover safely.* You can take a roast out a little below its target and let carryover finish it, but you
**check after resting with the probe**, and you never assume the rise. For the pork tenderloin, an intact cut, the safe
core temperature is 63 °C held for 15 seconds ([assessment
§9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)): take it out at around 60 °C, rest it, and confirm at
least 63 °C before slicing. Poultry is different: it comes out only once it has reached 75 °C at the core, and carryover
is a bonus, not part of the plan.

**Practice:**
1. **Measure carryover (45 min, within a meal).** Salt and oil the tenderloin and roast it at 200 °C. Start probing the
   thickest part after about 12 minutes. Take it out at around 60 °C and note the time and reading. Probe again every 2
   minutes for 10 minutes, leaving it in the same place, and log each reading. Draw the rise as a small graph in the
   journal. Before slicing, confirm at least 63 °C held for 15 seconds; if it is lower, it goes back into the oven.

**Look for:** the core reading rising for several minutes after removal, then levelling off.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No idea how far it rose | Probing only once, at removal | Probe every 2 minutes for 10 minutes |
| A puddle of juice on the board | Slicing at once | Rest 5–10 minutes, loosely covered |
| Chicken taken out early "to finish resting" | Relying on carryover for poultry to reach 75 °C | Poultry comes out at 75 °C at the core, checked |

**Safety:** confirm the safe core temperature after resting, every time (§9). Clean the probe with hot soapy water
between readings in undercooked and cooked meat (`SAF-1.M3`). Leftovers go into the fridge within 2 hours.

**Record:** the readings and the graph, the size of the rise, and the reading before slicing.

### HEA-1.M1.X1 — Boiled, steamed and roasted potato

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-hea-1-m1-x1 -->
*comparison, recipe* — The same potato cubes boiled, steamed and roasted; time and texture compared.
<!-- END generated:exercise-hea-1-m1-x1 -->

**Task:** see the media at work on one ingredient, with the potato and the cut held constant.

**You need:** 1 kg of waxy or all-round potatoes; 15 ml oil; salt; a saucepan; a steamer insert or a sieve over a pot
with a lid; a sheet pan; the oven at 200 °C; a knife; a timer; a phone for photos.

**Recipe:**
1. **Cut.** Peel the potatoes and cut them into 2 cm cubes (`KNF-1.M3`). Divide them into three equal batches by weight.
2. **Roast.** Toss one batch with the oil and a two-finger pinch of salt, spread it on the sheet pan with space between
   the cubes, and roast at 200 °C. Turn once, halfway. Start this batch first: it takes longest.
3. **Boil.** Bring 1.5 litres of water with 15 g of salt to the boil and add the second batch.
4. **Steam.** Bring 3 cm of water to the boil under the steamer, add the third batch in one layer, and cover. Salt it
   after cooking with the same pinch as the roasted batch.
5. **Time each** from the moment it goes in until a knife meets no resistance: check the boiled and steamed batches
   every 3 minutes, the roasted one every 5.
6. **Photograph** the surfaces of the three batches side by side, and a cube of each cut in half.
7. **Taste** side by side, and write two sentences for each batch: what you see and taste, and how the medium explains
   it.

**Show:** the three times, the photos and the explanations. `SC-HEAT` level 2 rests on this module's quiz (its success
row); the comparison is the practice behind it.

**Time:** 1 h; the potatoes are a side dish.

### HEA-1.M2.L1 — The Maillard reaction

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m2-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M1.L2` · moves SC-BRWN→2*

*Objectives:* [K] temperature, dryness, pH and sugar; flavour and colour; [S] dry a surface for browning [S16]
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** browning is where much of the flavour of cooked food comes from: crust, toast, roasted roots, seared
meat. It doesn't happen by luck. It needs a dry surface well above the boiling point of water, and a few habits that
give it one.

**You need:** the inputs of round 1 of `HEA-1.M2.X1`: 250 g of mushrooms (or courgette in summer); 2 tablespoons of oil;
salt; the skillet or sauté pan; paper towels; a timer; a phone.

**Explanation:**

*The Maillard reaction* is a cascade of reactions between amino acids (from proteins) and reducing sugars. It makes
hundreds of flavour compounds and the brown colour of crusts. It is the flavour of bread crust, toast, roasted
vegetables and seared meat [S16].

*The conditions.*
- **Temperature:** it is very slow at 100 °C and fast well above it. A wet surface stays near 100 °C until its water has
  evaporated, so wetness delays browning.
- **Dryness:** water must leave the surface first. Every drop on the food is heat spent on evaporation instead of
  browning.
- **pH:** an alkaline surface browns faster. A pinch of bicarbonate of soda on onions speeds their browning.
- **Sugars and proteins:** both are needed, and more sugar browns faster. That is why carrots and onions colour quickly.

*The practical rules.*
1. **Dry the surface:** pat it with paper; brush mushrooms clean rather than washing them.
2. **Salt ahead or just before, not in between:** salt draws water out, which then sits on the surface for a while.
3. **Preheat the pan** before the food goes in.
4. **Don't crowd it:** leave space, so steam can escape.
5. **Don't move the food** until a crust forms.

**Practice:**
1. **Dry against wet (45 min).** Round 1 of `HEA-1.M2.X1`. Split the mushrooms into two batches of 125 g. Pat one batch
   dry; rinse the other and leave it wet. Heat the pan with a tablespoon of oil until it shimmers, cook the dry batch in
   one layer without moving it for 3 minutes, then stir. Photograph it at 3 and 6 minutes. Wipe the pan, and do the same
   with the wet batch. Salt both at the end.

**Look for:** a sizzle that stays loud; a deep, even brown on the dry batch while the wet batch is still grey.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Grey mushrooms in a puddle | Mushrooms washed just before cooking | Brush or wipe them; if they must be rinsed, dry them well |
| No sizzle when the food goes in | A cold pan | Preheat until the oil shimmers |
| Patchy, pale colour | Moving the food every few seconds | Leave it until it releases and shows colour at the edges |

**Safety:** wet food spits in hot oil: lay it in away from you, and keep the pan handle turned inwards (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the photos at 3 and 6 minutes for both batches (in `HEA-1.M2.X1`).

### HEA-1.M2.L2 — Caramelisation and burning

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m2-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M2.L1` · moves SC-BRWN→2*

*Objectives:* [K] sugar browning; where flavour turns bitter; [S] caramelise onions versus burn them
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** the line between deep brown and burnt is narrow, and it is crossed quickly at high heat. Browning
tastes sweet and roasted; burning tastes bitter and acrid. You learn to tell them apart by smell and colour, and you
learn why good caramelised onions can't be rushed.

**You need:** 6 onions; 3 tablespoons of oil or 30 g of butter with a little oil; salt; three frying pans, or one used
three times; a cup of water; a wooden spoon; a timer; a phone; the extractor on or a window open.

**Explanation:**

*Caramelisation* is sugar alone breaking down with heat into brown, sweet-bitter and nutty compounds. It needs higher
temperatures than the Maillard reaction. Onions contain both sugars and proteins, so caramelised onions brown by both
reactions at once.

*Browning against burning.*

| | Browning | Burning |
|---|---|---|
| Smell | Sweet, roasted, nutty | Acrid, sharp, smoky |
| Colour | Even golden to deep brown | Black spots and edges |
| Taste | Sweet, deep | Bitter |
| Pan | Brown fond | Black fond, smoke |

At high heat the step from one column to the other takes seconds.

*Caramelised onions* take a long time at moderate heat, often 30–45 minutes. You stir every few minutes, and when the
fond on the base of the pan turns deep brown, you add a splash of water and scrape it up, so it joins the onions instead
of burning. Quick, high-heat "caramelised" onions are burnt outside and raw inside: the outside reaches burning before
the inside has softened and released its sugar.

**Practice:**
1. **Onions three ways (60 min).** Slice the onions thinly and divide them into three equal piles, each with a
   tablespoon of oil and a pinch of salt.
   - **Pan 1, high heat, 10 minutes**, stirred now and then.
   - **Pan 2, moderate heat, 40 minutes**, stirred every few minutes, with a splash of water whenever the fond is deep
     brown.
   - **Pan 3, deliberately too far:** high heat, stirred rarely, until the edges blacken and the smell turns acrid. Then
     stop.

   Photograph and taste all three side by side.

**Look for:** the slow onions jammy and deep brown; the fast ones blackened at the edges and sharp; the burnt ones
bitter.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Burnt edges and raw centres | Rushing with high heat | Moderate heat, and time: plan 40 minutes |
| Black spots on the base of the pan | The fond not deglazed, so it burnt | A splash of water whenever the fond is deep brown |
| Bitter onions | "Darker is better", past the acrid smell | Stop at deep brown, when the smell is still sweet |

**Safety:** pan 3 smokes: extractor on, window open, and take the pan off the heat as soon as it smells acrid. A pan
that smokes heavily is moved off the heat, not left (`SAF-1.M4.L2`).

**Record:** the three photos and a tasting line for each. The slow onions go on toast, into a soup or on a burger.

### HEA-1.M3.L1 — Sauté

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M2.L1` · moves TS-DRY→2*

*Objectives:* [S] preheat, fat, pan load, tossing and stirring; [K] why overcrowding steams
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** sautéing is the quickest way to give vegetables colour and flavour, and it is a test of control:
heat, fat, pan load and movement all at once. Get one wrong and the vegetables steam, burn or stay raw.

**You need:** for each session, 500 g of seasonal vegetables for 2 (for example carrot, Brussels sprouts and red onion
in winter; courgette and peppers in summer); 2 tablespoons of neutral oil; 10 g of butter; salt and pepper; the sauté
pan or skillet; a plate or tray to hold the first batch; for practising the toss, a handful of dried beans.

**Explanation:**

*Sautéing* means small, even pieces over high heat in a little fat, kept moving. The pieces must be the same size, so
they cook in the same time (`KNF-1.M3.L5`): carrot in bias slices, sprouts halved, onion in wedges.

*The sequence.*
1. **Preheat the pan.**
2. **Add the fat** and let it shimmer.
3. **Add the food in one layer.**
4. **Listen** for a steady sizzle.
5. **Toss or stir** only once a side has coloured.

*Pan load.* A pan holds as much as fits in one layer with a little space between the pieces. Overcrowding releases more
water than can evaporate; the pan's temperature drops, the water pools, and the food steams. A 28 cm pan sautés about
250 g at a time. For more, cook in batches, and keep the first batch on a plate.

*Fat choice.* A neutral oil (rapeseed, sunflower) takes the high heat. Butter tastes better but its milk solids burn at
sauté heat, so it goes in at the end, for flavour.

*Tossing.* A flick of the wrist that slides the food to the far edge of the pan and lifts it back over itself. Practise
with a cold pan and dried beans first. Stirring with a spatula does the same job, more slowly.

**Practice:**
1. **Sauté vegetables (60 min).** Cut the vegetables. Sauté them in two batches, stirring with a spatula only once each
   side has coloured. Return the first batch, add the butter, season and toss to coat. Photograph a spoonful.
2. **Repeat with a different mix (60 min).** Practise the toss with the cold pan and beans for five minutes, then sauté,
   tossing instead of stirring. Compare the colour with the first session.

**Look for:** a loud, even sizzle; pieces browned on several sides; no liquid pooling.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No sizzle, then pale, soft vegetables | Food in a cold pan | Preheat; oil shimmering before the food goes in |
| Liquid pooling, everything grey | Everything in one batch | One layer per batch; hold the first on a plate |
| Pale on every side, soft | Stirring constantly | Let each side colour before moving |
| Black flecks, bitter taste | Butter on high heat from the start | Oil for the heat; butter at the end |

**Safety:** keep the pan handle turned inwards, and toss away from yourself. If the oil smokes heavily, take the pan
off the heat (`SAF-1.M4.L2`).

**Record:** a photo from each session, and one line on what tossing changed.

### HEA-1.M3.L2 — Pan-frying and shallow frying

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M3.L1`, `SAF-1.M3.L2` · moves TS-DRY→2, IN-FSH→1, IN-MEA→1*

*Objectives:* [S] pan-fry a fish fillet and a chicken thigh skin-side down; [K] oil depth and temperature
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a crisp-skinned fish fillet and a chicken thigh with glassy, golden skin are two of the most useful
dinners there are, and two of the easiest to spoil: torn skin, burnt skin, or skin that is soft and fatty. Both are
won by the start in the pan and by patience.

**You need:** for session 1, a skin-on fish fillet per person (mackerel, saithe or farmed sea bass), bought that day;
for session 2, 2 boneless, skin-on chicken thighs per person; neutral oil; salt; the skillet or a heavy pan; the fish
spatula; tongs; paper towels; the probe; the cue log.

**Explanation:**

*Pan-frying* cooks larger pieces in a thin layer of fat, without tossing. *Shallow frying* uses fat up to about half the
height of the food, at 160–180 °C measured with the thermometer: hot enough to crisp, cool enough not to burn the
outside before the inside cooks.

*Skin-side down.*
- **A chicken thigh** starts skin-side down in a moderate pan, with a little oil. The fat under the skin melts out
  slowly (it renders), and the skin crisps in it. Too hot, and the skin burns before the fat has gone.
- **A fish fillet** goes skin-side down into a hot pan, and you press it gently with the spatula for the first 10–20
  seconds, because the skin shrinks and the fillet curls.
- **In both cases, leave it until it releases.** Skin sticks at first and lets go by itself once it is crisp. Forced
  early, it tears.

Most of the cooking happens on the skin side; the other side gets a short finish.

*Doneness* is checked with the probe in the thickest part
([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)):
- **chicken:** 75 °C;
- **fish:** 63 °C, when the flesh has turned opaque and flakes.

Write the cue first (the fish opaque to the top, the chicken juices clear), then probe.

**Practice:**
1. **A fish fillet (60 min).** Pat the skin dry and salt it. Heat a tablespoon of oil until it shimmers. Lay the fillet
   in skin-side down, away from you, and press for 15 seconds. Cook until the flesh is opaque most of the way up (about
   3–4 minutes), turn, and finish for 30–60 seconds. Write the cue, then probe. Photograph the skin.
2. **A chicken thigh (60 min).** Pat the thighs dry and salt them. Heat the pan over moderate heat with a teaspoon of
   oil, and lay them in skin-side down. Leave them for 12–15 minutes, until the skin is deep golden and
   releases. Turn and cook another 8–10 minutes. Cue first, then probe to 75 °C.

**Look for:** the skin crisp and golden over its whole surface; the fish releasing by itself; the probe reading in
range.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The fish skin tears and stays in the pan | Turning the fish too early | Wait until it releases by itself |
| Chicken skin black and the fat still under it | Too hot a pan for the chicken | A moderate pan; the fat must render first |
| Greasy, soft food | Oil too cool, so the food soaks it up | Measure shallow-frying oil: 160–180 °C |

**Safety:** hot oil is never left alone. If it catches fire, cover the pan with its lid or the fire blanket and switch
off the heat; never water (`SAF-1.M4.L2`). Wash your hands, the board and the tongs after the raw chicken (`SAF-1.M2`).

**Record:** both photos and both cue-log entries.

### HEA-1.M3.L3 — Searing and fond

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m3-l3 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M3.L1` · moves TS-DRY→2, JD-CUES→1*

*Objectives:* [S] sear for colour; [K] fond as the base of a sauce; [J] when to turn
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m3-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a seared surface is flavour, and the brown residue it leaves in the pan is the start of a sauce. The
skill is in the timing: turning when the crust is ready and not before, and knowing when the fond has gone from brown
to burnt.

**You need:** for each session, three or four pieces of one item: halloumi in 1 cm slices, celeriac in 1.5 cm slices, or
burgers; neutral oil; salt; a heavy stainless pan or the skillet (not non-stick); for session 2, 150 ml of stock or
water and 10 g of butter; tongs; the probe for burgers.

**Explanation:**

*Searing* means colouring the surface at high heat, for flavour. It doesn't "seal in juices": a seared piece loses as
much juice as an unseared one. It gives the crust, and that is reason enough.

*When to turn.* Turn when the piece releases with a gentle push and a deep crust shows at its edge. A piece that sticks
is not ready. Turned early, the crust tears off and stays in the pan.

*Fond* is the browned residue on the base of the pan: juices and bits of the food, browned by the same reactions as the
crust. It is full of flavour, and it is the base of a **pan sauce**. You lift it with a liquid: that is **deglazing**.
Pour in stock or water, scrape with a wooden spoon, and let it bubble down. Golden-to-brown fond makes a good sauce.
Black fond is bitter and is discarded, and the pan washed before going on.

A non-stick pan browns food, but little fond sticks to it, so it makes poor pan sauces.

*Celeriac* needs cooking through as well as searing. Sear both sides, then lower the heat and cover the pan for a few
minutes until a knife meets no resistance. *Burgers* are minced meat: they must reach 75 °C at the core
([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)), so finish them over lower heat and probe.

**Practice:**
1. **Sear (60 min).** Pat the pieces dry. Heat the pan over medium-high heat with a thin film of oil. Lay the pieces in,
   space between them, and don't move them. Turn each by the cue: it releases with a gentle push and the crust shows at
   the edge. Note the time each took, and photograph both sides.
2. **Deglaze (60 min).** Sear again. Lift the pieces onto a plate. Look at the fond: golden to brown? Pour in the stock,
   away from you, and scrape. Let it bubble until reduced by half, take it off the heat, swirl in the butter, and taste
   it as a simple sauce. Season.

**Look for:** a deep brown crust; the piece releasing with a gentle push; fond golden to brown, not black.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A patchy crust, torn off | Flipping too often, or too early | Once, by the cue |
| Little fond, a thin sauce | A non-stick pan | Stainless or cast iron for searing |
| A bitter sauce | Black fond deglazed | Discard burnt fond; lower the heat next time |

**Safety:** the liquid hits the hot pan as a burst of steam: pour it away from you and keep your face back. Burgers to
75 °C at the core (§9), with the probe cleaned between readings.

**Record:** the photos, the turning times, and a tasting line on the sauce.

### HEA-1.M3.L4 — Reading the pan

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m3-l4 -->
*1 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M3.L3` · moves JD-CUES→2*

*Objectives:* [J] sound, smoke, colour, smell; adjust the heat before it goes wrong
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m3-l4 -->

**Why it matters:** the pan tells you what is happening long before the food is ruined, if you listen and look. A cook
who reads the pan adjusts the heat before anything goes wrong; a cook who watches only the clock finds out afterwards.

**You need:** a simple dinner that uses a pan, for example sautéed vegetables with seared halloumi or a pan-fried
chicken thigh; the probe; a knife; the cue log.

**Explanation:**

*Sound.*
- A steady sizzle: right.
- A quiet hiss, or bubbling: too cool, or too wet; the food is steaming.
- Spitting and crackling: too hot, or water in the fat.

*Smoke.* A first wisp as the fat reaches temperature is normal. Heavy smoke means the pan is too hot for that fat, which
is breaking down: take it off the heat.

*Colour.* The edges of the food and the fond show what is happening underneath. A dark ring at the edge before the
expected time means too much heat.

*Smell.* Roasted and nutty is right; acrid is burning, and it comes before the smoke.

*Acting early.* Lower the heat, or lift the pan off the burner for a moment, as soon as a cue turns, not after the
damage. A gas flame responds at once; an electric plate is slow, so lifting the pan is faster (`EQP-1.M2`).

*Cue first, then reading.* At a few moments, stop and write what the cue says and what you will do. Then check it, with
the probe or by cutting a piece, and note whether the cue was right. Over time the log shows which cues you can trust.

| Cue | Verdict | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Sizzle turns to bubbling, liquid in the pan | Overloaded or too cool | Raise the heat; take out half |
| Heavy smoke before the food goes in | Fat too hot | Off the heat; wipe out; fresh fat, lower setting |
| Acrid smell | Burning | Off the heat; lift the food out |

**Practice:**
1. **Cue log (45 min).** Cook the dinner, and stop at four moments: when the fat goes in, when the food goes in,
   halfway, and when you think it is done. At each, write the cue, the verdict and the action in the cue log; then probe
   or cut to check, and note whether they agreed.

**Look for:** at least three of four verdicts confirmed by the check.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Done or burnt at the wrong time | Watching only the clock | Listen and look; the time is only a guide |
| Smoke before you noticed anything | Ignoring the smell until there is smoke | Act on the first acrid smell |
| Wet food boiled in its juices | Heat turned up when the food is quiet because it is wet | Take out half the food, and pat the rest dry next time |

**Safety:** a smoking pan comes off the heat at once. If the fat catches fire: lid or fire blanket, heat off, never
water (`SAF-1.M4.L2`).

**Record:** the four cue-log entries.

### HEA-1.M4.L1 — Roasting vegetables

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m4-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `EQP-1.M2.L3`, `KNF-1.M3.L5` · moves TS-DRY→2*

*Objectives:* [S] cut size, oil, spacing, temperature, turning; [J] judge when they're done
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a tray of roasted roots is one of the best things a Dutch winter kitchen makes, and one of the most
often spoiled: pale and soft in one corner, burnt in another, beetroot still hard when the onions are charred. Five
variables decide the result, and all of them are yours to control.

**You need:** about 1 kg of mixed seasonal roots and onions, for example carrot, beetroot, celeriac, parsnip, red onion,
or a piece of squash; 30 ml oil; salt; a few sprigs of thyme or rosemary; two sheet pans; your oven table
(`EQP-1.M2.X1`); a knife; a timer; oven gloves.

**Explanation:**

*Roasting vegetables* browns them in dry heat and concentrates their sugars as water evaporates. The variables:
- **cut size:** even pieces cook evenly (`KNF-1.M3.L5`);
- **oil:** a thin coat on every piece, so the surface browns rather than dries;
- **spacing:** room between the pieces, or the water they release can't escape, and they steam;
- **temperature:** set from your oven table, usually 200–220 °C;
- **turning:** once, halfway, so two sides colour.

*Mixed trays.* Dense roots (carrot, beetroot, celeriac, parsnip) take longer than onions or squash. Two ways to make
them finish together:
- **cut them smaller:** dense roots in 2 cm pieces, onion and squash in 3–4 cm wedges;
- **start them earlier:** the dense roots go in first, the onion and squash 10–15 minutes later.

Beetroot is the densest; give it both.

*Done* means tender inside (a knife meets no resistance) and browned at the edges. Taste a piece of each kind: the time
is only a guide.

**Practice:**
1. **A tray of roots (75 min).** Heat the oven to the setting from your table. Cut the dense roots into 2 cm pieces and
   the onion and squash into wedges. Toss the dense roots with two-thirds of the oil and some salt, spread them over the
   sheet pans with space between, and roast. After 15 minutes, add the onion and squash, tossed with the rest of the
   oil. Turn everything halfway, add the herbs for the last 10 minutes, and test each kind with the knife. Photograph
   the tray.

**Look for:** browned edges, tender centres, no burnt small pieces.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pale, soft and wet | A crowded tray | Two trays, space between the pieces |
| Dry, leathery, unevenly brown | Too little oil | A thin coat on every piece; toss in a bowl |
| Charred onion, hard beetroot | Beetroot and onion cut the same size and put in at the same time | Beetroot smaller and first; onion larger and later |

**Safety:** oven gloves for the trays, and open the oven door at arm's length: the steam that comes out burns
(`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the photo, the times, and what you would change next time.

### HEA-1.M4.L2 — A simple roast with a thermometer

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m4-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAF-1.M3.L2`, `HEA-1.M1.L3` · moves TS-DRY→2, SA-TEMP→3, TS-PRO→1*

*Objectives:* [S] roast chicken thighs or a pork tenderloin to a safe core temperature; rest it
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a roast is done when its core reaches a temperature, not when the timer rings. Ovens, cuts and
sizes differ too much for time alone. With the probe, a roast is both safe and juicy, and you can prove it.

**You need:** 4 bone-in chicken thighs, or a pork tenderloin of about 500 g; oil; salt and pepper; a sheet pan or a
roasting dish; the oven at 200 °C; the probe; hot soapy water and a clean cloth for the probe; a board; foil; the
journal.

**Explanation:**

*Roasting a protein to a measured core temperature*
([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)):
- **chicken thighs:** 75 °C;
- **a pork tenderloin,** an intact cut: 63 °C, held for 15 seconds.

Roasting is not done by time. The times below are a guide to when to start probing.

*Probing.* Push the probe into the thickest part, from the side, so the tip sits in the centre. Keep it away from the
bone: bone conducts heat and gives a reading that is too high. Check the reading again after resting (`HEA-1.M1.L3`).
Clean the probe with hot soapy water between readings, since it has been in meat that wasn't yet done.

*Resting.* A few minutes for thighs, 5–10 minutes for a tenderloin, loosely covered with foil. The tenderloin rises
during the rest; the thighs must already have reached 75 °C when they come out.

*Leftovers* go into the fridge within 2 hours (§9), covered, and are eaten within 2 days.

**Practice:**
1. **Roast and probe (75 min).** Pat the meat dry; don't rinse it (`SAF-1.M2`). Oil and season it.
   - **Thighs:** skin up on the sheet pan, roasted at 200 °C; start probing after about 30 minutes; usually 35–45
     minutes in all.
   - **Tenderloin:** roasted at 200 °C; start probing after about 12 minutes; take it out at around 60 °C.

   Probe at removal and after resting, and log both readings with the time. Photograph the meat and a cut piece.

**Look for:** golden skin or crust; clear readings at or above the safe core temperature after resting.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Juices lost on the board, doneness still uncertain | Cutting into the meat to check instead of probing | The probe, in the thickest part |
| A high reading, then raw meat near the bone | Probing next to the bone | Away from the bone, in the centre of the thickest part |
| No proof the meat was safe | Forgetting to log the reading | Log every reading, with the time |

**Safety:** the core temperature after resting is the safety check (§9). Wash your hands, the board and any tools after
handling the raw meat (`SAF-1.M2`). Leftovers into the fridge within 2 hours.

**Record:** both readings with their times, and the photos.

### HEA-1.M5.L1 — Boil, simmer and poach

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m5-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M1.L2` · moves TS-MOI→2, CK-METH→2*

*Objectives:* [K] temperature ranges and what the bubbles look like; [S] hold a simmer and a poach
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** "simmer" and "poach" are among the most common words in recipes, and they mean particular
temperatures. A stock that boils turns cloudy; a chicken breast that boils turns stringy; an egg that boils breaks up.
You can read the temperature from the surface of the water once you have checked it against a thermometer a few times.

**You need:** a saucepan with about 2 litres of water; the thermometer; a phone for photos; the cue log.

**Explanation:**

*The temperature ranges, and what they look like:*

| Range | Temperature | The surface | For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boil | 100 °C | Large, rolling bubbles that don't stop when stirred | Pasta, green vegetables, potatoes |
| Simmer | About 85–95 °C | Small bubbles breaking gently at the surface | Stocks, soups, most braises |
| Poach | About 70–85 °C | The surface shivering, steam rising, no bubbles breaking | Eggs, fish, chicken breast |

*Why it matters.* A boil tosses delicate food about and toughens proteins. A simmer extracts flavour from bones and
vegetables without churning the fat and particles into the liquid, so a stock stays clear. A poach cooks gently and
evenly, so the outside isn't overcooked by the time the centre is done.

*Holding a temperature.* Three things hold it:
- **the burner setting:** lower than you expect, once it is there;
- **a lid:** on, it traps heat and a simmer turns into a boil; off, it lets heat escape;
- **the volume of liquid:** a large volume changes temperature slowly, and absorbs cold food without crashing.

**Practice:**
1. **Read the bubbles (45 min).** Put the water on a medium heat with the thermometer in it, its tip in the middle of
   the water. As it heats, stop at each range: first write what the surface looks like and which range you think it is,
   then read the thermometer. Photograph the surface at each. Then let it come to a full boil, turn it down, and find
   the setting that holds a simmer.

**Look for:** you name the range from the surface alone, then confirm it with the thermometer.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A cloudy stock, a broken-up fish | Calling a boil a simmer | Small bubbles breaking gently; large rolling bubbles are a boil |
| A simmer boiling over | A lid left on, so a simmer turns into a boil | Lid off or ajar; a lower setting |
| A poach that goes cold and starts again | Adding cold food to a small pot, so the temperature crashes | A larger volume of liquid; bring it back gently |

**Safety:** lift a lid away from you, so the steam rises on the far side; turn pan handles inwards (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the photos with your verdict and the reading for each, and the setting that holds a simmer on your hob.

### HEA-1.M5.L2 — Blanching and shocking

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m5-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M5.L1` · moves TS-MOI→2*

*Objectives:* [S] blanch green vegetables in plenty of salted water; shock or spread to cool; [K] why
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** green vegetables can be bright, tender-crisp and well seasoned, or olive-grey and limp. The
difference is a few minutes, the size of the pot, and what you do the moment they come out.

**You need:** 500 g of green beans or broccoli (or peas and sugar snaps in summer); a large pot with 4 litres of water;
40 g of salt; a large bowl of ice water; a slotted spoon or spider; a colander; a tray; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Blanching* cooks vegetables briefly in plenty of well-salted, boiling water. The large volume matters: 250 g of cold
vegetables in 4 litres barely slow the boil, but in 1 litre the water stops boiling and the vegetables stew. The salt
seasons them from the outside (`TST-1.M2.L3`): 10 g/l is a good start.

*Shocking* means plunging them into ice water as soon as they are done. It stops the cooking at once, keeps the texture
and sets a bright colour. Without it, the heat stored in the vegetables keeps cooking them. For vegetables to be eaten
warm straight away, spreading them on a tray to steam off is enough.

*Why green turns olive.* Chlorophyll changes with long cooking and with acid. Three habits keep it green:
- **short cooking:** taste from 2 minutes;
- **an open pan,** so the acids that escape from the vegetables leave with the steam;
- **acid only at the end,** at the table: lemon in the cooking water turns beans grey.

*Uses.* A side dish, reheated in butter; vegetables prepared ahead for a busy evening; and loosening tomato skins
(`KNF-1.M4.L1`).

**Practice:**
1. **Blanch and shock (45 min).** Bring the water and salt to a rolling boil. Blanch the vegetables in two batches of
   250 g; taste from 2 minutes, and when tender-crisp lift them out with the spider straight into the ice water. Leave a
   third, small batch in the colander to cool by itself, for comparison. After 5 minutes, drain the shocked batches and
   compare the colour, texture and taste of all of them.

**Look for:** bright green, tender-crisp, seasoned; the comparison batch dull and soft.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The water stops boiling, the vegetables stew | Too little water, or overloading the pot | 4 litres; batches of 250 g |
| Bland vegetables | Unsalted water | 10 g/l, tasted |
| Soft and olive | Skipping the shock | Ice water at once, or spread out to cool |
| Grey beans | Lemon juice added early | Acid only at the end |

**Safety:** a large pot of boiling water is heavy: lift the vegetables out with the spider instead of carrying the pot
to the sink. Set the ice bowl next to the hob before you start (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** a photo of the shocked and the unshocked batches side by side, and your blanching time.

### HEA-1.M5.L3 — Steaming

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m5-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M5.L1` · moves TS-MOI→2*

*Objectives:* [S] steam vegetables and fish; [K] steaming versus boiling (flavour, nutrients)
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m5-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** steaming is fast, gentle and clean-tasting. Vegetables keep more of their flavour, and fish cooks in
minutes without falling apart. It also teaches you what salted water was doing for you: steamed food needs salt
afterwards.

**You need:** 400 g of carrot or celeriac; a fish fillet per person (cod, saithe or another white fish, bought that
day); a steamer insert, or a sieve or colander over a pot with a lid; a second saucepan; salt; a knife; the probe; a
timer.

**Explanation:**

*Steaming* cooks food in the vapour above boiling water. When steam touches the cooler food it condenses back to water,
and that releases a lot of heat at once, so steaming transfers heat fast. The food isn't in the water.

*Against boiling.*

| | Steaming | Boiling |
|---|---|---|
| Flavour and water-soluble vitamins | Fewer leach out | More leach into the water |
| Texture | Stays more intact | Can break up |
| Seasoning | None from the water: salt afterwards | Seasoned by salted water |

*Set-up.*
- A steamer insert, a basket, or a sieve over a pot, with a lid.
- The water must not touch the food: 3–4 cm in the pot is usually enough.
- Space between the pieces, so the steam can circulate. Stacked pieces cook unevenly.
- Check the water level on longer steams; top it up with boiling water from the kettle.

*Fish* steams well in a few minutes. It is done when it is opaque and flakes, at 63 °C at the core
([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)).

**Practice:**
1. **Steam vegetables and fish (60 min).** Cut the carrot or celeriac into batons about 1 cm thick and divide them into
   two batches. Steam one batch, covered, and boil the other in salted water (10 g/l); test both with the knife and note
   the times. Salt the steamed batch with a pinch and taste both. Then steam the fish fillet on a lightly oiled plate or
   paper in the steamer, about 5–8 minutes depending on thickness; cue first, then probe.

**Look for:** the steamed vegetable tasting more of itself; the fish just flaking.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A burnt smell, the pot dry | A pot that runs dry | Check the water; top up from the kettle |
| Slow, uneven cooking | A lid that lets the steam escape | A tight lid, or foil under it |
| Some pieces raw | Pieces stacked so the steam can't reach them | One layer, space between |

**Safety:** steam burns before you see it: lift the lid away from you and reach in with tongs or oven gloves. Fish to
63 °C at the core (§9).

**Record:** the two times, the tasting notes, and the cue-log entry for the fish.

### HEA-1.M5.L4 — Gentle poaching

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m5-l4 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M5.L1`, `SAF-1.M3.L2` · moves TS-MOI→3*

*Objectives:* [S] poach chicken breast in a court bouillon below a simmer; [J] check doneness
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m5-l4 -->

**Why it matters:** a chicken breast is lean and dries out easily. Poached gently in a flavoured liquid, held below a
simmer, it stays juicy from edge to centre, and the liquid becomes a soup. It is also the first time you hold a liquid
at a set temperature for a whole cooking time.

**You need:** 2 chicken breasts, about 200 g each; for the court bouillon: 1.5 litres of water, 1 onion, 1 carrot, 1
celery stick, a bay leaf, a few parsley stalks, 10 peppercorns, 15 g of salt and 30 ml of white-wine vinegar; a
saucepan; the thermometer and the probe; a timer; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*A court bouillon* is water with aromatics, salt and a little acid, simmered briefly so it has flavour to give. It is
quick: 15 minutes of simmering is enough.

*Poaching chicken breast below a simmer* cooks it gently and evenly.
- Start the breast in the warm liquid, not boiling liquid.
- Bring the liquid to about 75–80 °C and hold it there.
- The breast is done at 75 °C at the core
  ([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)).

Because the liquid is barely hotter than the target, the outside can't overcook. The price is time: often 25–35 minutes
for a 200 g breast. If the core stalls just below 75 °C, hold the liquid at the top of its range until it gets there.

*Doneness by cue and probe.* The breast feels firm but springy when pressed, and its juices run clear when pierced.
Write that cue down, then confirm with the probe in the thickest part.

*After the poach.* The court bouillon has taken flavour from the chicken. Strain it, cool it, and it is the base of a
soup.

**Practice:**
1. **Poach (75 min).** Put the water, the roughly chopped vegetables, herbs, peppercorns, salt and vinegar in the
   saucepan, and simmer for 15 minutes. Turn the heat down and let it settle to the poaching range. Put in the breasts
   and bring the liquid to about 75–80 °C. Log the liquid temperature every 5 minutes, adjusting the burner to stay in
   range. After 20 minutes, check by the cue, then probe. Lift the breasts out at 75 °C at the core and rest them
   briefly before slicing.

**Look for:** the liquid held without bubbles; the breast juicy, and 75 °C at the core.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stringy, dry chicken | Boiling the breast | Hold the liquid at 75–80 °C, no bubbles breaking |
| Dry outside, underdone centre | Starting it in boiling liquid | Start in warm liquid and bring it up gently |
| Unknown safety | Not probing | Probe the thickest part: 75 °C |

**Safety:** chicken to 75 °C at the core (§9). Wash your hands and the board after the raw chicken (`SAF-1.M2`). Cool
the strained bouillon and put it in the fridge within 2 hours; use it within 2 days.

**Record:** the liquid temperature log, the cue and the core reading.

### HEA-1.M5.L5 — Choosing dry or moist heat

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m5-l5 -->
*1 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M3.L1` · moves CK-METH→2, JD-CUES→2, JD-CHSE→1*

*Objectives:* [J] choose a method from the ingredient's structure and the result wanted
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m5-l5 -->

**Why it matters:** a recipe tells you which method to use; a cook knows why. With the reasoning, you can cook an
ingredient you have no recipe for, and choose a different method when you want a different result. This is the first
step of `JD-CHSE`, the competency of choosing.

**You need:** this lesson, your notes from this course, and the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The ingredient's structure decides the method.*
- **Tender, quick-cooking food** (green beans, spinach, thin fish, small pieces of chicken) suits short methods: sauté,
  blanch, steam.
- **Dense roots** (carrot, beetroot, celeriac, potato) need time for the heat to reach the centre: roasting or
  simmering.
- **Delicate fish** breaks up when moved and dries when overcooked: poaching or steaming.
- **Tough, collagen-rich cuts** (shin, cheek, a pork shoulder) need long moist heat to soften. That comes in S2.

*The result wanted decides the medium.*
- **Colour and crust** need dry heat: a surface well above 100 °C (`HEA-1.M1.L2`).
- **A delicate, even texture** needs moist heat.
- **Both:** brown first, then cook in liquid (combination).

The same ingredient can go either way. Carrots roasted are sweet and coloured; carrots steamed are clean-tasting and
tender, a better base for a purée.

*The reasoning in one sentence:* "this ingredient is X, I want Y, so method Z."
- "Cauliflower is dense but tender once cooked; I want nutty colour; so I roast it in florets."
- "Cod is delicate and lean; I want it moist; so I poach it."

**Practice:** theory only (1 h): for ten ingredients, choose a method for two different results, and write the
one-sentence reason for each: carrot, cauliflower, cod fillet, chicken thigh, leek, potato, Brussels sprouts, mackerel,
beetroot and green beans (or kale in winter).

**Look for:** every choice reasoned from the structure and the wanted result, not from habit.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Every vegetable roasted | "Always roast vegetables" | Ask what result you want first |
| A pale, soft dish that should have had colour | Steaming food that should have colour | Colour needs dry heat |
| A method copied from a recipe that didn't fit the ingredient | Choosing by recipe rather than by result | Write the sentence: structure, result, method |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the twenty sentences.

### HEA-1.M5.X2 — Holding water at 70, 85 and 95 °C

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-hea-1-m5-x2 -->
*drill, recipe* — Hold water at 70, 85 and 95 °C for 10 minutes each; record what it looks like.
<!-- END generated:exercise-hea-1-m5-x2 -->

**Task:** hold water at a chosen temperature on your own hob, and learn to read it by eye. You adjust the burner by the
cue first, and only then check the thermometer.

**You need:** a saucepan with about 2 litres of water; the thermometer, with a clip if it has one; a timer; a phone for
photos; the cue log; your hob map (`EQP-1.M2.X1`).

**Recipe:** the drill, for each of 70, 85 and 95 °C in turn.
1. **Heat** the water on a medium setting, the thermometer out of the water.
2. **Judge by the surface** when the target is reached, and write the verdict:
   - **70 °C:** steam rising, tiny bubbles clinging to the base, the surface still;
   - **85 °C:** the surface shivering, small bubbles rising from the base, hardly any breaking;
   - **95 °C:** small bubbles breaking steadily at the surface, not yet rolling.
3. **Read the thermometer**, its tip in the middle of the water, and write the reading next to your verdict.
4. **Hold it for 10 minutes.** Turn the burner down to the setting you think will hold it, and log the temperature every
   2 minutes. Adjust by the surface first, then confirm with the thermometer.
5. **Photograph** the surface at each temperature.
6. Note the setting that held each temperature on your hob, and add it to your hob map.

**Show:** the readings held within about ±3 °C for 10 minutes at each temperature (`TS-MOI`); the cue-then-reading
entries (`JD-CUES` level 2, with the cue items of the quiz); the three photos.

**Time:** 45 min.

### HEA-1.M6.L1 — One ingredient, four methods

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-hea-1-m6-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M5.L5` · moves RD-TEST→1, CK-METH→2*

*Objectives:* [S] cook one vegetable boiled, steamed, sautéed and roasted; [J] compare, conclude, record
<!-- END generated:lesson-hea-1-m6-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** this is your first structured experiment. Cooks improve by testing their ideas, and a test is only
worth something if it is fair and its conclusion reaches beyond the one dish. The lab report you write here is the
template for every experiment in the program.

**You need:** the inputs of `HEA-1.M6.X1`: 1 kg of one vegetable (carrot, cauliflower or celeriac, by season); oil,
butter, salt; the pans, the steamer, a sheet pan; four plates marked underneath; household tasters; the lab report
template.

**Explanation:**

*A fair test changes one thing.* Here it is the method. Everything else stays the same:
- the vegetable, from one batch;
- the cut: the same size for every method;
- the portion: weighed;
- the salt: the same amount per portion.

Salt is the tricky variable, because boiling seasons through the water. Either boil in unsalted water and salt all four
portions the same after cooking, or salt the water and note the difference as a limitation. Decide before you start,
and write it down.

*Observation against conclusion.*
- **An observation** is what you saw and tasted: "the roasted carrots were sweeter and browned."
- **A conclusion** explains it and transfers it: "dry heat concentrates and browns, so dense, sugary roots suit
  roasting."

A conclusion that only says which you liked is a preference, not a conclusion.

*Household tasters,* blind where possible, keep your own preferences in check. A helper puts the portions on marked
plates; the tasters rank them without knowing which is which.

*The lab report:*

| Section | What goes in |
|---|---|
| Question | What you want to find out |
| Variables | What changes (the method) and what is fixed |
| Method | What you did, in steps |
| Observations | Times, photos, tasting notes, the tasters' ranking |
| Conclusion | What it shows, and when each method is the right choice |
| Next | What you would test next |

**Practice:**
1. **The lab (2 h).** Run `HEA-1.M6.X1`: the vegetable boiled, steamed, sautéed and roasted, tasted blind by the
   household, and the report written the same evening.

**Look for:** a conclusion that follows from the observations and names when each method is the right choice.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| One method "wins" for the wrong reason | Different cut sizes per method | One cut for all, measured |
| The boiled batch tastes best because it is saltier | Salting one batch more | Weigh the salt per portion; or note the limitation |
| "I liked the roasted one best" as the conclusion | A conclusion that is only a preference | Explain why, and say when each method fits |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the lab report (in `HEA-1.M6.X1`).

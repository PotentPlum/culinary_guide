**Before you start.** Vegetables are most of a home cook's work, and the part most often cooked by habit: too soft, grey
or watery, or the same three vegetables all year. This course treats them as a subject of their own, the way a
professional kitchen does:
- **structure:** cells, pectin and water, and why heat, acid and alkali change texture;
- **groups:** what a root, a brassica or a leaf has in common in the pan;
- **pigments:** why green turns olive and red cabbage turns blue;
- **the season:** buying by the Dutch calendar, and storing to keep quality;
- **method by group:** matching the method to the vegetable;
- **potatoes:** the preparations of the Dutch kitchen, from boiled to mashed to roast.

It ends with a plate on which three vegetables, cooked three ways, share one clean plate.

*Dutch vegetables, by season.* Every lesson gives a winter and a summer choice, so you can start the course in any
month. The seasonal calendar you build in module 2 is used in every later course. Roasting uses the settings of your own
oven table (`EQP-1.M2.X1`, used in `HEA-1.M4.L1`).

Plan about five weeks. The group dishes of module 4 run over two weeks of dinners, and the market visit fits a Saturday
morning. The vegetables are the household's side dishes and soups; trim goes into stock, and comparison batches become

### VEG-1.M1.L1 — Cells, pectin and water

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M1.L2` · moves IN-VEG→1, SC-STRC→1*

*Objectives:* [K] cell walls, pectin, turgor, starch; what heat, acid and salt do to them [S16]
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** every vegetable texture, from a crisp raw carrot to a silky purée, comes from what is happening to
its cells. Once you know what holds them together and what loosens them, you can predict why beans in a tomato sauce
stay hard, why bicarbonate turns vegetables to mush, and why a limp carrot can be revived.

**You need:** the inputs of `VEG-1.M1.X1`: 500 g of carrots; vinegar; bicarbonate of soda; three saucepans; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*A vegetable is mostly water in cells.* Each cell is a small bag of water inside a stiff wall. The pressure of that
water against the wall is called **turgor**, and it is what makes a raw vegetable crisp. A limp carrot has lost water,
and its cells have gone slack.

*Pectin glues the cells together.* The cell walls are held to each other by **pectin**. Heat dissolves it gradually, so
the cells separate and the vegetable softens. Starchy vegetables, like potatoes and parsnips, also soften as their
starch absorbs water and swells [S16].

*What changes the speed of softening:*

| Factor | Effect | In the kitchen |
|---|---|---|
| Acid (tomato, wine, vinegar) | Slows the breakdown of pectin | Vegetables cooked in it stay firm |
| Alkali (bicarbonate) | Speeds it | Vegetables turn mushy |
| Calcium | Firms the cell walls | Hard tap water slows softening a little |
| Salt | Speeds it slightly, and seasons | Salted water is still the default |

This is why beans or potatoes added to a tomato sauce take much longer to soften: add the tomato later, or cook them
first. And it is why the old trick of bicarbonate "for colour" ruins texture.

**Practice:**
1. **Three waters (60 min).** Run the comparison of `VEG-1.M1.X1`: the same carrot sticks for 15 minutes in plain,
   acidic and alkaline water. Before you taste, write down the order of softness you expect, and why.

**Look for:** your prediction of the order of softness written before tasting, and checked.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Beans still hard after an hour in the sauce | Tomatoes added too early to beans that must soften | Soften the beans first, then add the tomato |
| Mushy, slippery vegetables | Bicarbonate added "for colour" | Short cooking and an open pan keep the colour (`VEG-1.M3.L1`) |
| No difference between the batches | Different stick sizes, or different times | One cut, one timer, three pans side by side |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** your prediction and the result (in `VEG-1.M1.X1`).

### VEG-1.M1.L2 — The vegetable groups

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m1-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · moves IN-VEG→2*

*Objectives:* [K] roots, tubers, brassicas, alliums, leaves, stems, squash, fruit vegetables, pods and seeds
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** there are dozens of vegetables, but only a handful of groups, and the group predicts most of what a
vegetable will do in the pan. Learn the groups, and a vegetable you have never cooked is no longer a mystery.

**You need:** this lesson, this week's vegetables (or a list of them), and the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The groups, and what each has in common in the kitchen:*

| Group | Examples | In the kitchen |
|---|---|---|
| Roots | Carrot, beetroot, parsnip, celeriac | Dense and sweet; need time; roast, glaze, purée |
| Tubers | Potato, Jerusalem artichoke | Starchy; the variety decides the texture (`VEG-1.M5`) |
| Brassicas | Cabbages, Brussels sprouts, kale, cauliflower, broccoli | Turn sulphurous when overcooked; short cooking, or long braising |
| Alliums | Onion, leek, garlic, shallot | The base of most dishes; sweat, brown or caramelise |
| Leaves | Spinach, lettuces, chard, lamb's lettuce | Cook in seconds and shrink hugely; or eaten raw |
| Stems and shoots | Asparagus, celery, fennel | Fibrous outside, tender inside; trim and peel |
| Squash | Pumpkins and winter squash | Dense and sweet; roast, purée |
| Fruit vegetables | Tomato, courgette, pepper, cucumber | Very watery; cook hot and fast, or cook down |
| Pods and seeds | Beans, peas, broad beans | Sweet when young; quick cooking |

*The group predicts* density, water, sugar and the risk of overcooking. Dense roots take long, high heat; leaves take
seconds; brassicas punish the middle ground.

*Basic preparation by group.*
- **Roots:** scrub young ones, peel old or waxed ones; not everything needs peeling.
- **Brassicas:** trim the base and outer leaves; halve sprouts; cut cauliflower into florets.
- **Leaves:** wash in a large bowl of water and lift them out, so the grit stays on the bottom; never pour them out
  with the water.
- **Pods:** top and tail beans, and string them if they have strings.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): sort this week's vegetables, or a list of twelve from the seasonal calendar, into
groups. For each group, write the main cooking consequence and one method that suits it, and how you would prepare each
vegetable before cooking.

**Look for:** any vegetable placed in its group, with the method consequence.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Broccoli and spinach cooked the same way | Treating all "green vegetables" alike | Ask the group: brassica or leaf? |
| Flavour and time lost to peeling | Peeling everything | Scrub young roots and new potatoes |
| Grit in the spinach | Washing leaves under a running tap in the sink | A bowl of water; lift the leaves out |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`). Soil on vegetables is washed off before they meet the
board you use for ready-to-eat food (`SAF-1.M2`).

**Record:** the sorted list with a consequence and a method per group.

### VEG-1.M2.L1 — Seasons and freshness

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m2-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `VEG-1.M1.L2` · moves IN-SEL→2*

*Objectives:* [K] the local seasonal calendar; [J] judge freshness (firmness, colour, smell, cut ends)
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a vegetable in season is cheaper, fresher and better. Cooking by the season also widens what you
cook: the Dutch calendar runs from kale and chicory to asparagus to beans and back. And at the shop, the freshest of
several bunches is worth picking.

**You need:** a Dutch seasonal calendar (from a greengrocer, a vegetable box scheme or the program); the journal; a
recent shopping receipt or your usual list.

**Explanation:**

*The Dutch seasonal calendar,* in outline:
- **Winter:** storage roots (carrot, beetroot, celeriac, parsnip) and cabbages, kale (*boerenkool*), Brussels sprouts,
  leeks, chicory (*witlof*).
- **Spring:** asparagus (*asperges*, roughly April to late June; the season traditionally ends around 24 June), early
  greens and radishes.
- **Summer:** beans, courgettes, tomatoes, cucumbers, lettuces.
- **Autumn:** squash, cauliflower, the first cabbages, and the start of the storage season.

In November, for example, the list runs to kale, Brussels sprouts, leeks, red, white and savoy cabbage, celeriac,
carrots, beetroot, parsnip, pumpkin, chicory, Jerusalem artichoke and lamb's lettuce. Keep your calendar in the journal,
and add to it as the year goes round.

*Judging freshness:*
- **firmness:** roots and stems that don't bend;
- **colour:** bright, not faded;
- **smell:** fresh, not sour or musty;
- **cut ends:** moist, not dried out (asparagus, leeks), because cut ends dry with time;
- **leaves:** without yellowing or slime;
- **beans:** they snap crisply.

*Choosing by use.* Not everything needs the best. A slightly limp carrot is fine for soup or stock; a raw salad needs
the crispest. Matching quality to use saves money and waste.

**Practice:**
1. **The calendar (45 min).** From the seasonal calendar, build this month's list of vegetables in the journal. Mark the
   ones you already cook and the ones that are new. Compare it with your last shopping receipt: what did you buy out of
   season? Write a shopping list for next week that matches the season, with the freshness sign to look for beside each
   item.

**Look for:** a shopping list that matches the season; the freshness signs named for each item.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Expensive, tasteless tomatoes in January | Buying by recipe instead of by season | Start the week's menu from the calendar |
| A large, woody vegetable | Choosing the biggest instead of the freshest | Firm, bright and heavy for its size |
| Old asparagus or leeks | Ignoring the cut ends | Look at the cut: moist and pale is fresh |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** this month's list, and next week's seasonal shopping list.

### VEG-1.M2.L2 — Storing vegetables

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m2-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAF-1.M3.L1` · moves IN-STO→3, KM-WAST→2*

*Objectives:* [S] fridge, cool and room storage; ethylene; reviving limp vegetables; using them up
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** vegetables keep their quality for days or weeks when stored well, and lose it overnight when stored
badly. Good storage is also the cheapest way to waste less: most of the vegetables a household throws away were
forgotten, or kept in the wrong place.

**You need:** this week's vegetables; bags that let a little moisture out (perforated, or a paper bag inside a plastic
one); a cool, dark cupboard or a box in a cool place; masking tape and a pen; a limp carrot and a tired head of lettuce;
two bowls of cold water; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Where each keeps:*

| Place | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fridge drawer (the fridge set to 4 °C) | Most vegetables: roots, brassicas, leaves, beans, leeks | Cold slows ageing; a loose bag keeps them from drying without making them wet |
| Cool, dark and dry, not the fridge | Potatoes, onions, squash | The cold turns potato starch to sugar; light turns potatoes green |
| Room temperature | Tomatoes | They lose flavour in the cold |

Keep potatoes and onions apart: each speeds the other's spoiling.

*Ethylene.* Apples, pears and ripening tomatoes release ethylene, a gas that speeds ripening and ageing. It yellows
broccoli and leaves and makes cucumbers soft. Keep them in a different place from the vegetable drawer.

*Reviving.* Limp roots and leaves regain crispness in cold water, because the cells take water back in (`VEG-1.M1.L1`).
Stand a carrot in a glass of cold water, or plunge lettuce leaves into a bowl of cold water, for 30–60 minutes.

*Using them up.*
- **First in, first out:** new vegetables go behind the old ones.
- **The tired ones** go into soup, stock or a stir-fry.
- **Opened or half-used vegetables** are wrapped, labelled with the date, and used first.
- **A weekly waste log** (`KM-WAST`): what you threw away, and why.

**Practice:**
1. **Storage and revival (45 min).** Store this week's vegetables by the rules: fridge drawer, cool store or counter.
   Label anything opened with the date. Photograph the fridge drawer and the cool store. Then revive the limp carrot and
   the lettuce in cold water, and compare them before and after.

**Look for:** the revived vegetables crisp again; the fridge drawer organised; nothing forgotten at the back.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Potatoes that taste sweet and brown too fast when fried | Potatoes in the fridge | A cool, dark cupboard |
| Sprouting potatoes, soft onions | Onions and potatoes stored together | Separate places |
| Mealy, tasteless tomatoes | Tomatoes in the fridge | On the counter, eaten within a few days |
| Yellow broccoli | Apples in the vegetable drawer | Fruit elsewhere |

**Safety:** cut or cooked vegetables go into the fridge within 2 hours and are used within 2 days (assessment §9).
Discard anything mouldy or slimy.

**Record:** the storage photos, and the before and after of the revival.

### VEG-1.M3.L1 — Pigments

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m3-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `VEG-1.M1.L1` · moves IN-VEG→2*

*Objectives:* [K] chlorophyll, anthocyanins, carotenoids under acid, alkali and time; [S] keep greens green
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** colour is the first thing anyone notices on a plate, and it tells you how a vegetable was cooked.
Olive-grey beans, blue red cabbage and orange-stained butter all have a simple chemical cause, and each can be
controlled.

**You need:** 400 g of red cabbage, finely shredded; 2 tablespoons of vinegar; 300 g of green beans (or broccoli, or
Brussels sprouts in winter); salt; two small pans with lids and a large pan; a timer; a phone.

**Explanation:**

*Chlorophyll* (green):
- turns olive with long cooking and with acid;
- a lid traps the vegetable's own volatile acids, which then dull the colour;
- short cooking in plenty of boiling water, uncovered, then shocking or spreading out to cool, keeps it bright
  (`HEA-1.M5.L2`).

*Anthocyanins* (red, purple, blue: red cabbage, beetroot, red onion):
- red in acid, blue or grey in alkali;
- this is why Dutch red cabbage is cooked with apple or vinegar;
- hard tap water, which is slightly alkaline, can turn it bluish.

*Carotenoids* (orange and yellow: carrot, squash, pepper) are stable to acid and heat. They dissolve in fat, which is
why carrots cooked in butter colour the butter orange, and why a little fat makes their colour glow.

| Pigment | Acid | Alkali | Long cooking | Fat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorophyll | Olive | Bright but mushy | Olive | — |
| Anthocyanins | Red | Blue-grey | Fades | — |
| Carotenoids | Stable | Stable | Stable | Dissolves and carries the colour |

**Practice:**
1. **Colour tests (45 min).** *Red cabbage:* divide it between the two small pans with 150 ml of water each; add the
   vinegar to one; cover both and cook gently for 20 minutes. *Beans:* bring the large pan of salted water to the boil;
   cook one half of the beans for 4 minutes and the other for 12, uncovered. Before lifting each lid or draining each
   batch, write the colour you predict. Photograph them side by side.

**Look for:** your prediction of each colour written before lifting the lid, and checked.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Grey beans | Lemon juice in the green beans while they cook | Acid at the table, not in the pan |
| Blue-grey red cabbage | Red cabbage with no acid | Apple and vinegar from the start |
| Dull, olive greens | A lid on greens | Uncovered, at least for the first minutes |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the predictions and the photos.

### VEG-1.M3.L2 — Texture and firmness

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m3-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `VEG-1.M1.L1` · moves IN-VEG→2, SC-STRC→2*

*Objectives:* [K] why acid and calcium keep vegetables firm; overcooking; [J] choose a doneness
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** "cook until tender" hides a choice. A cauliflower for a salad, for a purée and for a gratin needs
three different donenesses. Deciding the texture before you cook, and then cooking to it, is what separates a cook from
someone following a timer.

**You need:** 600 g of one vegetable: cauliflower or carrots in winter, green beans in summer; a saucepan of salted
water; a slotted spoon; a small knife; three plates; a phone; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Acid and calcium keep vegetables firm* (`VEG-1.M1.L1`):
- potatoes in a tomato stew stay firm long after potatoes in water would have softened;
- vegetables cooked in hard, calcium-rich water soften a little more slowly;
- pickles stay crisp in their acid.

Use it deliberately: add potatoes earlier, or tomatoes later, if you want the potatoes soft.

*Overcooking.* Past the doneness you wanted, texture is lost for good, and so are colour and flavour, which leach into
the water. Brassicas turn sulphurous. A vegetable left in the hot pan, or in a hot colander, keeps cooking: carryover
works on vegetables too (`HEA-1.M1.L3`).

*Choosing a doneness:*

| Doneness | The knife | The bite | For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tender-crisp | Goes in with a little resistance in the centre | Crisp, with bite | A salad, a side with bite |
| Just tender | Goes in easily, the piece holds its shape | Tender, still whole | A vegetable that will cook further in a sauce or gratin |
| Fully tender | No resistance; the piece breaks when pressed | Soft throughout | A purée, a soup |

Decide the doneness before cooking, write it down, and cook to it. Test with the knife, then bite a piece: the two
together are more reliable than either alone.

**Practice:**
1. **Doneness (45 min).** Cut the vegetable into even pieces (cauliflower florets, carrot rounds or beans). Put it all
   into the boiling salted water. Lift out a third at tender-crisp, a third at just tender and the rest at fully tender,
   testing with the knife and by biting before each. Note the times. Photograph the three on their plates, and one piece
   of each cut in half.

**Look for:** three clearly different textures, each matching its target.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Every vegetable soft | One doneness for every use | Write the target before cooking |
| Hard one day, soft the next | Cooking by the clock only | Knife and bite; the time is only a guide |
| Tender-crisp in the pan, soft on the plate | Forgetting the carryover in a vegetable left in the hot pan | Out of the water at once; spread out or shock |

**Safety:** lift the vegetables out with the slotted spoon rather than carrying the pan of boiling water (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the times and the photos, with the target for each.

### VEG-1.M4.L1 — Roots

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m4-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M4.L1` · moves TS-VEG→2*

*Objectives:* [S] roast, glaze (*glacer*), purée; [J] cut size versus method
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** roots are the backbone of the Dutch winter kitchen, and three methods cover most of what they can
do: roasting for depth, glazing for a shiny side dish, and a purée for silk. Glazing also teaches you to cook by a
ratio, so that the sauce and the vegetable are ready at the same moment.

**You need:** for session 1, 1 kg of carrots, 50 g of butter, 20 g of sugar, salt, two wide pans with lids; for session
2, 600 g of beetroot or parsnip, oil, salt, a sheet pan, and for the purée 600 g of celeriac, 30 g of butter, about 100
ml of milk or cream, a blender or a masher.

**Explanation:**

*Roasting* (`HEA-1.M4.L1`) concentrates and browns: larger chunks, oiled, spaced, at your oven table's setting for
roots.

*Glazing* (*glacer*, *glaceren*) cooks small, even pieces in a little liquid with butter and a pinch of sugar, partly
covered, until the liquid has reduced to a shiny coating just as the vegetables become tender. The ratio of vegetable to
liquid decides whether the two arrive together:

| Per 100 g of vegetable | Amount |
|---|---|
| Liquid (water or stock) | 40 ml |
| Butter | 5 g |
| Sugar | 2 g |
| Salt | A small pinch |

For 500 g of carrots: 200 ml of water, 25 g of butter, 10 g of sugar. Cook partly covered over medium heat until the
carrots are nearly tender, then uncover and let the liquid reduce, shaking the pan, until it coats them.

*Purée.* Cook the vegetable fully tender (boiled or steamed), drain it very well and let it steam dry for a minute, then
blend or press it. Enrich with butter or cream, and season. Water is the enemy: a wet vegetable gives a thin purée.

*Cut size and method:* small, even pieces for glazing (bâtonnets or rounds, `KNF-1.M3`); larger chunks for roasting; any
size for a purée.

**Practice:**
1. **Glazed carrots (60 min).** Cut the carrots into even rounds or bâtonnets and divide them into two batches of 500 g.
   Glaze batch 1 by the ratio (200 ml of water) and batch 2 with twice the liquid (400 ml), the butter and sugar the
   same. Note which finishes first in each pan, the liquid or the carrots, and photograph both.
2. **Roast and purée (75 min).** Roast the beetroot or parsnip in chunks. Meanwhile, cook the celeriac in cubes until
   fully tender, drain and dry it, and blend it with the butter and enough warm milk for a purée that holds a soft peak.
   Season both.

**Look for:** the glaze coats the carrots as they turn tender; the purée is smooth and holds a soft peak.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Soft carrots in a pool of liquid | Too much liquid, so the vegetables are soft before it reduces | Less liquid by the ratio; meanwhile, lift the carrots out and reduce |
| Burnt glaze, hard carrots | Too little liquid | Add a splash of water and lower the heat |
| A thin, watery purée | Vegetables not drained or dried | Drain well; steam dry before blending |

**Safety:** a sugar glaze burns quickly once the water has gone: shake the pan and watch it. Use oven gloves for the
sheet pan (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the two glazing results with your correction, and the photos.

### VEG-1.M4.L2 — Brassicas

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m4-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M5.L2` · moves TS-VEG→2*

*Objectives:* [S] blanch, roast, sauté and braise cabbage, sprouts and kale
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** brassicas have a bad name, mostly from overcooking: grey sprouts and sulphurous cabbage. Cooked
fast, or cooked long and gently with acid, they are some of the best vegetables of the Dutch winter.

**You need:** for session 1, 500 g of Brussels sprouts and a cauliflower; for session 2, half a white or savoy cabbage
and a small red cabbage, with a sour apple (such as a Goudreinet), an onion, 2 tablespoons of vinegar, 1 tablespoon of
sugar, a bay leaf and a clove (in summer: a pointed cabbage, *spitskool*, and a kohlrabi); oil; butter; salt; a large
pan of salted water; the sauté pan; a heavy pan with a lid; sheet pans.

**Explanation:**

*Four methods for brassicas:*
- **Blanch:** fast, in plenty of boiling salted water, for green brassicas like broccoli and kale (`HEA-1.M5.L2`).
- **Roast:** halved sprouts laid cut-side down; cauliflower in florets. Browning adds sweetness and limits the sulphur.
- **Sauté:** shredded cabbage or sprouts, quick and hot, in batches (`HEA-1.M3.L1`).
- **Braise:** white or red cabbage cooked slowly with a little liquid, fat and acid until silky.

*The sulphur problem.* Brassicas hold sulphur compounds that are released, and multiply, as they cook. The way round
them:
- **short cooking** (a few minutes), before many have formed;
- **long, gentle braising with acid**, which softens the cabbage and mellows them.

The middle ground, 15–20 minutes of boiling, smells worst.

*Kale* (*boerenkool*) is tough: strip the leaves from the stems, blanch for 3–4 minutes, squeeze, then sauté with
garlic. Undercooked it is tough; cooked to mush, it is grey.

*Braised red cabbage.* Sweat the sliced onion in butter, add the shredded cabbage, the grated apple, the vinegar, sugar,
bay, clove, salt and 100 ml of water. Cover and cook gently for about an hour, stirring now and then, until silky. The
acid keeps it red (`VEG-1.M3.L1`).

**Practice:**
1. **Sprouts and cauliflower (60 min).** Blanch half the sprouts until just tender. Halve the rest, toss them in oil and
   salt, and roast them cut-side down beside the cauliflower florets. Compare the two sprouts.
2. **Cabbage (60 min).** Sauté the shredded white or savoy cabbage in batches in a hot pan. Braise the red cabbage with
   apple. In summer: sauté the pointed cabbage, and make a slaw of shredded kohlrabi with a little vinegar, oil and
   salt.

**Look for:** roasted sprouts browned on the cut side and tender; braised red cabbage glossy and deep red.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Grey, sulphurous sprouts | Sprouts boiled to grey | A few minutes, or roast them |
| Tough or mushy kale | Kale undercooked, or cooked to mush | Blanch 3–4 minutes, then sauté |
| Blue, dull red cabbage | Red cabbage without acid | Apple and vinegar from the start |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`). The braised red cabbage keeps 2 days in the fridge and
reheats well.

**Record:** photos of each dish, and a line comparing the blanched and the roasted sprouts.

### VEG-1.M4.L3 — Leaves

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m4-l3 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M3.L1` · moves TS-VEG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] wash and dry; wilt and sauté greens; [J] cook down volume
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m4-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** leaves cook in seconds and shrink to almost nothing, which surprises every cook once. Washed and
dried well, and cooked just until wilted, they are bright, glossy and full of flavour. Wet or overcooked, they are slimy
and sit in a puddle.

**You need:** for session 1, 500 g of spinach; for session 2, a bunch of chard (summer and autumn) or 400 g of kale
(winter); 2 cloves of garlic; oil and butter; salt; a large bowl; a salad spinner or a clean tea towel; the sauté pan;
the scale; a sieve.

**Explanation:**

*Washing and drying.* Wash leaves in a large bowl of water, swish them, and lift them out, so the grit stays on the
bottom (`VEG-1.M1.L2`). Repeat if the water is gritty. Dry them in a salad spinner, or roll them in a tea towel. Wet
leaves steam in the pan instead of sautéing, and they dilute a dressing.

*Wilting.* Spinach and chard collapse to a small fraction of their raw volume. A pan piled high with leaves gives a
small portion. For four people as a side, plan on about 1 kg of raw spinach. Add the leaves in handfuls, each one
wilting before the next goes in.

*Sautéing greens.* A hot pan, a little fat and sliced garlic; the leaves turned with tongs until just wilted. Then the
water they release is squeezed out for a side dish, or kept for a soup.

*Chard* has thick stems that take much longer than its leaves. Cut the stems out, slice them and cook them first; add
the leaves near the end. Kale is the same: stripped from its stems, and the stems either discarded or cooked separately.

*Weigh before and after.* It is the quickest way to learn how much to buy.

**Practice:**
1. **Spinach (45 min).** Wash and dry the spinach and weigh it. Wilt half in a dry, hot pan, turning it with tongs.
   Sauté the other half with garlic in a little oil. Weigh both after squeezing gently in a sieve, and note the fraction
   left. Taste them side by side.
2. **Chard or kale (45 min).** Separate the stems from the leaves. Cook the sliced stems first in a little butter until
   tender, then add the leaves and turn until just wilted. Season.

**Look for:** bright green, glossy, just wilted; no puddle on the plate.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Spinach stewing in grey water | Wet leaves in the pan | Spin or towel them dry |
| A spoonful for four | Too little bought | About 1 kg raw spinach for four |
| Slimy, dark leaves | Overcooking until slimy | Off the heat as soon as they wilt |
| Raw stems, mushy leaves | Stems and leaves cooked together | Stems first, leaves at the end |

**Safety:** wash leaves well: soil can carry bacteria (`SAF-1.M2`). Cooked spinach goes into the fridge within 2 hours
and is eaten within 2 days.

**Record:** the raw and cooked weights, and the photos.

### VEG-1.M4.L4 — Alliums

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m4-l4 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M2.L2` · moves TS-VEG→3, SC-BRWN→2*

*Objectives:* [S] sweat versus brown versus caramelise onions and leeks; [K] what each gives a dish
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m4-l4 -->

**Why it matters:** onions start more dishes than any other vegetable, and how you cook them sets the flavour of
everything that follows. The same onion can be sweet and mild, savoury and deep, or jammy and rich: three different
foundations from one ingredient.

**You need:** 3 onions; a leek; 3 tablespoons of oil or butter; salt; three small pans, or one used three times; a lid;
about 300 ml of hot stock; three cups; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Three ways of cooking onions, and what each gives a dish:*

| Method | How | Result | For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sweating | Low heat, a little fat, often a lid; about 10 minutes | Soft and translucent, no colour; sweet and mild | Soups, risotto, white sauces |
| Browning | Medium-high heat, stirred now and then; about 8–10 minutes | Colour on the edges; savoury depth | Stews, gravies, a pan sauce |
| Caramelising | Long and slow, moderate heat, splashes of water to lift the fond; 30–45 minutes (`HEA-1.M2.L2`) | Deep, sweet and jammy | A topping, an onion soup |

A pinch of salt at the start of sweating draws out moisture and helps the onion soften without colour.

*Leeks* sweat sweet and silky, but their thin layers burn easily. They are best sweated, braised, or grilled after
blanching. Wash them well: slit the green part lengthwise and rinse between the layers, where soil collects.

*Why it matters in a dish.* A delicate white soup made with browned onions turns beige and tastes of stew. A beef stew
made with sweated onions tastes flat. The method of the onion is chosen for the dish.

**Practice:**
1. **Three onions (60 min).** Slice the onions the same way. Sweat one, brown one and caramelise one. Photograph the
   three side by side. Put a spoonful of each into a cup of hot stock, and taste the three cups. Then sweat the sliced
   leek and taste it.

**Look for:** three distinct colours and flavours, none burnt.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Sweated" onions with brown edges | "Sweating" at high heat, which browns them | Low heat, a lid, a pinch of salt |
| A grey-beige white sauce | Browning leeks for a white sauce | Sweat them, without colour |
| Burnt edges, raw centres | Rushing the caramelising | Moderate heat, 30–45 minutes |

**Safety:** caramelising onions need watching at the end; take the pan off the heat at the first acrid smell
(`SAF-1.M4.L2`).

**Record:** the photo of the three onions and a tasting line for each cup.

### VEG-1.M4.L5 — Squash and fruit vegetables

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m4-l5 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M4.L1` · moves TS-VEG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] roast squash; cook tomatoes, courgette and peppers without making them watery
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m4-l5 -->

**Why it matters:** squash and the fruit vegetables are opposites. Squash is dense and sweet and rewards long roasting;
courgettes, tomatoes and peppers are mostly water and turn to a watery stew unless you cook them hot and fast or cook
the water away.

**You need:** in autumn and winter, a squash of about 1 kg (a butternut, *flespompoen*, or a red kuri, *potimarron*),
oil, salt, a sheet pan; in summer, also 2 courgettes, 500 g of tomatoes and 2 peppers; the sauté pan; tongs; a bowl with
a plate to cover it.

**Explanation:**

*Squash* is dense and sweet. It roasts well in wedges or cubes, and purées and makes soup well. Cut it safely: a flat
side first, on a stable board (`KNF-1.M4.L2`). Red kuri skin softens in the oven and can be eaten; butternut is usually
peeled.

*Fruit vegetables* (courgette, tomato, pepper) are very watery:
- **Courgette:** salt the slices and let them drain in a colander for 20 minutes, or cook them hot and fast, in batches,
  in one layer, so they brown instead of stewing.
- **Tomatoes:** cooked down slowly to concentrate them into a sauce, or roasted, halved, until their edges caramelise.
- **Peppers:** charred whole over a gas flame, under the grill or in a hot oven until the skin blackens, then covered in
  a bowl for 10 minutes so the skin loosens, and peeled. Or roasted in strips.

*Seasonality.* Squash is an autumn and winter vegetable; courgettes, tomatoes and peppers are summer ones. Out of
season, this lesson uses squash and stored roots, and the fruit-vegetable part waits for summer, with a note in the
journal to do it then.

**Practice:**
1. **Roast and sauté (75 min).** Cut the squash into wedges, toss with oil and salt, and roast at your oven table's
   setting for roots until tender and caramelised at the edges, turning once. In summer, also: sauté the courgette in
   batches in a hot pan; roast the halved tomatoes; char and peel a pepper.

**Look for:** squash caramelised at the edges and tender; courgette browned, not watery.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Courgette grey and wet | A crowded pan of courgette in its own water | One layer, high heat, in batches |
| Squash hard in the middle | Undercooked squash | Smaller wedges, or longer; test with a knife |
| Peppers that taste of little | Peppers peeled under running water, which washes away flavour | Peel by hand; wipe off the black bits |

**Safety:** cut squash on a stable board, flat side down; char peppers with long tongs and keep your sleeves clear of
the flame (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the photos, and, out of season, the note for summer.

### VEG-1.M5.L1 — Potato types and starch

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m5-l1 -->
*1 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `VEG-1.M1.L1` · moves IN-STA→1, IN-VEG→2*

*Objectives:* [K] waxy versus floury; starch and texture; storage and greening (solanine)
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the Netherlands grows and eats potatoes like few other countries, and the bag tells you how each
variety will behave. Choose the wrong class and the mash turns gluey or the salad falls apart, however well you cook.

**You need:** this lesson; three bags of potatoes, or their labels or descriptions online; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The Dutch classes* are printed on the bag:

| Class | Texture when cooked | For |
|---|---|---|
| *Vastkokend* (waxy) | Holds its shape, firm and smooth | Salads, boiled potatoes, gratins, pan-fried slices |
| *Iets kruimig* (slightly floury) | Mostly holds its shape, a little dry | All-round: boiled, roast, pan-fried |
| *Kruimig* (floury) | Falls apart into a light, dry texture | Mash, roast potatoes, chips |

Varieties you will see include Annabelle and Nicola (*vastkokend*) and Eigenheimer (*kruimig*), but the label is what
counts.

*Starch.* Floury potatoes have more starch, and when cooked their cells separate easily, giving a light, crumbly
texture. In waxy potatoes the cells stay together, so they hold their shape.

*Storage.* Cool, dark and dry, not in the fridge (`VEG-1.M2.L2`). The cold turns starch to sugar, so the potatoes taste
sweet and brown too fast when fried.

*Greening.* Light makes potatoes green, and the greening goes with a rise in **solanine**, a bitter natural toxin.
- Cut green parts and sprouts away generously.
- Discard potatoes that are very green.
- A bitter taste in a cooked potato is a warning: don't eat it.

Peeling thinly does not remove the solanine under a green patch.

**Practice:** theory only (1 h): read the labels of three potato varieties, put each in its class, and plan a use for
each: one for mash, one for a salad and one for roasting. Then check where your potatoes are stored at home, and move
them if needed.

**Look for:** the right class chosen for mash, salad and roast without looking it up.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Gluey mash | Waxy potatoes for mash | *Kruimig* for mash |
| A potato salad that falls apart | Floury potatoes for a salad | *Vastkokend* for salads |
| A bitter potato | Green potatoes used as they are | Cut the green away generously, or discard |

**Safety:** green patches and sprouts are cut away generously, and very green potatoes are discarded; they contain
solanine, a natural toxin.

**Record:** the three varieties with their class and use.

### VEG-1.M5.L2 — Boiled and mashed

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m5-l2 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `VEG-1.M5.L1` · moves TS-VEG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] start in cold water; mash without going gluey; *purée*
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** boiled potatoes and mash are on Dutch tables more than any other dish, and both are easy to do
badly: falling apart outside and hard inside, or a mash that is lumpy, wet or gluey. The fixes are small and exact.

**You need:** for session 1, 750 g of waxy potatoes; for session 2, 1 kg of floury potatoes, about 150 ml of milk, 40 g
of butter, nutmeg, and the next time 500 g of floury potatoes and 100 g of butter for a *purée*; salt; a saucepan; a
masher, and a ricer if you have one; a small pan for the milk; a knife.

**Explanation:**

*Boiled potatoes* start in cold, salted water (10 g/l, `TST-1.M2.L3`), brought to the boil and then simmered, so the
outside and the centre cook evenly. In boiling water from the start, the outside falls apart before the centre is done.
They are done when a knife slides in without resistance. Drain them, and put them back in the hot pan off the heat for a
minute, shaking, so the surface steams dry.

*Mash.*
1. **Floury potatoes,** peeled and cut into even pieces, boiled from cold until fully tender.
2. **Drained and dried** in the pan for a minute.
3. **Mashed or riced while hot.** Cold potatoes go lumpy.
4. **Warm milk and butter** worked in gently with a spoon; a pinch of nutmeg and salt.

Overworking bursts the cells and releases their starch into a gluey paste. A blender or a food processor does that in
seconds. Use a masher or a ricer.

*Purée* (*pommes purée*) is the fine version: the potatoes riced, then beaten over low heat with a lot of butter (about
100 g per 500 g of potatoes, in cold cubes), and loosened with a little warm milk, for a silky, smooth texture.

**Practice:**
1. **Boiled (45 min).** Peel the waxy potatoes, or scrub them if they are new, and cut them to an even size. Cover with
   cold water by 2 cm, add the salt, bring to the boil, and simmer until a knife slides in. Drain and steam dry.
   Photograph a potato cut in half.
2. **Mashed (60 min).** Make the mash from the floury potatoes. On another day, make the riced *purée* and compare.

**Look for:** a knife slides in without resistance; a fluffy, smooth mash with no lumps and no glue.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Ragged outside, hard centre | Potatoes into boiling water | Start them in cold water |
| A cool, lumpy mash | Cold milk | Warm the milk first |
| Gluey, elastic mash | A blender for mash | A masher or a ricer, and gentle mixing |
| Watery mash | Undrained, watery potatoes | Drain well and steam dry |

**Safety:** drain the potatoes in a colander in the sink, pouring away from you (`SAF-1.M4`). Leftover potatoes go into
the fridge within 2 hours and are eaten within 2 days.

**Record:** the photos, and a line comparing the mash and the *purée*.

### VEG-1.M5.L3 — Roast and pan-fried

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-veg-1-m5-l3 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `VEG-1.M5.L1`, `HEA-1.M3.L1` · moves TS-VEG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] parboil and rough up for roasting; pan-fried and sautéed potatoes
<!-- END generated:lesson-veg-1-m5-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a crisp roast potato and a pan of golden *gebakken aardappelen* are among the best things a potato
can become, and both are made by preparing the surface: roughed up for roasting, cooked and cooled for the pan.

**You need:** for session 1, 1.2 kg of floury potatoes, 60 ml of neutral oil, salt, a sheet pan or roasting tin, a
colander; for session 2, about 600 g of potatoes boiled the day before and cooled in the fridge, 2 tablespoons of oil,
15 g of butter, salt and pepper, the skillet or sauté pan, a fish spatula.

**Explanation:**

*Roast potatoes.*
1. **Floury potatoes,** peeled and cut into chunks of about 5 cm.
2. **Parboiled:** started in cold salted water, brought to the boil and simmered until the edges are soft but the
   centre is still firm, about 8 minutes.
3. **Drained and shaken** in the colander or the pan to rough up the edges. The rough, starchy surface is what becomes
   the crisp crust.
4. **Into hot fat in a hot oven:** heat the oil in the tin in the oven at your oven table's roasting setting for about
   10 minutes, then add the potatoes, turn them in the fat, and roast for 45–60 minutes, turning once or twice, until
   deep golden.

*Pan-fried potatoes* (*gebakken aardappelen*) are made from boiled potatoes, cooled, ideally overnight in the fridge.
Cold, cooked potatoes hold together and brown evenly. Sautéed raw slices are possible, but they need a lid first to cook
through, then high heat to brown.

*Patience.* Potatoes stick at first and release once a crust forms. Turned too early, they break and leave their crust
in the pan. Leave them for 4–5 minutes before the first turn.

**Practice:**
1. **Roast (60 min).** Parboil and rough up two-thirds of the potatoes. Cut the rest into plain cubes of the same size,
   not parboiled. Roast both in the hot fat on the same tray, and compare the crust and the inside.
2. **Pan-fried (45 min).** Slice yesterday's boiled potatoes about 1 cm thick. Heat the oil and butter in the pan over
   medium-high heat, add the slices in one layer, and leave them until golden underneath before turning. Season.

**Look for:** a deep golden, crisp crust and a fluffy inside; the pan-fried slices golden on both sides and intact.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pale, greasy roast potatoes | Fat not hot enough | Heat the fat in the tin in the oven first |
| Steamed, soft potatoes | A crowded tray | Space between them; two tins if needed |
| Broken slices, crust left in the pan | Turning too early, which breaks the slices | Wait until they release |

**Safety:** the tin of hot fat is the dangerous moment: take it out with oven gloves, set it on the hob, and add the
potatoes with a spoon, away from you; never add wet potatoes. Boiled potatoes for the pan go into the fridge within 2
hours of cooking.

**Record:** the photos of the two roast batches and the pan-fried slices.

### VEG-1.M5.X1 — Mash from a waxy and a floury variety

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-veg-1-m5-x1 -->
*comparison, recipe* — Mash made from a waxy and a floury variety.
<!-- END generated:exercise-veg-1-m5-x1 -->

**Task:** see why the variety decides the result, with the method held constant: two varieties, the same weight, water,
salt, butter and milk.

**You need:** 750 g each of a waxy (*vastkokend*) and a floury (*kruimig*) potato; 40 g of butter; 150 ml of milk;
salt; two saucepans of the same size; a masher; a small pan for the milk; two bowls marked underneath; a helper; a
phone; the tasting form.

**Recipe:**
1. **Prepare.** Peel both varieties and cut them into pieces of about 4 cm, the same size.
2. **Boil from cold, side by side:** each in its pan with 1.5 litres of cold water and 15 g of salt. Simmer until a
   knife slides in. Note the time of each.
3. **Drain and dry** each in its pan for a minute.
4. **Mash both the same way:** the masher, then 20 g of butter and 75 ml of warm milk each, worked in gently. Taste and
   add the same pinch of salt to each if needed.
5. **Photograph** them side by side, and a spoonful of each lifted from the bowl.
6. **Blind tasting.** The helper puts them in the marked bowls. You and the household taste both, and say which is the
   better mash and why.
7. **Explain** the difference by starch and cells.

**Show:**
- `TS-VEG`: the floury mash smooth and fluffy, one of the six vegetables of level 3.
- `IN-VEG`: the potatoes chosen and prepared correctly for their use, with this module's quiz at 80 %.

**Time:** 1 h; the mash covers two meals, and the waxy mash becomes potato cakes.

*Objectives:* [S] three seasonal vegetables, three methods, one clean plate; [J] evaluate against criteria

**Why it matters:** this is your first composed plate: three vegetables, cooked three different ways, ready at the same
moment and arranged on one plate. It brings together the methods of this course, the timing of `WRK-1` and the cue log
of `HEA-1`, and it starts plating (`TS-PLT`).

**You need:** the inputs of `VEG-1.M6.X1`; warm plates; the timeline template (`WRK-1.M2.X2`); the evaluation form; a
taster's card for each household member (assessment §3).

**Explanation:**

*Three vegetables, three methods, one plate.*
- **Variety:** contrast in colour, texture and flavour. A purée, a roasted root and a quick-sautéed green give soft,
  caramelised and bright on one plate.
  - *Winter:* celeriac purée, roasted beetroot, sautéed kale or Brussels sprouts.
  - *Summer:* pea purée, roasted tomatoes, green beans blanched and tossed in butter.
- **Timing:** everything ready and hot together. Plan backwards (`WRK-1.M2.L3`): the roast goes in first; the purée can
  be held warm, covered; the green is cooked last, in minutes.
- **The plate:** a clear arrangement, clean rims, nothing piled. A spoon of purée as a base or to one side, the roasted
  root beside or on it, the green across. Wipe the rim before it leaves the kitchen.

*Evaluating against criteria.* Score the plate on five criteria:
- **texture** as intended for each vegetable;
- **colour:** bright greens, deep browns, no grey;
- **seasoning;**
- **temperature** at serving;
- **the plate:** clear, clean, nothing piled.

The household's cards are then compared with your own scores. Where they differ, the photo and the tasting notes help
you find out why.

*Cue, then check.* For each vegetable, judge the doneness by eye and touch first and write it in the cue log; then
confirm it with a knife and a taste.

**Practice:**
1. **The plate (2 h).** Run `VEG-1.M6.X1`: plan, cook, plate, photograph and evaluate.

**Look for:** three textures and colours that contrast; all three hot; the household's cards agreeing with your scores.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A monotonous plate | Three vegetables done by one method | Three methods, chosen for contrast |
| A plate swimming in purée | A purée that floods the plate | A thicker purée; a spoonful, not a ladle |
| One cold element | Poor timing | Plan backwards; hold the purée covered; green last |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`). Leftovers into the fridge within 2 hours.

**Record:** the plan, the cue log, the photos and the scores (in `VEG-1.M6.X1`).

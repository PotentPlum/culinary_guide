### SAF-1.M1.L1 — Microbes, toxins and high-risk foods

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-1-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · moves SC-MICR→1, SA-HYG→1*

*Objectives:* [K] bacteria, viruses, toxins; high-risk foods (poultry, minced meat, eggs, rice, shellfish); vulnerable groups
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-1-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** you can't see, smell or taste most of what makes food unsafe. To keep food safe you need to know
what grows in it, under which conditions, and what stops it. This lesson is the basis of every rule in the program.

**You need:** nothing but the journal.

**Explanation:**

*Bacteria.* Bacteria are living cells that multiply in food when they have time, warmth, moisture and nutrients. The
longer food stays warm, the more of them there are. The ones that matter most at home are *Salmonella*
(poultry, eggs), *Campylobacter* (poultry), *Listeria* (chilled ready-to-eat foods) and *Bacillus cereus* (cooked rice
and other starches).

*Viruses.* Viruses such as norovirus don't grow in food. They are carried onto it by hands and surfaces. That is why
nobody cooks for others while they are ill with vomiting or diarrhoea. Norovirus stays infectious after the symptoms
stop, so in the days just after, let someone else cook if you can; if you must cook, wash your hands thoroughly and
often.

*Toxins.* Some bacteria produce toxins in the food as they grow. Heat kills the bacteria but not every toxin, so
reheating can't rescue food that stood warm for too long.

*Spores.* *B. cereus* and *Clostridium perfringens* form spores, a resting form that survives cooking. When cooked food
cools slowly, the spores wake up and multiply. That is why cooling fast matters (`SAF-1.M3.L3`).

*High-risk foods.* Some foods need more care because pathogens grow in them easily or are often present:
- poultry and minced meat;
- raw and lightly cooked eggs;
- shellfish;
- cooked rice and other starches kept warm;
- cooked foods held for later;
- for vulnerable people, also soft cheeses and smoked fish.

*Vulnerable people.* Young children, pregnant people, people over 70 and people with weakened immunity fall ill more
easily, and more seriously. For them a mild illness can become dangerous. From S2 on you will cook some dishes
differently for them.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): read this lesson, then sort this list by risk (high or lower) and write the reason
for each: a roast chicken, a bowl of cooked rice left on the stove, a raw carrot, a soft-boiled egg, dry pasta, a
leftover stew, bread, mussels, a hard cheese, minced beef.

**Look for:** for any food, you can say what could grow in it, under which conditions, and what stops it.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Food that smells fine is served after hours on the counter | Thinking smell and look reveal unsafe food | Judge by time and temperature, not by smell: most pathogens change neither |
| Leftovers that stood warm are "made safe" by reheating | Believing reheating always works | Remember the toxins and spores; keep to the 2-hour rule (`SAF-1.M1.L2`) |
| Rice or pasta left in the pan all evening | Not seeing starches as a risk | Treat cooked rice and pasta like cooked meat: cool fast, keep cold |

**Safety:** the rules this lesson explains are taught as figures in `SAF-1.M1.L2` and `SAF-1.M3`.

**Record:** the sorted list with a reason for each food.

### SAF-1.M1.L2 — The four core rules

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-1-m1-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAF-1.M1.L1` · moves SA-HYG→2, SA-TEMP→1*

*Objectives:* [K] clean, separate, heat and chill [S21]; [J] apply them to a week of home cooking
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-1-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** the Voedingscentrum sums up home food safety in four rules [S21]. Each stops one route by which
food makes people ill. This lesson turns them from a list into moments in your own week of cooking.

**You need:** next week's meal plan (or the meals you expect to cook), and the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The four rules* [S21]:
- **clean** (*schoon*): hands, tools and surfaces;
- **separate** (*gescheiden*): raw food away from food that is ready to eat;
- **heat** (*verhitten*): right through;
- **chill** (*koelen*): fast, and cold enough.

*Each rule stops one route.* Clean and separate stop pathogens moving from raw food to food that won't be cooked again.
Heat kills them. Chill slows their growth.

*The household figures* that frame this course [S22]:

| Rule | Figure |
|---|---|
| The fridge | Set to 4 °C |
| Leftovers out of the fridge | No longer than 2 hours |
| Leftovers in the fridge | At most 2 days, covered; freeze them to keep longer |

*Rules as moments.* "Clean, separate, heat, chill" is easy to recite and easy to forget. It works when you know where in
a meal each rule applies: the chicken goes on the board last, and the board is washed straight away; the leftovers go
into the fridge before you sit down to coffee.

**Practice:**
1. **Rule audit (60 min).** Take one week of planned home cooking. For each meal, mark the step where each rule
   applies: where hands are washed, where raw and ready-to-eat food could meet, what must be heated through, what goes
   into the fridge and when. Then list the three riskiest moments of the week and what you will change.

**Look for:** for each meal, a named control point, for example "chicken last on the board; board washed straight
away".

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You can say the rules but miss them while cooking | The rules learned as a list, not as moments | Write the control point into each meal's plan |
| Raw meat leaks onto vegetables | Forgetting "separate" in the shopping bag and the fridge | Pack raw meat and fish separately; store them at the bottom of the fridge |
| Leftovers sit out during a long dinner | Leftovers not planned | Plan the moment they go into the fridge, within 2 hours |

**Safety:** the fridge at 4 °C, leftovers out for no more than 2 hours, and kept at most 2 days in the fridge
(assessment §9: the fridge, leftovers out of the fridge and leftovers in the fridge rows).

**Record:** the week's audit, with the three riskiest moments and the changes.

### SAF-1.M2.L1 — Hands, cloths and sponges

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-1-m2-l1 -->
*1 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAF-1.M1.L2` · moves SA-HYG→2*

*Objectives:* [S] wash hands properly; manage and replace towels, cloths and sponges
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-1-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** hands and cloths carry more pathogens around a kitchen than anything else. A good routine for both
removes most of the risk before any cooking starts.

**You need:** soap, a timer (your phone), a clean hand towel, a clean dish cloth, and someone to watch or a phone to
film.

**Explanation:**

*Washing hands properly.* Use warm water and soap, and cover every surface: the palms, the backs, between the fingers,
the thumbs and the nails. Rinse, then dry on a clean towel or a paper towel. Wash:
- before you start cooking;
- after touching raw meat, fish or eggs;
- after the bin, your phone, your face or the toilet.

*Cloths and sponges.* A cloth or sponge stays damp and warm, which is ideal for bacteria. A cloth wiped over a
raw-meat spill spreads it everywhere it goes next.

*The routine:*
- one cloth for the counter, and a separate towel for hands;
- cloths and tea towels replaced daily and washed hot;
- sponges replaced often, or replaced by a dish brush that dries between uses;
- paper towels for raw-meat spills, thrown away at once.

**Practice:**
1. **Hand-washing drill and towel routine (30 min).** Wash your hands with the timer running while someone watches (or
   film it). Check that you covered every surface, and note how long a thorough wash takes you. Set up the cloth and
   towel routine: hang the hand towel apart from the dish cloth, and put a stack of fresh cloths where you will reach
   for them. Then list the moments in a usual meal when you must wash your hands.

**Look for:** you wash your hands without a reminder at every change from raw to ready-to-eat food, and there is a
fresh cloth every day.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Hands wiped on the apron or the dish towel | No hand towel within reach | Hang a hand towel at the sink, used only for hands |
| A spill from raw chicken spreads across the counter | One cloth for everything | Paper for raw spills; a separate cloth for the counter |
| The sponge smells | A sponge kept for weeks | Replace it often, or switch to a brush that dries |

**Safety:** this lesson is the "clean" rule of `SAF-1.M1.L2`.

**Record:** the list of hand-washing moments in a usual meal, and how long your thorough wash took.

### SAF-1.M2.L2 — Separating raw and cooked

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-1-m2-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · moves SA-HYG→3, IN-STO→2*

*Objectives:* [S] separate boards and utensils; fridge order with raw meat and fish at the bottom [S22]
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-1-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** raw meat, poultry and fish often carry pathogens that cooking kills. The risk is that they reach
food that won't be cooked again, such as a salad or bread. Separating them is the "separate" rule made concrete.

**You need:** the second cutting board (a different colour), a label or marker, the fridge, and a phone camera.

**Explanation:**

*Cross-contamination.* Raw meat, poultry, fish and unwashed produce can carry pathogens to ready-to-eat food by hands,
boards, knives, cloths and drips.

*Separate tools.* Keep a separate board for raw meat and fish, in a colour you will recognise at a glance. With one
board, cut the ready-to-eat food first and the raw protein last, then wash the board, the knife and your hands. Change
or wash knives and tongs between raw and cooked: the tongs that turned raw chicken on the barbecue don't serve it.

*The fridge order* [S22]:

| Shelf | What goes there | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom shelf | Raw meat and fish, covered, in a container that catches drips | Nothing below can be dripped on |
| Middle and top shelves | Ready-to-eat and cooked food, leftovers | Kept above anything raw |
| Drawer | Vegetables | Kept moist, apart from raw meat |
| Door | Drinks and sauces | The warmest part of the fridge |

*Don't rinse raw chicken.* Rinsing splashes bacteria around the sink and onto anything nearby. Pat it dry with paper
instead, and throw the paper away.

**Practice:**
1. **Fridge and board set-up (45 min).** Reorganise the fridge by the order above. Put raw meat and fish in a
   container on the bottom shelf. Label the raw-protein board. Photograph the fridge, shelf by shelf, and the board.

**Look for:** nothing raw above anything ready to eat, and a raw board you recognise at a glance.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Raw mince on the top shelf | Putting the newest shopping on top | Raw meat and fish always go to the bottom, however full the fridge |
| Cooked chicken served with the tongs that turned it raw | One set of tongs | A second pair, or wash the tongs before the food is cooked |
| Water splashed from the sink onto the salad | Raw chicken rinsed in the sink | Don't rinse it; pat it dry with paper |

**Safety:** raw meat and fish at the bottom of the fridge, covered [S22]; one board for raw protein; nothing raw
touches ready-to-eat food.

**Record:** the photos of the fridge order and the labelled board.

### SAF-1.M2.L3 — Cleaning versus disinfecting

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-1-m2-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 2 wk · moves SA-HYG→3*

*Objectives:* [K] what each removes; [S] a cleaning routine for boards, counters, the fridge and the sink
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-1-m2-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a board that looks clean can still carry what raw chicken left on it. Knowing the difference
between cleaning and disinfecting, and when each is needed, gives you a routine that works without making the kitchen
a laboratory.

**You need:** washing-up liquid, hot water, a scrubbing brush, a household disinfectant (optional; used as its label
says), and a sheet of paper for the schedule.

**Explanation:**

*Cleaning* uses detergent, hot water and friction. It removes dirt, grease and most microbes.

*Disinfecting* kills or removes most of the microbes that are left on a surface that is already clean. Dirt shields
microbes and weakens disinfectants, so cleaning always comes first.

*Disinfecting at home* is done by heat (a hot dishwasher cycle, or boiling water poured over a board) or by a household
disinfectant used exactly as its label says.

*When disinfecting is needed:* after raw meat, poultry, fish or eggs, and when someone in the house has been ill.
Everyday surfaces need cleaning, not disinfectant.

*The board cycle after raw protein:*
1. scrape off the residue into the bin;
2. wash with hot water and detergent, scrubbing;
3. rinse;
4. disinfect: the dishwasher, boiling water, or the disinfectant as the label says;
5. dry upright, in the air.

*The routine:*
- boards and knives: after each use;
- the counter: after each cooking session;
- the sink and the fridge: once a week, on a schedule taped to the fridge door.

**Practice:**
1. **Routine set-up (40 min).** Clean the fridge and the sink. Write the weekly schedule and tape it to the fridge.
   After your next raw-protein prep, do the board cycle step by step.
2. **Repeat after two weeks (20 min).** Run the routine again from the schedule alone, without this lesson, and note
   what slipped.

**Look for:** a schedule you actually follow; boards that air-dry upright; no smell from the sink or the cloths.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Disinfectant sprayed on a greasy board | Disinfecting instead of cleaning | Clean first, then disinfect |
| A raw-chicken board rinsed under the cold tap | Rinsing is not cleaning | The full board cycle, with hot water and detergent |
| Clean boards dried with the hand towel | The hand towel used for everything | Let boards dry upright in the air |

**Safety:** a household disinfectant is used only as its label says, never mixed with another product.

**Record:** the schedule, the board cycle you followed, and the note from the repeat after two weeks.

### SAF-1.M3.L1 — The danger zone and the fridge

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-1-m3-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 wk · builds on `SAF-1.M1.L2` · moves SA-TEMP→2, IN-STO→2*

*Objectives:* [K] growth between the cold and hot holding limits (5 and 57 °C) [S25]; [S] check that the fridge holds 4 °C and the freezer −18 °C [S22]
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-1-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** temperature is the main control you have over bacterial growth. This lesson gives you the limits
the program uses, and a fridge you know holds its temperature, because you have measured it.

**You need:** the fridge thermometer, the journal, and a week.

**Explanation:**

*The growth range.* Most pathogens multiply between fridge temperature and cooking temperature, fastest around body
temperature. The program uses the measurable limits of assessment §9: cold food at 5 °C or below, hot food at 57 °C
or above [S25]. Between them, time counts: the longer food stays there, the more the bacteria grow.

*The fridge and the freezer.* The fridge is set to 4 °C and the freezer to −18 °C or colder [S22]. The Dutch fridge
rule is stricter than the 5 °C limit, so 4 °C applies.

*A dial is not a thermometer.* The numbers on a fridge dial are settings, not temperatures. The temperature differs
from shelf to shelf, rises when the door opens and when warm food goes in, and is usually lowest at the back of the
bottom shelf. Only a thermometer tells you what the fridge really holds.

*Freezing.* Freezing stops growth but kills few bacteria. When the food thaws, they start again. Food thaws in the
fridge, not on the counter.

**Practice:**
1. **Fridge log (15 minutes a day, for a week).** Put the fridge thermometer on the middle shelf. Read it every morning
   and evening, before opening the door for anything else, and write down the reading, how often the door was opened,
   and any warm food that went in. If it reads above 4 °C, turn the setting colder and read it again the next day,
   until it holds 4 °C. Read the freezer once during the week.

**Look for:** a reading stable at 4 °C or below on the middle shelf, and back there within an hour after the weekly
shop.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The fridge is warmer than you think | Trusting the dial | Measure with the thermometer and adjust the setting |
| A high reading that is gone an hour later | Reading straight after the door was open | Read before opening the door for anything else |
| Some shelves stay warm | The fridge packed so full that air can't circulate | Leave space between items and around the back wall |
| Meat thawing on the counter | Old habit | Thaw it in the fridge, on the bottom shelf, in a container |

**Safety:** the fridge at 4 °C, the freezer at −18 °C or colder (assessment §9, the fridge and freezer rows); cold
holding at 5 °C or below and hot holding at 57 °C or above (the hot and cold holding row).

**Record:** the week's log: morning and evening readings, door openings, warm food added, the setting changes, and the
freezer reading.

### SAF-1.M3.L2 — Thermometers and core temperatures

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-1-m3-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · moves SA-TEMP→2, KM-EQPT→1*

*Objectives:* [S] calibrate with ice water; probe correctly; [K] the safe core temperatures of the main protein groups [S25]
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-1-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** "cooked right through" is a temperature at the centre of the food, not a colour. A thermometer you
trust, placed in the right spot, is the only way to know. Every later lesson with meat, poultry or fish relies on this
one.

**You need:**
- the instant-read thermometer;
- a tall glass of crushed ice topped up with a little cold water, and a pan of water at a rolling boil;
- for the meal: 2 chicken thighs (bone in) and 250 g of minced beef shaped into 2 burgers or 6 meatballs, with
  whatever you usually serve with them;
- a clean cloth or paper and hot soapy water to clean the probe.

**Explanation:**

*Calibration.* Stir the ice slurry for half a minute and put the probe in, without touching the glass. It should read
0 °C. In water at a rolling boil at sea level it reads about 100 °C. If it is off, note the offset and correct each
reading by it, or adjust the thermometer if it allows.

*Probing.*
- Put the tip in the thickest part of the food, away from bone, fat and the pan.
- In thin pieces (a chicken breast, a burger), go in from the side so the tip sits in the centre.
- Wait until the reading stops climbing before you read it.
- Clean the probe between raw and cooked food.

*The core temperatures* the program uses (assessment §9: the Voedingscentrum [S47], and the FDA *Food Code* [S25] where
it gives a time):

| Food | Core temperature |
|---|---|
| Poultry, and stuffed foods | 75 °C |
| Minced meat and minced fish | 75 °C instantaneous, or 68 °C held for 17 seconds |
| Fish and whole cuts of meat | 63 °C for 15 seconds |
| Whole roasts | A time-and-temperature table, taught in `MEA-2` |

The Dutch rule "right through" (*door en door gaar*) [S21] is the same rule; the Voedingscentrum's number for meat
and chicken is at least 75 °C at the core [S47].

*Why minced meat needs more than a steak.* On a whole cut of meat, bacteria sit on the outside, and searing kills them.
Mincing spreads the outside through the whole piece, so the centre must get hot enough too.

**Practice:**
1. **Calibrate and probe (60 min, as part of a meal).** Calibrate the thermometer in the ice slurry and in boiling
   water, and write down both readings. Cook the chicken thighs and the burgers or meatballs your usual way. Probe each
   in the thickest part, write down the reading and where the probe went, and keep cooking any piece that is below its
   figure. Clean the probe between pieces.

**Look for:** a reading of 0 °C in the ice slurry (or a known offset); the probe tip in the centre of the thickest
part; a reading that has stopped climbing.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A high reading, but the centre is underdone | The probe at the surface | Push the tip to the centre of the thickest part |
| A reading far higher than expected | The tip touches the bone or the pan | Move it away from the bone and the pan |
| A reading that is too low | Read too early | Wait until the number stops climbing |
| Readings that don't match each other | An uncalibrated probe | Calibrate it in ice water and note the offset |
| Chicken served pink at the bone, or dry | Judging by the colour of the juice | Probe it: 75 °C at the core |

**Safety:** poultry 75 °C, minced meat 75 °C instantaneous (or 68 °C for 17 seconds), fish and whole cuts 63 °C for
15 seconds (assessment §9: the poultry, minced meat, and fish and intact meat rows).

**Record:** the calibration readings and the offset; for each piece, the reading and where the probe went.

### SAF-1.M3.L3 — Cooling, storing and reheating

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-1-m3-l3 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 d · builds on `SAF-1.M3.L1` · moves SA-TEMP→3, IN-STO→3*

*Objectives:* [S] cool food fast in shallow containers; label it; reheat until steaming hot; [K] rice and *Bacillus cereus*
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-1-m3-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** spores that survive cooking grow while food cools slowly. A big pot of soup left to cool on the
stove overnight is one of the most common causes of food poisoning at home. This lesson makes cooling fast, and
reheating once, a habit.

**You need:** a batch dish (see `SAF-1.M3.X2`), shallow containers with lids about 5 cm deep, a sink or a bowl for a
cold-water bath, labels, the thermometer, and two days.

**Explanation:**

*Cooling fast.* The deeper the pot, the longer its centre stays warm. Spread the food out to cool it faster.

*At home* [S22]: leftovers go into the fridge within 2 hours. Cool them faster first, in shallow containers or in a pan
standing in cold water, and stir now and then.

*The measurable standard* for batches and stocks [S25]: from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C or below
within 6 hours in total. You will use it from `STK-2` on; here you meet it.

*Storing.* Cover the food, label it with what it is and the date, and keep it at most 2 days in the fridge, or freeze
it [S22].

*Reheating.* Reheat until steaming hot right through (*stomend heet*) [S21]. Measured, that is 74 °C at the centre
[S25]. Reheat leftovers once only: each cooling and reheating gives the bacteria another chance.

*Rice.* *B. cereus* spores survive cooking. If cooked rice stands warm, the bacteria make a toxin that reheating does
not destroy. Cool rice quickly, keep it in the fridge, and reheat it once only.

**Practice:**
1. **Cool, store, reheat (90 min over two days, plus waiting).** Cook the batch dish of `SAF-1.M3.X2`. Cool it in
   shallow containers in a cold-water bath, taking its temperature every 30 minutes, and put it in the fridge within
   2 hours. Label it. On day two, reheat one portion until steaming hot and probe the centre.

**Look for:** the food in the fridge within 2 hours; labels with a date; a reheating reading of 74 °C or above at the
centre.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The soup's centre is still warm the next morning | A full pot put in the fridge to cool overnight | Divide it into shallow containers and cool them in cold water first |
| Food takes hours to cool | Containers too deep | Use containers about 5 cm deep |
| You can't tell how old a leftover is | No date on the label | Label every container with the contents and the date |
| Leftovers reheated a second time | Reheating the whole batch each time | Reheat only the portion you will eat |
| Rice kept warm in the cooker all afternoon | Treating rice as harmless | Cool it fast and refrigerate it; never keep it warm for hours |

**Safety:** leftovers in the fridge within 2 hours, cooled in shallow containers or a cold-water bath; at most 2 days in
the fridge; reheated once, until steaming hot (74 °C at the centre); for batches, 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours and to
5 °C or below within 6 hours (assessment §9: the leftovers, cooling, reheating and cooling cooked food rows).

**Record:** the cooling log (time and temperature every 30 minutes), the label, and the reheating reading.

### SAF-1.M3.X2 — Cool, store and reheat a batch

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-saf-1-m3-x2 -->
*guided, recipe* — Cool, store and reheat a batch of leftovers with a temperature log.
<!-- END generated:exercise-saf-1-m3-x2 -->

**Task:** measure the full temperature chain of a home batch: cooked through, cooled fast, stored, reheated once. Use
your usual recipe for a stew or sauce with minced meat or chicken, or the one below. Two days.

**You need:**
- a pan with a lid, a wooden spoon, the thermometer;
- three or four shallow containers with lids, about 5 cm deep;
- a sink or a large bowl with cold water (and ice if you have it) for the cold-water bath;
- labels and a pen;
- the ingredients below, for 4–6 portions.

**Recipe:** minced-beef and vegetable sauce, for 4–6 portions.

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Minced beef | 500 g |
| Onions | 2 (about 250 g), chopped |
| Carrots | 2 (about 200 g), diced |
| Leek (in autumn and winter) or a courgette (in summer) | 1, sliced |
| Chopped tomatoes | 1 tin of 400 g |
| Water or stock | 200 ml |
| Sunflower or rapeseed oil | 15 ml |
| Fine salt | 5 g, then to taste |
| Black pepper | to taste |

Day 1:
1. **Brown the meat (10 min).** Heat the oil in the pan over medium-high heat. Add the mince and break it up with the
   spoon until no pink is left on the outside. Wash your hands and the spoon after handling the raw mince.
2. **Soften the vegetables (10 min).** Add the onions, carrots and leek or courgette, with the salt. Stir over medium
   heat until the onion is soft and translucent.
3. **Simmer (30 min).** Add the tomatoes and the water or stock. Bring to the boil, cover, lower the heat and simmer
   gently, stirring now and then, until the carrots are tender.
4. **Probe it.** Probe the sauce in the centre of the pan: the minced meat must reach 75 °C (assessment §9; a simmering
   sauce is well above it). Write down the reading and the time. Taste, and season with salt and pepper.
5. **Serve** the portions you eat tonight.
6. **Cool (up to 2 hours).** Divide the rest into the shallow containers, no more than about 5 cm deep. Stand them in
   the cold-water bath, uncovered, and stir now and then. Take the temperature in the centre of one container every
   30 minutes and write it down.
7. **Refrigerate.** Within 2 hours of the end of cooking, put the lids on, label each container with "beef sauce" and
   the date, and put them in the fridge.

Day 2:

8. **Reheat one portion** in a small pan over medium heat, stirring, until it is steaming hot right through. Probe the
   centre: it must read 74 °C or above. Write it down.
9. **Use the rest** within the 2 days, or freeze it before then. Don't reheat the same portion twice.

**Show:** the core temperature at cooking; the cooling log, showing the dish in the fridge within 2 hours; the reheating
reading of 74 °C or above (the `SA-TEMP` level 3 success row); and a photo of the labels (`IN-STO`).

**Time:** 1.5 h active over two days; the meal is dinner on both days.

### SAF-1.M4.L1 — The 14 allergens and cross-contact

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-1-m4-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · moves SA-ALLG→2*

*Objectives:* [K] the EU list and hidden sources; reading labels; [S] prevent cross-contact when cooking for others
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-1-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** for someone with a food allergy, a trace of the wrong ingredient can cause a severe reaction.
Cooking for others means knowing where allergens hide and keeping them out of a dish, from the shop to the plate.

**You need:** five prepacked products from your pantry (for example a stock cube, a sausage, a sauce, bread and a
biscuit), and the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The 14 groups* that EU law requires to be declared [S23]:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Cereals containing gluten (wheat, rye, barley, oats, spelt) | Crustaceans | Eggs |
| Fish | Peanuts | Soybeans |
| Milk | Nuts (tree nuts) | Celery |
| Mustard | Sesame | Sulphites (above 10 mg/kg or 10 mg/l) |
| Lupin | Molluscs | |

*Where they hide.* Some allergens turn up where you would not expect them:
- milk in sausages and bread;
- celery in stock cubes and spice mixes;
- mustard in dressings;
- fish in Worcestershire sauce (it contains anchovies);
- sulphites in wine and dried fruit;
- lupin flour in some breads and gluten-free products;
- soy in many processed foods.

*Reading labels.* On a prepacked product, the allergens are emphasised in the ingredient list itself, for example in
bold, including inside compound ingredients [S23]. Read the whole list, not only the front of the pack. "May contain"
warnings are voluntary: their absence doesn't prove a product is free of traces.

*Cross-contact.* Cooking does not remove allergens, and a trace can be enough to harm an allergic guest. Prevent
cross-contact by:
- cooking the allergen-free dish first, and covering it;
- using clean tools and pans;
- keeping separate spoons for tasting.

**Practice:**
1. **Label hunt (45 min).** Read the ingredient lists of your five products. For each, list every allergen group,
   including the ones hidden in compound ingredients. Then plan one household meal free of a named allergen (for
   example celery): which products you can't use, what replaces them, and in what order you cook.

**Look for:** every allergen on a label found, including the ones in bold inside compound ingredients.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| An allergen missed | Reading only the "contains" line on the front | Read the full ingredient list; look for the emphasised words |
| Celery in a "plain" soup | Assuming a homemade stock is free of celery | Check every ingredient of the stock, including the cube |
| Traces of nuts in the allergen-free dish | Nuts toasted in the same pan | Cook the allergen-free dish first, with clean pans |

**Safety:** an allergic guest's dish is cooked first, with clean tools, covered and kept apart. When in doubt about a
product, leave it out.

**Record:** the five allergen lists, and the allergen-free meal plan.

### SAF-1.M4.L2 — Burns, cuts and fires

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-1-m4-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · moves SA-PHYS→2*

*Objectives:* [K] causes of accidents; [S] smother a fat fire (never water); first aid for cuts and burns
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-1-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** most kitchen accidents have a few common causes and can be prevented. When one happens anyway, the
first minute decides how bad it gets. You learn the actions now, so that you don't have to think when it counts.

**You need:** a lid that fits your largest frying pan, the fire blanket, the first-aid kit, and a cold, empty pan.

**Explanation:**

*Causes of accidents.*
- **Burns:** steam from a lid or a pan; handles turned outwards and knocked; splashing oil; wet towels used as oven
  gloves, which let the heat straight through.
- **Cuts:** dull knives that slip; knives left in the washing-up water, out of sight; cutting towards your hand.
- **Fires:** oil left unattended on the heat.

*A fat fire.* If oil in a pan catches fire:
1. turn off the heat, if you can reach it safely;
2. cover the pan with a lid or the fire blanket;
3. leave it covered until it is completely cold;
4. if the fire doesn't go out, leave the kitchen, close the door and call 112.

Never use water: it turns to steam at once and throws burning fat out of the pan. Never carry the pan to the sink or
outside.

*First aid.*
- **Burns:** cool the burned skin at once for 10 to 20 minutes, preferably under lukewarm, gently running tap water,
  with the stream just above the burn so the water runs over it [S49]. Cool only the burn, not the whole person. Leave
  blisters intact. Afterwards, cover a burn that has blistered or broken the skin with cling film or a sterile
  non-stick dressing, and call the GP urgently while you cool it. Call 112, or have someone call, while you cool a
serious or large burn [S49].
- **Cuts:** press a clean cloth or dressing on the cut until it stops bleeding, then clean and cover it.
- **Get medical help** for deep cuts, and for large or deep burns.

*Prevention.*
- a dry towel or oven gloves, never a wet one;
- handles turned inwards, away from the edge of the hob;
- a lid and the fire blanket within reach whenever there is oil in a pan;
- knives washed straight after use, never left in the water;
- a falling knife is let fall: step back.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): study the procedures. Then mount the fire blanket within reach of the hob (not
behind it) and put the first-aid kit where you can find it without looking. With a cold, empty pan on the hob, walk
through the fat-fire steps aloud: reach for the switch, slide the lid on from the front, leave it.

**Look for:** you say the fat-fire steps in order without hesitating; the lid and the blanket are within reach of the
hob.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Water thrown on burning oil | Reflex | Practise the steps aloud until "lid, not water" is the reflex |
| A burning pan carried to the sink | Trying to get the fire away | Leave the pan where it is; cover it |
| The fire blanket can't be reached in time | Stored in a cupboard or the cellar | Mount it near the hob, where you can reach it without leaning over the pans |
| A cut hand | Catching a falling knife | Step back and let it fall |

**Safety:** this lesson is the safety. For burns, the Dutch Red Cross [S49]; for fires, call 112 if a fire doesn't go
out.

**Record:** where the fire blanket and the first-aid kit are, and the fat-fire steps in your own words.

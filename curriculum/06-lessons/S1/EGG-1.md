# EGG-1 — Eggs and breakfast cookery

<!-- BEGIN generated:header -->
**Stage:** S1 · **Hours:** 18.5 h · **Lessons:** 11 · **Exercises:** 6 · **Course file:** [`EGG-1`](../../05-courses/S1/EGG-1.md)
<!-- END generated:header -->

**Status:** draft — awaiting the second reader's log ([`06-safety-review.md`](../../06-safety-review.md)) and the S1
review.

**Before you start.** The egg is your first protein and the best doneness lab there is. It is cheap, it cooks in
minutes, and every difference of a few degrees or a minute shows in the cross-section. What you learn here about eggs is
the logic that the meat and fish courses depend on:
- proteins set at defined temperatures;
- carryover continues after the heat is off;
- gentle heat gives a tender result, and harsh heat a rubbery one.

The course moves from egg science, through the classical preparations (boiled, poached, fried, scrambled and the
omelette, each to a stated standard), to a stirred and a baked custard, and ends with a breakfast for four served on
time. Most of the evidence is cross-section photos: a boiled egg cut in half shows exactly what happened inside.

*Eggs and risk groups.* Soft eggs (a runny or jammy yolk, a soft omelette, soft scrambled eggs, the water-bath eggs of
module 1) are not served to pregnant people, young children, older people or people with weakened immunity. For them,
an egg is cooked until it is fully set (assessment §9, the risk-group row). Ask before you cook for guests.

Plan about three weeks. Most lessons are breakfasts or light dinners. The omelette series and the boiled-egg series each
need a dozen eggs in one session, so buy eggs by the size class you will use for the timing table (M or L), and keep
using that class. Every egg is eaten: boiled eggs become lunch, omelettes become breakfast or supper, and the whites
left over from the custards are frozen for meringue in `BAK-2`.

**Have ready:** a small saucepan and a larger one; a 20 cm non-stick or seasoned omelette pan; a 24–26 cm ovenproof
frying pan; the instant-read thermometer; a slotted spoon; a small sieve or tea strainer; a large bowl for ice water;
four ramekins or heatproof cups and a deep oven dish; a stick blender if you have one; a timer; a phone for photos; the
cue log (`HEA-1`).

### EGG-1.M1.L1 — Anatomy, freshness and safety

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAF-1.M1.L1` · moves IN-DAI→2*

*Objectives:* [K] shell, white, yolk; sizes; freshness tests; storage; raw-egg guidance; [S] test and grade a box of eggs
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** an egg's age changes how it behaves: a fresh egg poaches into a neat oval, an older one spreads into
wisps but peels easily once boiled. And an egg is a high-risk food, so how you store and handle it matters from the
moment you bring it home.

**You need:** six eggs of different ages: a few from a fresh box and a few older ones from the fridge, each marked in
pencil with its best-before date; a tall glass of cold water; a flat white plate; a phone; a helper to number the eggs.

**Explanation:**

*Anatomy.*
- **The shell** is porous, so moisture and air pass through it slowly. Under it lie two **membranes**.
- **The air cell** sits between the membranes at the broad end. It grows as the egg ages and loses moisture.
- **The white** has two parts: a **thick white** around the yolk and a **thin white** outside it. With age, the thick
  white thins.
- **The yolk** is held in the centre by two twisted cords, the **chalazae**.

*Sizes.* EU size classes go by weight: S (under 53 g), M (53–63 g), L (63–73 g) and XL (73 g and over). A recipe's "one
egg" can mean anything from a small to an extra-large egg, a difference of 20 g or more, so baking recipes weigh them.

*Freshness.*
- **The water test:** a fresh egg lies flat on the bottom; an older one tilts or stands on its end, because its air cell
  has grown; one that floats is old.
- **On a plate:** a fresh egg has a high, domed yolk and a thick white that holds its shape around it; an old egg
  spreads flat and thin.

*Safety.* Eggs are a high-risk food because of *Salmonella* (`SAF-1.M1.L1`).
- Keep them in their box, in the fridge, unwashed. Washing removes the protective layer on the shell.
- Don't use cracked or dirty eggs.
- Wash your hands after cracking them.
- Risk groups avoid raw and runny eggs [S21] (assessment §9).

The best-before date assumes the eggs were kept cool.

**Practice:**
1. **Grade a box (45 min).** The helper numbers the six eggs and keeps the dates hidden. Water-test each and note how it
   lies. Then crack each, one at a time, onto the plate, and photograph it from the side and from above. Rank the six
   from freshest to oldest, from the yolk and the white alone. Then check your ranking against the dates. The cracked
   eggs go into scrambled eggs or an omelette the same day.

**Look for:** the eggs ranked by age correctly from the white and the yolk alone.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Eggs washed "to be safe" | Washing eggs, which removes the protective layer | Store unwashed; wash your hands, not the eggs |
| Bits of shell in the egg | Cracking eggs on the bowl's rim, which pushes shell inside | Crack on the flat counter |
| A runny egg served to a pregnant guest | Not asking about risk groups | Ask; for risk groups, fully set eggs only |

**Safety:** wash your hands after handling raw eggs; the cracked eggs are cooked the same day or covered in the fridge.

**Record:** the water-test notes, the photos, your ranking and the dates.

### EGG-1.M1.L2 — How eggs set

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m1-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `EGG-1.M1.L1` · moves SC-PROT→2*

*Objectives:* [K] protein denaturation; the white and the yolk set at different temperatures; [S] a water-bath test
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** every egg preparation, and later every piece of meat and fish, is a question of how far the proteins
have set. Once you know at what temperatures the white and the yolk set, you can predict a soft-boiled egg, rescue a
custard, and understand why scrambled eggs weep.

**You need:** the inputs of `EGG-1.M1.X1`: 6 eggs; a saucepan with about 3 litres of water; the thermometer; a bowl of
ice water; a timer; labels.

**Explanation:**

*Denaturation and coagulation.* Egg proteins start as tight, folded coils. Heat unfolds them (**denaturation**), and the
unfolded proteins bond to each other into a network that traps water (**coagulation**). A liquid becomes a soft gel,
then a solid. Too much heat tightens the network so far that it squeezes out its water: rubbery whites, and scrambled
eggs sitting in a puddle [S16].

*Different proteins set at different temperatures.*

| Part | What happens |
|---|---|
| White | Begins to set in the low sixties, but its main protein needs about 80 °C to be firm |
| Yolk | Thickens from about 65 °C and sets by about 70 °C |

That is why a soft-boiled egg can have a set white and a runny yolk: in boiling water the white, on the outside, gets
far hotter than the yolk in the centre. And it is why an egg held at 65 °C for an hour is the reverse: a barely set
white around a thick, fudgy yolk.

*Dilution raises the setting temperature.* Sugar and milk keep the egg proteins apart, so a custard thickens only around
80 °C instead of setting at 70 °C. That gives you room to work, and it is why a custard can be cooked on the stove at
all (`EGG-1.M4`).

*Holding a temperature.* The test only works if the water stays at its temperature for the whole 45 minutes. Use the
method of `HEA-1.M5.X2`: adjust the burner by the surface first, then check the thermometer, and log it.

**Practice:**
1. **Water-bath test (45 min active, about 2 h elapsed).** Run `EGG-1.M1.X1`: two eggs at each of 62, 65 and 70 °C, held
   for 45 minutes each, the water temperature logged every 10 minutes. Before you crack each pair, write what you expect
   to see.

**Look for:** three clearly different cross-sections, in the order the theory predicts.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Two temperatures look alike | Letting the bath drift by several degrees | Log every 10 minutes and adjust; keep a kettle of hot water ready |
| The eggs look raw | Cracking the eggs too early | The full 45 minutes |
| Photos that can't be told apart afterwards | Not photographing each egg with its temperature label | A label next to every egg in every photo |

**Safety:** these eggs are soft eggs: eat them yourself, or serve them only to guests outside the risk groups (§9).

**Record:** the water log, the predictions and the labelled photos (in `EGG-1.M1.X1`).

### EGG-1.M1.X1 — Eggs held at 62, 65 and 70 °C

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-egg-1-m1-x1 -->
*comparison, recipe* — Eggs held at 62, 65 and 70 °C; photograph the cross-sections.
<!-- END generated:exercise-egg-1-m1-x1 -->

**Task:** see the setting temperatures of the white and the yolk, and check the freshness of the eggs you use. One pot,
held at each temperature in turn for 45 minutes, with the thermometer in the water.

**You need:** 6 eggs, from the fridge; a saucepan with about 3 litres of water; the thermometer; a slotted spoon; a bowl
of ice water; two small bowls; a sharp knife; labels (62, 65, 70); a kettle; a timer; the journal; a phone.

**Recipe:**
1. **Freshness.** Water-test the six eggs and note how each lies. Photograph the test.
2. **62 °C.** Heat the water to 62 °C, checking with the thermometer. Lower two eggs in on the slotted spoon. Hold the
   water at 62 °C for 45 minutes, logging its temperature every 10 minutes; add a little hot water from the kettle, or
   lower the heat, to correct it.
3. **Cool and open.** Lift the eggs into the ice water for 1 minute. Crack one into a small bowl. Cut the other in half
   in its shell with the knife, over the second bowl. Photograph both with the label.
4. **65 °C and 70 °C.** Raise the water to 65 °C and repeat with the next two eggs; then 70 °C with the last two.
5. **Explain** each result in two or three sentences by the setting temperatures of the white and the yolk.

*What to expect:* at 62 °C, a milky, barely set white and a liquid yolk; at 65 °C, a soft, jelly-like white and a
thick yolk; at 70 °C, a softly set white and a yolk that holds its shape.

**Show:**
- The water log, the labelled cross-sections and the explanation. `SC-PROT` level 2 rests on this module's quiz.
- For `IN-DAI` level 2, in part: the freshness test photographed, with this module's items on freshness, structure and
  safe handling at 80 %. The custard part of the row follows in `EGG-1.M4.X1`.

AI review checks that the cross-sections match the expected pattern: *competent* is the three eggs, each at its
temperature and correctly labelled.

**Time:** 45 min active; about 2 h 30 min elapsed.

### EGG-1.M2.L1 — Boiled eggs

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m2-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `EGG-1.M1.L2` · moves TS-EGG→2, JD-CUES→2*

*Objectives:* [S] soft to hard boiled; peeling; [K] the green ring and how to avoid it
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a boiled egg is the simplest dish there is, and the one where a minute shows most. You learn to
predict the yolk before you cut it, to avoid the grey-green ring, and to peel an egg cleanly.

**You need:** 6 eggs of one size class, from the fridge; a saucepan with enough water to cover them by 2 cm; a slotted
spoon; a bowl of ice water; a timer; a knife; a board; the cue log; a phone.

**Explanation:**

*Into boiling water, from the fridge.* Lower fridge-cold eggs gently into water that is already boiling, on a spoon, and
start the timer. This gives consistent timing: the water and the eggs start from the same point every time. A cold
start depends on the pan and the hob. Eggs started in boiling water also peel more easily.

*Shocking.* Lift the eggs straight into ice water. That stops the carryover (`HEA-1.M1.L3`), so the yolk stays as you
timed it, and the white contracts slightly from the shell, which helps peeling.

*The green ring* is iron from the yolk reacting with sulphur from the white when the egg is overcooked. It smells and
tastes sulphurous. Avoid it by not overcooking, and by cooling at once.

*Peeling.* Very fresh eggs peel badly, because the white sticks to the membrane. Eggs a week or more old peel more
easily. Crack the shell all over on the counter, then peel under water, starting at the broad end, where the air cell
is.

*A starting table* for M or L eggs from the fridge, timed from when they go in:

| Time | Yolk |
|---|---|
| 6 minutes | Runny, white just set |
| 8 minutes | Jammy: soft and sticky at the centre |
| 10 minutes | Set through, still yellow and moist |

Your own table, for your eggs and your pan, comes in `EGG-1.M2.L2`.

**Practice:**
1. **Soft to hard (45 min).** Boil two eggs each for a soft, a jammy and a hard yolk, using the starting table. Shock
   them, peel them and cut them in half. Before cutting each egg, write the yolk you predict in the cue log; then cut,
   photograph, and note whether you were right.

**Look for:** clean whites, no green ring, the yolk as predicted.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Cracked shells, white leaking into the water | The eggs went in too fast, or dropped | Lower them on a spoon |
| All eggs undercooked | Overcrowding the pan, so the water stopped boiling | Enough water to cover by 2 cm; fewer eggs, or a larger pan |
| A green ring, a chalky yolk | Skipping the ice water, so carryover overcooked them | Ice water at once, for at least 5 minutes |

**Safety:** lower the eggs on a spoon, and keep your hand out of the steam. Only the fully set eggs go to risk groups
(§9).

**Record:** the cue-log entries (prediction, then cut) and the photos.

### EGG-1.M2.L2 — Timing by size and temperature

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m2-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `EGG-1.M2.L1` · moves TS-EGG→3*

*Objectives:* [J] adjust for size, fridge temperature and altitude; [S] hit a target yolk three times running
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a recipe's "7 minutes" was right for someone else's eggs, pan and hob. Control means building your
own table and hitting the same yolk every time, and, when it misses, knowing which variable to change.

**You need:** for session 1, the inputs of `EGG-1.M2.X1`; for session 2, 3 eggs of the same size class; the saucepan; a
slotted spoon; ice water; a timer; a knife; a phone.

**Explanation:**

*The variables.*
- **Egg size:** a larger egg takes longer to heat through.
- **Starting temperature:** eggs from the fridge take longer than eggs at room temperature.
- **How many eggs against how much water:** more cold eggs in less water take longer to come back to the boil.
- **Altitude:** water boils at a lower temperature high up, but in the Netherlands this hardly matters.

Shell colour has no effect.

*Your own table.* Times for your eggs, your pan and your hob, built from the series in `EGG-1.M2.X1`. Write down the
conditions with it: the size class, fridge or room temperature, the pan, how much water, and whether you time from when
the eggs go in or from when the water returns to the boil. Choose one way of timing and keep it.

| Size | Start | Water | Time | Yolk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L | Fridge | 1.5 l, 4 eggs | 6 min | … |

*Hitting a target* three times running is the test of control. When a run misses, ask what changed, and change only one
variable at a time to correct it. If you change the size and the starting temperature together, you can't tell which
one did it.

**Practice:**
1. **Build the table (45 min).** Run the timing series of `EGG-1.M2.X1`: 6, 7, 8 and 10 minutes, two eggs per time,
   and write your table.
2. **Hit the target (45 min, another day).** Choose your time for a jammy yolk. Without the table at hand, boil three
   eggs one after another at that time, under the same conditions. Halve and photograph each.

**Look for:** three matching cross-sections.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A miss you can't explain | Changing the size and the starting temperature at the same time | One variable at a time |
| The same time gives different results | Timing from when the eggs went in one day, and from the return to the boil the next | Choose one, write it in the table, keep it |
| The third egg is harder than the first two | The eggs left in the hot pan while the others were cut | Into the ice water as soon as the time is up |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`) and the risk-group rule above.

**Record:** your table with its conditions, and the three photos.

### EGG-1.M3.L1 — Poached eggs

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m3-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `EGG-1.M1.L2`, `HEA-1.M5.L1` · moves TS-EGG→2*

*Objectives:* [S] fresh eggs, a sieve, water temperature, timing; poach ahead and reheat
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a poached egg is the test of a gentle hand: a compact white with no wisps around a runny yolk. And
once you can poach ahead and reheat, you can serve poached eggs to a table of eight at once, the way restaurants do.

**You need:** for session 1, 4 fresh eggs; for session 2, 4 fresh eggs and 4 slices of bread; a wide pan with water
about 8 cm deep; a small sieve; a cup; a slotted spoon; a bowl of ice water; a kettle; paper towels; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Fresh eggs* have more thick white, which holds a compact shape around the yolk. Older eggs have more thin white, which
spreads into wisps. Use eggs from a recent box (`EGG-1.M1.L1`).

*A sieve* drains off the thin white. Crack the egg into the sieve over a bowl, swirl gently for 20–30 seconds while the
thin white runs through, then tip what is left into a cup.

*The water* should be just below a simmer: a few small bubbles rising from the base, the surface shivering but not
breaking (`HEA-1.M5.L1`). It should be deep enough for the egg to set before it touches the bottom. A spoon of vinegar
per litre helps the surface of the white set, but it is optional with fresh, drained eggs, and it can be tasted.

*Timing.* Slide the egg from the cup into the water, close to the surface. About 3 minutes gives a set white and a runny
yolk. Lift it out with the slotted spoon and press the white gently with a finger: it should feel set, with the yolk
soft beneath. Rest it on a paper towel to drain.

*Poaching ahead.* Lift each poached egg straight into ice water. Just before serving, reheat them in hot water from the
kettle, in a pan off the heat, for under a minute. The yolk stays runny, and you can poach in calm batches and serve
everyone together.

**Practice:**
1. **Poach (60 min).** Poach one egg at a time, then two at a time. Photograph each on its paper towel, then cut one
   open.
2. **Poach ahead (60 min).** Poach four eggs, one or two at a time, and hold them in the ice water. Toast the bread.
   Reheat the eggs together and serve them on the toast.

**Look for:** a compact white with no wisps; a runny yolk under a soft, opaque white.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The egg torn, white everywhere | A rolling boil | Just below a simmer |
| Wisps and a flat white | Old eggs | A fresh box for poaching |
| A cloud of white around the egg | Cracking straight into the pan without draining | Sieve first, then the cup |
| Eggs stuck together, cooked unevenly | Too many eggs at once | One or two at a time |

**Safety:** poached eggs with runny yolks are soft eggs: fully set for risk groups (§9). Eggs held in ice water are used
the same morning; keep the bowl in the fridge if you poach well ahead.

**Record:** the photos from both sessions.

### EGG-1.M3.L2 — Fried eggs

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m3-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-1.M3.L1` · moves TS-EGG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] sunny side up, basted, crispy-edged; [J] heat and fat choice
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a fried egg seems trivial until you try to get the white fully set while the yolk stays runny. The
three styles each need their own heat and fat, which makes this a short lesson in reading the pan (`HEA-1.M3.L4`).

**You need:** 3 eggs; butter; neutral oil; salt and pepper; a 24 cm non-stick or seasoned frying pan with a lid; a
spoon; a cup; a fish spatula; a phone.

**Explanation:**

*Three styles and their heat.*

| Style | Heat and fat | Method | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sunny side up | Low to medium; butter or oil | A lid for the last 1–2 minutes, so steam sets the top of the white | No colour; a glossy, runny yolk |
| Basted | Medium; 2 tablespoons of butter or oil | Tilt the pan and spoon the hot fat over the white around the yolk | Lightly golden edges; the white set on top |
| Crispy-edged | High; 2 tablespoons of oil, shimmering | Leave it until the edges are lacy and brown, 1–2 minutes | Crisp, brown lace; a runny yolk |

*Fat choice.* Butter tastes best, but its milk solids burn above moderate heat. Oil takes high heat.

*The white must set while the yolk stays runny.* The thick white around the yolk is the last part to set, because it is
thickest and closest to the cool yolk. That is where you look, and where the lid or the basting is aimed.

*Cracking.* Crack each egg into a cup first, so a broken yolk or a piece of shell stays out of the pan, and slide it in
from low down.

**Practice:**
1. **Three ways (45 min).** Fry one egg in each style. Photograph each from above and from the side, then cut into the
   white next to the yolk to check it is set.

**Look for:** no raw white around the yolk; the style's colour (none, light or lacy-brown) as intended.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A brown, crisp base and a raw top | High heat for sunny side up, so the base burns before the top sets | Low to medium heat and a lid |
| A pale, oily egg with no lace | Low heat for crispy edges, so the egg stews in oil | Oil shimmering before the egg goes in |
| Raw white around the yolk | Taken out too soon | Lid, or baste aimed at the thick white |

**Safety:** hot oil spits when an egg goes in: slide it in from the cup, away from you. For risk groups, fry the egg on
both sides until the yolk is fully set (§9).

**Record:** the six photos, and a line on each style.

### EGG-1.M3.L3 — Scrambled eggs

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m3-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `EGG-1.M1.L2` · moves TS-EGG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] soft French style versus large curds; [K] how heat and stirring shape the curd
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m3-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** scrambled eggs can be anything from a creamy, almost pourable spoonful to dry, rubbery lumps in a
puddle, from the same eggs. The difference is heat, stirring and the moment you stop. It is the clearest lesson there is
in how cooking shapes a protein network.

**You need:** 6 eggs; 40 g of butter; salt; two small pans, or one used twice; a silicone spatula; two warm plates; a
phone.

**Explanation:**

*Two styles.*
- **Soft French style:** low heat, butter, and constant stirring. The curds are tiny, the texture creamy and almost
  pourable. Take the pan off the heat just before the eggs look done, because the heat of the pan and the eggs carries
  on cooking them.
- **Large curds:** medium heat, and gentle folding with a spatula at intervals. The curds are big, soft and moist.

*Why.*
- **Stirring** breaks the setting network into pieces as it forms: constant stirring gives small curds; occasional
  folding gives large ones.
- **Heat** decides how tight the network becomes: low heat keeps it tender; high heat tightens it until it squeezes out
  water.
- **Salt** beaten into the eggs a few minutes before cooking gives a more tender curd.

*Weeping.* Scrambled eggs sitting in liquid have either been overcooked, or had wet additions (raw tomato, raw mushroom)
stirred in. Cook mushrooms first, until their water has gone.

*Serving.* Scrambled eggs go on a warm plate at once. Left in the hot pan, they overcook in a minute.

**Practice:**
1. **Two styles (45 min).** Beat the eggs with a two-finger pinch of salt, and leave them for a few minutes. Divide the
   mix in two.
   - **French:** melt 20 g of butter in a small pan over low heat, add half the eggs, and stir constantly, scraping the
     base and the sides, for about 6–10 minutes. Off the heat just before they look done; stir in a little more butter;
     serve.
   - **Large curds:** melt the rest of the butter over medium heat, add the eggs, and fold them from the edge to the
     centre every 20–30 seconds, about 2–3 minutes in all; off the heat while still moist.

   Photograph and taste both.

**Look for:** small, glossy curds that flow slowly in the French style; soft, moist folds in the large-curd style.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dry, rubbery curds in a puddle | High heat | Low heat for French style, medium for large curds |
| Perfect in the pan, dry on the plate | Serving from the hot pan, so it overcooks on the plate | Off the heat early; onto a warm plate at once |
| Grey liquid around the eggs | Raw mushrooms stirred in | Cook additions first, until dry |

**Safety:** soft scrambled eggs are soft eggs: for risk groups, cook them until fully set (§9).

**Record:** the two photos and a tasting line on each.

### EGG-1.M3.L4 — Omelettes

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m3-l4 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `EGG-1.M3.L3` · moves TS-EGG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] a rolled French omelette and a flat omelette; [J] fix tearing and browning
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m3-l4 -->

**Why it matters:** the French omelette is a classic test of a cook: pale, smooth and rolled, soft inside, made in under
two minutes. The flat omelette, the Dutch *boerenomelet* or a frittata, is its opposite: slow, filled and set through.
Between them, they teach heat control at both ends.

**You need:** for session 1, the inputs of `EGG-1.M3.X1`; for session 2, 6 eggs, about 400 g of seasonal vegetables
(cooked potato, onion and leek in winter; courgette, spring onion and fresh herbs in summer), 20 g of butter, a
tablespoon of oil, salt and pepper, the ovenproof frying pan and the oven grill.

**Explanation:**

*The French omelette.*
1. 2–3 eggs, beaten with a pinch of salt until no streaks of white remain.
2. A 20 cm pan over medium heat, with 10 g of butter, foaming but not browning.
3. Pour in the eggs and stir quickly with the back of a fork, flat against the base, while shaking the pan, until the
   eggs are a soft curd: about 20–30 seconds.
4. Spread them flat and let the base set for a few seconds.
5. Tilt the pan, roll the top third over with the fork, and roll the omelette out of the pan onto the plate, seam down.

The result is pale, with no colour, smooth outside and soft (*baveuse*) inside.

*The flat omelette* is cooked slowly with its fillings until set throughout, then often finished under the grill to set
and colour the top. The fillings are cooked first, so they are tender and dry.

*Fixes.*
- **Browning:** the pan is too hot. Lower the heat, and add the eggs while the butter foams, before it turns brown.
- **Tearing:** the egg is overcooked or sticking. Roll it sooner, while the top is still soft; use enough butter; use a
  well-seasoned or non-stick pan.

**Practice:**
1. **French omelettes (75 min).** The series of `EGG-1.M3.X1`: five in a row, one correction each time.
2. **A flat omelette (75 min).** Cook the vegetables in the butter and oil in the ovenproof pan until tender and lightly
   coloured. Heat the grill. Beat the eggs with salt and pepper, pour them over the vegetables, and cook over low heat
   without stirring for about 10 minutes, until the base is set and the top still a little liquid. Finish under the
   grill for 2–3 minutes until set and golden. Slide out, cut into wedges, and photograph a wedge.

**Look for:** a pale, smooth, rolled omelette with a soft centre; a flat omelette set through, golden on top.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A brown French omelette | The butter browned before the eggs went in | Medium heat; eggs in while the butter foams |
| A dry, overcooked centre | Rolled too late | Roll while the top is still soft |
| A watery flat omelette | Fillings added raw and wet | Cook them first, until dry |

**Safety:** the pan handle is as hot as the grill: use an oven glove, and leave a cloth over the handle afterwards as a
warning (`SAF-1.M4`). A *baveuse* omelette is a soft egg: for risk groups, the flat omelette, set through (§9).

**Record:** the photos and corrections of the series (in `EGG-1.M3.X1`), and the flat-omelette photo.

## EGG-1.M4 — Custards and eggs as binders

### EGG-1.M4.L1 — Stirred and baked custards

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m4-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 h · builds on `EGG-1.M1.L2` · moves TS-EGG→2, SC-PROT→2, JD-TRBL→1*

*Objectives:* [S] a stirred custard to the coating stage and a baked custard in a water bath; [J] rescue early curdling
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** custards are the first of the pastry foundations, and they return in `BAK-2` and `PST-3`. A stirred
custard is a lesson in stopping at exactly the right moment; a baked custard is a lesson in gentle, even heat. Both turn
on the setting temperatures of `EGG-1.M1.L2`.

**You need:** for the stirred custard, the inputs of `EGG-1.M4.X1`. For four baked custards (*crème caramel*): 140 g of
sugar (80 g for the caramel, 60 g for the custard), 400 ml of milk, 2 eggs and 2 yolks, a vanilla pod or extract; four
ramekins; a deep oven dish; a kettle; a small heavy saucepan; the probe.

**Explanation:**

*A stirred custard* (*crème anglaise*) is milk or cream, yolks and sugar, cooked gently while you stir, until it coats
the back of a spoon and a finger drawn through it leaves a clean line: the **coating stage** (*nappe*), at about
80–84 °C. Above that the proteins tighten and it curdles into sweet scrambled egg. The thickening comes suddenly near
the end, so you stir constantly and watch the spoon.

*A baked custard* (a *crème caramel* or a *flan*) sets in a water bath in a moderate oven, about 150 °C. The water
around the ramekins keeps the heat gentle and even, so the custard sets smooth, without bubbles or a curdled edge, and
keeps a slight wobble in the centre. It needs several hours in the fridge to chill and firm.

*Rescuing early curdling.* At the first tiny lumps, take the pan off the heat at once, pour the custard into a cold
bowl, blend it with a stick blender, and strain it. This works only when caught early; a fully curdled custard can't be
saved.

*Safety.* An egg-thickened dessert is cooked when every part has reached at least 71 °C, checked with a probe
([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)). A custard at the coating stage is past
that. It is cooled quickly and kept covered in the fridge.

**Practice:**
1. **Both custards (2 h active, plus about 4 h chilling).** Make the caramel first: melt 80 g of sugar with 2
   tablespoons of water over medium heat, without stirring, swirling the pan, until amber; pour it into the ramekins.
   Heat the oven. Warm the milk with the vanilla; whisk the eggs, yolks and 60 g of sugar; pour the milk on while
   whisking; strain into the ramekins. Set them in the oven dish, pour hot water from the kettle halfway up their sides,
   and bake for about 35–45 minutes, until set with a wobble at the centre. Probe the centre of one: at least 71 °C.
   Cool, and chill. While they bake, make the stirred custard of `EGG-1.M4.X1`, with its temperature logged. Serve it
   with stewed apple or pear in winter, rhubarb in spring, or strawberries in summer; the baked custards next day,
   turned out.

**Look for:** the custard thickens suddenly near the end; the line on the spoon stays clean; the baked custard trembles
as a whole when shaken gently.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Grainy, curdled custard | High heat | Low heat; at the first lumps, the rescue |
| Lumps on the base of the pan | Stopping stirring | Stir constantly, scraping the base and corners |
| Bubbled, rubbery baked custards | No water bath | A water bath, halfway up the ramekins |
| A custard that curdles after it came off the heat | Leaving the custard in the hot pan after it is done | Straight into a cold bowl |

**Safety:** caramel is far hotter than boiling water and sticks to skin: pour it carefully, keep children away, and cool
any burn at once (`SAF-1.M4.L2`). Lift the water bath out with oven gloves. Both custards into the fridge within 2
hours, eaten within 2 days. Freeze the spare whites in a labelled box for `BAK-2`.

**Record:** the stirred custard's log and spoon photo (in `EGG-1.M4.X1`), the baked custard's probe reading, and a photo
of one turned out.

### EGG-1.M4.L2 — What eggs do

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m4-l2 -->
*1 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `EGG-1.M1.L2` · moves IN-DAI→2, SC-EMUL→1*

*Objectives:* [K] binding, leavening, emulsifying, glazing, clarifying (an overview for later courses)
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** eggs do different jobs in different dishes, and often two at once. When you later adapt a recipe or
replace an ingredient (`RD-ADPT`), you need to know which job the egg was doing. This lesson is a map of those jobs and
of the courses where each returns.

**You need:** this lesson, and eight recipes from a cookbook, a website or the program.

**Explanation:**

*The functions of eggs:*

| Function | How it works | Example | Returns in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binding | Proteins set and hold the mixture together | Meatballs, *gehakt*, croquettes | `MEA-2` |
| Leavening | Whipped whites trap air, which expands in the oven | A sponge, a soufflé | `BAK-2`, `PST-3` |
| Emulsifying | The lecithin in the yolk holds oil and water together | Mayonnaise, hollandaise | `SAU-2` |
| Glazing | Proteins and sugars brown and shine | Egg wash on pastry and bread | `BAK-2`, `BRD-2` |
| Clarifying | A raft of whites traps particles as it sets | Consommé | `CLS-4` |
| Thickening | Proteins set gently in a liquid | Custards, some sauces | `EGG-1.M4.L1`, `PST-3` |

*Two functions at once.* Many recipes use eggs for more than one job.
- In **croquettes**, egg binds the filling and also glues the breadcrumbs on.
- In a **soufflé**, the yolks thicken the base and the whites leaven it.
- In a **pancake** batter, egg binds and gives some lift and colour.

*Reading a recipe for function.* Ask what would go wrong without the egg: would it fall apart (binding), stay flat
(leavening), split (emulsifying), stay pale (glazing), stay cloudy (clarifying), or stay thin (thickening)? That is the
job the egg was doing, and any replacement has to do the same one.

**Practice:** theory only (1 h): for eight recipes, name the function or functions of the eggs in each, and the course
where it returns. Use these if you have no others: meatballs, a sponge cake, mayonnaise, egg-washed bread rolls, a
consommé, a *crème anglaise*, croquettes and a cheese soufflé.

**Look for:** each function named correctly, with the later course where it returns.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Binding" written for every recipe | Thinking eggs only "hold things together" | Ask what would go wrong without the egg |
| One function named for croquettes or a soufflé | Missing that a recipe uses eggs for two functions | Look at each stage where egg is used |

**Safety:** a preparation in which the egg is raw when served, such as mayonnaise, is made with pasteurised egg for
every guest and kept at most 24 hours (assessment §9, the raw-egg row). It returns in `SAU-2`.

**Record:** the eight recipes with their functions.

### EGG-1.M4.X1 — A stirred custard with its temperature logged

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-egg-1-m4-x1 -->
*guided, recipe* — A stirred custard (*crème anglaise*) with its temperature logged.
<!-- END generated:exercise-egg-1-m4-x1 -->

**Task:** a smooth stirred custard taken to the coating stage, from a recipe, with the temperature that the cue matches.
You judge the cue first and read the thermometer second.

**You need:** 500 ml of milk (or 250 ml each of milk and cream); 5 yolks; 80 g of sugar; a vanilla pod or 1 teaspoon of
extract; a heavy saucepan; a whisk; a silicone spatula; the thermometer; a fine sieve; a bowl set in a larger bowl of
ice water; cling film; a wooden spoon for the spoon test; a timer; the journal; a phone.

**Recipe:** *crème anglaise*, for 4–6.
1. **Infuse.** Split the vanilla pod, scrape out the seeds, and put both in the pan with the milk. Heat until steaming,
   with small bubbles at the edge; take it off the heat and leave it for 15 minutes. (With extract, add it at the end.)
2. **Temper.** Whisk the yolks and sugar until pale. Reheat the milk to steaming, and pour about a third onto the yolks
   in a thin stream, whisking all the time. Pour the yolk mix back into the pan.
3. **Cook.** Over low heat, stir constantly with the spatula in a figure of eight, scraping the base and the corners.
   Put the thermometer in and log the temperature every 30 seconds.
4. **Judge by eye.** When the custard thickens, dip the wooden spoon, lift it, and draw a finger across the back. When
   the line stays clean, take the pan off the heat. Then read the thermometer, and log it: expect about 80–84 °C, and at
   least 71 °C (assessment §9).
5. **Strain and chill.** Pour it at once through the sieve into the bowl in ice water, and stir for a few minutes to
   cool it. Cover with cling film on the surface and put it in the fridge.
6. **Photograph** the spoon test.

**Show:** the coating-stage photo and the temperature log, with no curdling.
- `TS-EGG` level 2: the custard element, from a recipe, to the stated doneness.
- `IN-DAI` level 2: a recipe custard that does not curdle; with the knowledge part shown in `EGG-1.M1`, this completes
  the row.

AI review checks the line on the spoon and the smoothness: *competent* is a clean line on a coated spoon; *not yet* is
thin, or grainy with curds.

**Time:** 45 min; it keeps covered in the fridge for up to 2 days.

### EGG-1.M5.L1 — Timing a breakfast plate

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-egg-1-m5-l1 -->

*Objectives:* [S] a backwards timeline for eggs, toast, vegetables and coffee for four people; [S] a clean plate
<!-- END generated:lesson-egg-1-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** cooking one egg well is a technique; serving four hot breakfasts at once is planning. Eggs and toast
go cold in minutes, so everything has to arrive together. This is the backwards timeline of `WRK-1` at its tightest, and
your first step into plating.

**You need:** the inputs of `EGG-1.M5.X1`; the timeline template (`WRK-1.M2.X2`); four plates; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*A backwards timeline for four* (`WRK-1.M2.L3`). Start from the moment you serve and work back:
- **What can be ready early and held:** roast tomatoes or mushrooms in the oven at its lowest setting; fried potatoes
  kept warm on a tray; poached eggs held in ice water; coffee in a flask.
- **What must be last:** eggs cooked to order, and toast.
- **What runs in parallel:** the toaster and the egg pan; the kettle and the plates warming.

A sample, for serving at 9:00:

| Time | Task |
|---|---|
| 8:15 | Potatoes on; oven on low for holding |
| 8:35 | Mushrooms on; potatoes into the oven |
| 8:45 | Coffee; plates into the oven or under the hot tap |
| 8:50 | Eggs on; bread into the toaster |
| 8:57 | Plate: sides, eggs, toast |
| 9:00 | Serve |

*Warm plates.* An egg cools fast on a cold plate. Warm them for a few minutes in the oven at its lowest setting, or
under the hot tap and dried.

*A clean plate.* One clear focal point (the egg), the sides beside it, nothing piled, and the rims wiped. Four plates
that look alike: set them out in a row and add each component to all four before moving on, rather than finishing one
plate at a time. This is the first step of plating (`TS-PLT`), taught properly in `PLT-4`.

**Practice:**
1. **Breakfast for four (2 h including planning).** Run `EGG-1.M5.X1`: write the timeline, cook, log the actual times
   beside the plan, and serve.

**Look for:** everything hot and on the table within 10 minutes of the plan; four plates that look alike.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Rubbery, cold eggs | Starting the eggs first | Eggs last; the sides first, held warm |
| Cold, leathery toast | Toast made too early | Toast with the eggs, in the last minutes |
| The first plate cold, the last overcooked | Four plates assembled one by one | All four in a row; each component onto all four |

**Safety:** for a guest in a risk group, cook their egg until fully set (§9). Anything held warm is eaten at the meal;
leftovers go into the fridge within 2 hours.

**Record:** the timeline with the actual times (in `EGG-1.M5.X1`).

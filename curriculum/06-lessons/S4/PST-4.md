**Before you start.** `PST-3` completed the six pastry elements of `TS-BAK` level 4: choux, laminated dough, tarts,
creams, mousses and chocolate. This course adds the components of the restaurant dessert and teaches how to put them
together:
- **meringues:** French, Swiss and Italian, and what each is for;
- **ice cream and sorbet:** the science of freezing point, fat and air; a custard-base ice cream with or without a
  machine; sorbets by ratio;
- **sugar work:** the sugar stages, dry and wet caramel, caramel sauce, praline and brittle;
- **a layered dessert:** sponge, mousse, insert, crunch and glaze, planned over two days;
- **the plated dessert:** contrast of temperature, texture and taste, plated four times alike.

Eggs follow the egg rows of assessment §9:
- an egg-thickened base cooked for later (custard, ice-cream base, Swiss meringue) reaches 71 °C in every part, checked

- where the egg is raw or lightly cooked when served (an uncooked ice-cream base, a mousse lightened with uncooked
  meringue, an Italian meringue that stays below 71 °C), use pasteurised eggs or egg products, for every guest, and
  keep the dish at most 24 hours.

Fruit is Dutch and in season: rhubarb, strawberries, red currants, cherries, plums, apples and pears. Chocolate,
vanilla and citrus are pantry goods. Nuts are replaced by seeds by default, as in S3: the praline is made with pumpkin

### PST-4.M1.L1 — Three meringues

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m1-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M4.L2` · moves TS-BAK→4, IN-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] French, Swiss and Italian; [K] how sugar and heat stabilise egg foam
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the three meringues use the same egg white and sugar, but differ in stability and in safety. Knowing
which one a dessert needs, and why, comes before every pavlova, buttercream and mousse in this course.

**You need:** 300 g of egg white (about 10 eggs; pasteurised white from a carton for the Italian meringue) and 600 g of
caster sugar, in three batches of 100 g and 200 g; a mixer with a clean, dry, grease-free bowl; a heatproof bowl over a
pan of simmering water; a small heavy pan; the sugar thermometer and the probe; a spatula.

**Explanation:**

*How sugar and heat stabilise egg foam.* Whisking unfolds the egg-white proteins, which line the air bubbles. Sugar
dissolves into the water between the bubbles and makes it thicker, so the foam drains and collapses more slowly. Heat
sets the proteins and makes the foam permanent. Fat (a trace of yolk, a greasy bowl) stops the foam forming.

*The three meringues:*
- ***French*:** whites whisked to soft peaks, then the sugar added a spoon at a time until stiff and glossy. The least
  stable; baked dry (meringues, pavlova) or folded into batters. Raw when used uncooked.
- ***Swiss*:** whites and sugar warmed together over the water bath, whisking, until the sugar dissolves and the
  mixture reaches 71 °C on the probe (assessment §9); then whisked off the heat until stiff and cool. Dense, glossy and
  stable.
- ***Italian*:** a sugar syrup cooked to 118–121 °C and poured in a thin stream onto whites whisked to soft peaks, then
  whisked until cool. The most stable, used for mousses, buttercream and toppings. The hot syrup alone does not bring
  every part to 71 °C, so where it stays uncooked, use pasteurised whites (§9).

*The ratio.* About twice the sugar to the egg white by weight for a firm meringue (100 g of white to 200 g of sugar);
less for soft toppings.

**Practice:**
1. **French and Swiss (60 min).** Make 100 g of white and 200 g of sugar both ways. Probe the Swiss to 71 °C before
   whisking off the heat. Leave a spoonful of each on a plate for 30 minutes and compare how much each weeps or slumps.
2. **Italian (60 min).** Cook 200 g of sugar with 60 ml of water to 118–121 °C on the thermometer, and pour it down the
   side of the bowl onto 100 g of pasteurised whites at soft peaks, the mixer running. Whisk until cool; top a dessert
   with it.

**Look for:**
- a glossy meringue that holds a stiff peak and feels smooth between the fingers (no sugar grains);
- the Swiss mixture at 71 °C on the probe before you whisk it off the heat.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Whites that won't whip | A trace of yolk or a greasy bowl | A clean bowl; separate eggs one at a time into a cup |
| Low volume | Sugar added too early or all at once | Soft peaks first, then sugar gradually |
| Grainy, dry whites | Overwhipped | Stop at glossy and stiff |
| Threads of sugar on the whisk | Syrup poured onto the whisk | Pour down the side of the bowl |

**Safety:** the Swiss meringue to 71 °C on the probe; pasteurised whites for an Italian meringue served uncooked, and
for French meringue served raw (§9, the raw-egg row). Syrup at 118–121 °C burns deeply: long sleeves, and pour away from
you (`SAF-3.M2.L2`).

**Record:** the probe reading of the Swiss meringue; the syrup temperature; photos of the three after 30 minutes and
which held best.

### PST-4.M1.L2 — Meringue applications

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m1-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 2 h · builds on `PST-4.M1.L1` · moves TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] pavlova, Italian meringue for mousse or buttercream, toasted topping
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a pavlova, an Italian meringue buttercream and a torched topping are three desserts from one foam.
Making them shows what the stability of each meringue is good for.

**You need:** for the pavlova, 4 egg whites (about 130 g), 260 g of caster sugar, 1 teaspoon of cornflour and 1
teaspoon of white-wine vinegar; 250 ml of cream; seasonal Dutch fruit (rhubarb poached, strawberries, red currants,
plums or pears); for the topping and buttercream, the Italian meringue of `PST-4.M1.L1` with pasteurised whites, and
200 g of soft butter; a shortcrust tart base (`BAK-2`) or a lemon tart (`PST-3`); piping bags with plain and star
nozzles; the blowtorch; parchment and a baking tray.

**Explanation:**

*Pavlova.* A French meringue with a little cornflour and vinegar, which keep the inside soft. Shape it as a nest on
parchment. Bake it at a low temperature (about 120 °C) until crisp outside and marshmallowy inside, then cool it in the
switched-off oven with the door ajar, to avoid cracks. Top it at the last moment with whipped cream and seasonal fruit.

*Italian meringue at work:*
- **in a mousse:** folded into a fruit purée or a cream for lightness and body (with pasteurised whites; such a mousse
  is kept at most 24 hours, §9);
- **in buttercream:** soft butter beaten into the cooled meringue a piece at a time (Italian meringue buttercream). It
  may look curdled halfway; keep beating until it turns smooth.

*A toasted topping.* Swiss or Italian meringue piped onto a tart or a dessert and coloured with a blowtorch. Use the
torch as its maker's instructions say: on a stable surface, away from your body and anything that burns, the flame
moving, the gas closed after use (assessment §9).

*Piping* (`PST-3`). A bag with a plain or star nozzle, filled half full, twisted shut, and squeezed with even pressure
from the top while the other hand guides.

**Practice:**
1. **A pavlova (60 min active, 2 h elapsed).** Bake and cool it in the oven; top it with cream and seasonal fruit just
   before serving.
2. **Toppings and fillings (60 min).** Pipe an Italian meringue on a tart and torch it. Make an Italian meringue
   buttercream and pipe a few rosettes.

**Look for:**
- a pavlova crisp outside, soft inside, with few cracks;
- even, golden peaks from the blowtorch;
- a smooth buttercream that holds its shape.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A brown pavlova weeping sugar | Baked too hot | About 120 °C; check your oven with a thermometer |
| A soft, wet pavlova | Cream and fruit added hours early | Top it just before serving |
| Black peaks | Blowtorch too close | Keep the flame moving, a hand's width away |

**Safety:** the blowtorch by its maker's instructions (assessment §9, tier 3); pasteurised whites wherever the meringue
is served uncooked; desserts with cream in the fridge, out of it for at most 2 hours.

**Record:** the oven temperature and baking time; photos of the pavlova whole and cut; the torched tart.

### PST-4.M2.L1 — Ice cream science

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `PST-3.M4.L1` · moves IN-BAK→4, FL-TEXT→4*

*Objectives:* [K] sugar and freezing point, fat, solids, air, ice crystals
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** an ice cream is a balance of water, sugar, fat, solids and air. Writing it as a balance sheet in
percentages lets you predict why one batch is icy and another syrupy, and change one thing at a time.

**You need:** for one base: 500 g of whole milk, 250 g of cream (35 % fat), 6 egg yolks (about 120 g) and 150 g of
caster sugar; 30 g of dextrose; the precision scale; a saucepan; the probe; three small freezer tubs with lids,
labelled; a freezer thermometer; the balance-sheet template.

**Explanation:**

*Sugar and freezing point.* Dissolved sugar lowers the freezing point, so part of the water stays liquid at freezer
temperature and the ice cream stays scoopable. Too little sugar gives a hard, icy block; too much a soft, syrupy one.
Glucose syrup, dextrose and invert sugar lower it more than sucrose does for the same sweetness.

*Fat.* Milk fat, about 10–16 % of the mix in a custard ice cream, gives creaminess and slows melting. Egg yolk adds fat
and emulsifiers that keep the texture smooth.

*Solids.* Milk solids, sugar and fat together, about 36–42 % of the mix, bind water, so fewer, smaller ice crystals
form.

*Air.* Churning beats in air (the overrun) while freezing. Home machines reach about 20–30 %, which keeps the ice cream
dense and rich.

*Ice crystals.* Fast freezing and constant stirring give small crystals: smooth. Slow freezing and temperature swings
give large ones: icy. A pre-chilled base and a cold machine bowl help.

*The balance sheet.* Write the formula in percentages of the total mix, so it can be checked and changed:

| Ingredient | Weight | % of mix | Fat (from the label) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole milk | 500 g | 49 % | 3.5 % of 500 g = 17.5 g |
| Cream | 250 g | 24 % | 35 % of 250 g = 87.5 g |
| Yolks | 120 g | 12 % | From a food composition table |
| Sugar | 150 g | 15 % | — |
| **Total** | **1,020 g** | **100 %** | Add the yolk fat; divide by 1,020 |

Use the fat figures on your own labels; Dutch whole milk and *slagroom* state them.

**Practice:**
1. **One base, three changes (60 min, then frozen overnight).** Make the base as a custard (`PST-4.M2.L2`), probed to
   71 °C and cooled. Divide it in three: as written; with 3 % less sugar (about 10 g less per third); and with part of
   the sugar replaced by dextrose (10 g). Still-freeze all three in labelled tubs, side by side. Scoop and compare them
   the next day, at the same freezer temperature.

**Look for:** you can explain each result (harder, softer, smoother) from the balance sheet.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Results you can't explain | Two variables changed at once | Change one thing per sample |
| An unfair comparison | Samples at different freezer temperatures | Same shelf, same time |

**Safety:** the custard base to 71 °C in every part, checked with a probe, and cooled by the cooling rule (assessment
§9); the freezer at −18 °C or below.

**Record:** the balance sheet; the three samples' hardness and texture; your explanation of each.

### PST-4.M2.L2 — Custard-base ice cream

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m2-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 1 d · builds on `PST-4.M2.L1` · moves TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] with a machine, and still-frozen without one
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** the custard base is the classic ice cream, and it can be made with or without a machine. Cooked to
71 °C, aged overnight and frozen fast, it gives a smooth, dense scoop that is safe for every guest.

**You need:** the base of `PST-4.M2.L1` (milk, cream, yolks, sugar); an infusion (a vanilla pod, a few sprigs of lemon
verbena, or 30 g of toasted pumpkin seeds); a heavy saucepan; a whisk and spatula; the probe; a fine sieve; an ice bath;
a lidded container; the ice-cream machine if you have one (bowl frozen the day before); a shallow metal tray and a fork
for still-freezing.

**Explanation:**

*The base* (`PST-3.M4.L1`):
1. Heat the milk and cream with the infusion; cover and leave for 20 minutes.
2. Whisk the yolks and sugar.
3. Pour the warm milk onto the yolks, whisking (tempering), and return all to the pan.
4. Cook, stirring with a spatula over low heat, until the custard coats a spoon (*nappe*) and every part reads above
   71 °C on the probe (assessment §9), but below about 84 °C, where it curdles.

*Cooling and ageing.* Strain the custard, cool it fast in an ice bath (the cooling rule of §9), and age it in the fridge
for at least 4 hours or overnight: the fat crystallises and the proteins hydrate, which makes a smoother ice cream.

*With a machine.* Freeze the bowl (or run the compressor) before churning. Churn the cold base until it looks like
soft-serve, then harden it in the freezer for a few hours.

*Without a machine (still-frozen).* Freeze the base in a shallow tray and stir it hard with a fork or whisk every 30
minutes for 2–3 hours. Or freeze it solid in cubes and blend. For smoother results, fold whipped cream into a richer
base (a *parfait*), so the air comes from the cream.

*Storage.* At −18 °C or below (assessment §9), covered with paper pressed onto the surface; best within a week or two
for texture. Melted ice cream is not refrozen.

**Practice:**
1. **The base (45 min, aged overnight).** A vanilla or seasonal infusion; log the probe readings and the cooling.
2. **Churned and still-frozen (60 min).** Churn half in a machine, if you have one, and still-freeze the other half.
   Compare their texture at the same temperature.

**Look for:**
- a custard that coats a spoon, with the probe log above 71 °C;
- a smooth, dense scoop that melts cleanly.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A curdled custard | Boiled | Low heat, stir; take it off below 84 °C; blend if it is only slightly grainy |
| Never freezes properly in the machine | A warm base churned | Age it cold overnight |
| Icy, crunchy ice cream | Left soft on the counter and refrozen | Serve and return quickly; never refreeze melted |

**Safety:** every part of the custard above 71 °C (the egg-thickened row); cooled by the cooling rule; stored at −18 °C
or below; melted ice cream not refrozen (assessment §9).

**Record:** the probe and cooling logs; the infusion and time; photos of both scoops; which texture was smoother.

### PST-4.M2.L3 — Sorbets and granitas

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m2-l3 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 6 h · builds on `PST-4.M2.L1` · moves RD-RATO→4*

*Objectives:* [S] sugar ratio by taste and by measurement; [J] adjust sweetness for serving temperature
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m2-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a sorbet is fruit, sugar and water, and the sugar decides everything. Balancing it by measurement
and by taste, and tasting for the serving temperature, gives a sorbet that scoops and tastes fresh.

**You need:** 500 g of a seasonal Dutch fruit purée (rhubarb poached and blended, strawberries, red currants, plums);
a second fruit for the sorbet by taste; caster sugar and water for a syrup; a lemon; the refractometer if you have one;
a clean, washed raw egg for the egg test; the scale; a shallow tray and a fork; the machine or tubs for still-freezing.

**Explanation:**

*The sorbet.* Fruit purée or juice, sugar syrup and water; no dairy. The sugar decides the texture: about 25–30 % of
the mix for most fruit sorbets, counting the fruit's own sugar.

*Measuring sugar.*
- **By measurement:** a refractometer reads the sugar as °Brix (aim for around 25–30 °Brix). Without one, calculate it:
  read the purée's sugar first, then add sugar until the whole mix reaches the target. A worked example: 500 g of purée
  that reads 8 °Brix (40 g of sugar), with 100 g of water, needs about 180 g of added sugar to reach 28 %:
  (40 + 180) ÷ (600 + 180) is 0.28.
- **By the egg test:** a clean, washed egg floated in the mix shows a circle about the size of a coin above the surface
  when the sugar is right. Quick, but rough.

*Sweetness and serving temperature.* Cold dulls sweetness, so a base tasted at room temperature must taste slightly too
sweet. Acid (lemon, or a tart fruit) keeps it fresh.

*The granita.* A looser mix with less sugar, frozen in a tray and scraped with a fork every 30 minutes into flakes.

*Seasonal Dutch fruit.* Rhubarb, strawberries, red currants, cherries, plums, apples (with cider or *appelstroop*), and
pears.

**Practice:**
1. **A sorbet by measurement (60 min, then frozen).** Balance a fruit sorbet by calculation or refractometer; churn or
   still-freeze it.
2. **A sorbet by taste and a granita (60 min, then frozen).** Balance a second fruit by taste and the egg test. Make a
   granita from the rest with less sugar.

**Look for:**
- a sorbet that scoops smoothly from the freezer;
- sweetness that seems right when cold, with a fresh finish;
- a granita of separate, glittering flakes.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Rock hard | Too little sugar | Measure; aim for 25–30 % |
| Flat and too sweet, or sour, when served | Tasted only cold from the fridge | Taste at room temperature too |
| A block, not flakes | The granita left to freeze | Scrape every 30 minutes |

**Safety:** the egg for the test washed before and after, and not eaten; fruit washed; the freezer at −18 °C or below.

**Record:** the calculation or the °Brix readings; the egg-test result; photos of the scoops and the granita.

### PST-4.M3.L1 — Sugar stages and caramel

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-1.M2.L2`, `SAF-3.M2.L2` · moves SC-BRWN→4*

*Objectives:* [K] stages by temperature; dry versus wet caramel; [S] safety with hot sugar
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** sugar changes with every degree, from syrup to fudge to toffee to caramel. Reading the stages, and
making caramel without burns, opens every sugar component of the course.

**You need:** 1 kg of caster sugar; water; a heavy saucepan with a light-coloured inside; a pastry brush and a cup of
water; the sugar thermometer; a bowl of cold water for the tests; a white plate; long sleeves; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Stages by temperature.* As a syrup boils, water leaves and the temperature rises with the sugar concentration:

| Stage | Temperature | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Thread | about 110 °C | Syrups |
| Soft ball | 112–116 °C | Fudge |
| Firm ball | 118–120 °C | Italian meringue |
| Hard ball | 121–130 °C | Nougat, marshmallow |
| Soft crack | 132–143 °C | Toffee |
| Hard crack | 146–154 °C | Brittle, spun sugar |
| Caramel | from about 160 °C | Light to dark |

A sugar thermometer reads them; the cold-water test confirms them: drop a little syrup into cold water and feel it.

*Dry against wet caramel:*
- **dry:** sugar melted alone in a heavy pan, stirred only once it starts to melt at the edges; fast, but easy to burn;
- **wet:** sugar with water, boiled without stirring, the sides brushed with water, until it colours; slower and more
  even, but it can crystallise if stirred or seeded with stray crystals.

*Browning steered on purpose* (`SC-BRWN`). Choose the colour for the use: light for a glaze, deep amber for flavour.
Stop it at once by taking the pan off the heat and adding the liquid, or by dipping the base of the pan in cold water.

*Safety with hot sugar* (`SAF-3.M2.L2`). Caramel at 170 °C sticks to skin and burns deeply:
- long sleeves, a dry heavy pan, the handle turned in, no children nearby;
- a bowl of cold water ready for testing;
- cream added off the heat and slowly, because it boils up;
- a burn cooled at once under lukewarm running water, for as long as the first-aid guidance says (`SAF-1`, [S49]).

**Practice:**
1. **The stages (60 min).** Take a syrup of 300 g of sugar and 100 ml of water through the stages. Confirm each with the
   cold-water test, and photograph it.
2. **Dry and wet caramel (45 min).** One of each with 150 g of sugar, stopped at the same colour; log the times and
   temperatures.

**Look for:**
- the cold-water test matching the thermometer;
- an even amber caramel with no burnt smell.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A cloudy, grainy wet caramel | Stirred, or stray crystals | Swirl, don't stir; brush the sides |
| Burnt caramel | Looked away at 170 °C | Stay with the pan; stop it a shade early |
| Spitting caramel | Cold cream poured in fast | Warm cream, off the heat, in stages |

**Safety:** hot-sugar rules as above; the thermometer clipped to the pan, never held over it; the pan cooled before
washing.

**Record:** the stage photos with the thermometer readings; the times and colours of the two caramels.

### PST-4.M3.L2 — Caramel sauces, praline and brittle

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `PST-4.M3.L1` · moves TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] a salted caramel sauce, praline paste, a brittle
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** caramel becomes three of the most useful dessert components: a sauce, a paste and a crisp. Each
needs the caramel taken to a real colour, and each keeps only if it is stored the right way.

**You need:** caster sugar (about 600 g over both sessions); 200 ml of cream (35 % fat), warmed; 60 g of butter; salt;
250 g of pumpkin or sunflower seeds; a pinch of bicarbonate of soda; a heavy saucepan; the sugar thermometer; a silicone
mat or parchment on a sheet pan; an offset spatula; a blender or food processor; airtight jars; long sleeves.

**Explanation:**

*Salted caramel sauce.* Take a dry or wet caramel (`PST-4.M3.L1`) to deep amber. Off the heat, add the warm cream in
stages, stirring; it boils up hard. Then stir in the butter and salt, about 1 % of the sauce's weight. More cream gives
a thinner sauce, less a thicker one: write down your amount.

*Praline paste.*
1. Toast the seeds until they smell nutty; keep them warm on the mat.
2. Make a caramel and pour it over the seeds.
3. Cool it completely, then break it into pieces.
4. Blend until the oil is released and the crumbs turn to a smooth paste. This takes several minutes; stop to scrape
   down the sides, and let the motor rest if it warms.

Hazelnuts or almonds are used only with the allergen rule: every guest asked, and the label says so.

*Brittle.* Take sugar to hard crack (146–154 °C) with a little butter, stir in the seeds and a pinch of bicarbonate for
an airy texture, and spread it thin on the mat at once. Break it when it is cold.

*Storage.* Brittle and praline in an airtight jar: humidity softens sugar work. Caramel sauce in the fridge, covered.

**Practice:**
1. **Caramel sauce and brittle (60 min).** Make both. Photograph the brittle's colour against a white plate, and snap a
   piece to show the break.
2. **Praline paste (60 min).** Make a seed praline and blend it to a paste. Use it in a cream (folded into whipped
   cream, for example) or as a spread.

**Look for:**
- a sauce that coats a spoon and pours when warm;
- a brittle that snaps cleanly;
- a praline paste that is smooth and glossy.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A sauce that is only sweet | Caramel too pale | Take it to deep amber before the cream |
| A sandy praline | Blended too briefly | Keep blending until the oil releases |
| Sticky brittle the next day | Left open overnight | Into a jar as soon as it is cold |

**Safety:** the hot-sugar rules of `PST-4.M3.L1`; cream added off the heat and in stages; the caramel left to cool on
the mat before touching it. Seeds and any nuts listed for guests; nuts only with the allergen rule.

**Record:** the cream amount and the sauce's thickness; the brittle photo; the blending time for the praline.

### PST-4.M4.L1 — Components

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m4-l1 -->
*3.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `PST-3.M4.L3` · moves TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [K] sponge, mousse, insert, crunch, glaze; [S] make the components
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a layered dessert (an *entremets*) is a set of separate components built into one slice. Each must
be right on its own, and made at the right time, before assembly can work.

**You need:** for a 18 cm ring: 3 eggs, 90 g of sugar and 90 g of flour for a thin sponge sheet; 300 g of seasonal fruit
(rhubarb, plums or apples) with sugar and 2 sheets of gelatin for the insert; 60 g of the seed praline of `PST-4.M3`
with 30 g of puffed rice and 20 g of melted chocolate for the crunch; the mousse ingredients of `PST-3.M4.L3`; a
smaller ring or a silicone mould for the insert; sheet pans and parchment; the freezer; clingfilm; labels.

**Explanation:**

*The components of an* entremets:
- **a sponge base** (a *génoise* or a *biscuit*), thin and even, sometimes soaked with a syrup;
- **a mousse** (`PST-3.M4.L3`) set with gelatin: the body of the dessert;
- **an insert:** a contrasting centre (a fruit *compote* set with gelatin or pectin, a *crémeux*, a caramel), frozen
  first so it holds its shape;
- **a crunch:** a thin crisp layer, such as the seed praline of `PST-4.M3` with puffed rice or crushed wafers;
- **a glaze:** a shiny mirror glaze (sugar, glucose, condensed milk, chocolate and gelatin) or a fruit glaze, poured
  over the frozen dessert.

*Planning the flavours.* Choose one main flavour, one contrast and one texture accent. Taste each component alone and
then together, before you build.

*The crunch.* Fat in the crunch (the praline's oil, the melted chocolate) seals it, so it stays crisp under the mousse.
Spread it thin, in a circle the size of the sponge, and chill it.

*Making ahead.* Make the insert and sponge on day 1. Keep the components covered in the fridge, or freeze them:
- cooked creams within 2 days;
- a mousse with raw or lightly cooked egg (such as uncooked Italian meringue) within 24 hours (assessment §9).

**Practice:**
1. **The components (3.5 h over a day).** Bake a sponge sheet and cut a disc a little smaller than the ring. Cook the
   fruit insert, set it in the smaller mould and freeze it. Make the crunch layer. Prepare the mousse base (the
   whipped elements are finished at assembly in `PST-4.M4.L2`). Taste and photograph each one.

**Look for:**
- an even, springy sponge;
- an insert frozen solid, the size of the ring's inner mould;
- a crunch that stays crisp.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The sponge dominates the slice | Too thick | Spread it thinner; cut it in half horizontally |
| The insert melts during assembly | Not frozen solid | Freeze overnight; take it out at the last moment |
| A soggy crunch | No fat to seal it | Bind it with praline or melted chocolate |

**Safety:** the gelatin insert and the creams cooled by the cooling rule and kept at 5 °C or below; cooked creams used
within 2 days, a raw-egg mousse within 24 hours (assessment §9); every component labelled with its date.

**Record:** each component's photo and taste note; the freezer and fridge labels.

### PST-4.M4.L2 — Assembling, setting and glazing

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m4-l2 -->
*3.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `PST-4.M4.L1` · moves TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] assemble in a ring, freeze, glaze; [J] plan over two days
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** assembly turns the components into one clean slice. It works only if each step happens at the right
temperature, and if the two days are planned backwards from the moment of serving.

**You need:** the components of `PST-4.M4.L1`; the 18 cm ring; acetate strips the height of the ring; a flat board
lined with clingfilm or acetate; a piping bag; an offset spatula; the mirror-glaze ingredients (sugar, glucose,
condensed milk, chocolate, gelatin); the thermometer; a wire rack over a tray; a stick blender; a long knife and a jug
of hot water.

**Explanation:**

*Assembling in a ring.* Line the ring with acetate. Upside down:
1. Pipe in half the mousse and spread it up the sides.
2. Press in the frozen insert.
3. Pipe in more mousse.
4. Lay the crunch, then the sponge last.
5. Smooth the top flat.

Right way up is the reverse.

*Freezing.* Freeze it overnight, so the dessert is solid enough to glaze and unmould cleanly.

*Glazing.*
1. Bring the glaze to its pouring temperature: about 30–35 °C for a mirror glaze. The thermometer decides.
2. Blend it with the stick blender kept under the surface, so no air goes in.
3. Pour it over the frozen dessert on a rack in one movement.
4. Let the excess drip off, then move the dessert to a board.

*Thawing.* Thaw it in the fridge for several hours before serving, at 5 °C or below, and eat it within 2 days of
thawing.

*Slicing.* A long knife dipped in hot water and wiped dry between cuts.

*Planning over two days.* Write a plan backwards from service: components, assembly, freezing, glazing, thawing. Check
the fridge and freezer space before you start.

**Practice:**
1. **Assembly, glazing and slicing (over two days).**
   - Day 1 (2 h): assemble the dessert from `PST-4.M4.L1`'s components and freeze it overnight.
   - Day 2 (1.5 h): glaze, thaw and slice it with a hot knife; photograph the cross-section.

**Look for:**
- straight sides and clean layers in the slice;
- an even, glossy glaze with no bubbles;
- the plan followed without a rush.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The glaze runs off thin | Too hot | Cool it to its pouring temperature |
| The glaze sets in lumps | Too cold | Warm it gently and blend again |
| The glaze melts the surface | Not frozen solid | Freeze overnight |
| Torn sides | No acetate | Always line the ring |

**Safety:** the thawed dessert kept at 5 °C or below and eaten within 2 days of thawing; a dessert with a raw-egg
component within its 24 hours (assessment §9); the knife wiped and kept flat on the board.

**Record:** the two-day plan with what actually happened; the glaze temperature; the slice photo.

### PST-4.M5.L1 — Components of a plated dessert

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m5-l1 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · builds on `FLV-3.M4.L1` · moves FL-TEXT→4, CR-COMP→4*

*Objectives:* [K] hot and cold, soft and crisp, sweet and sharp; [S] choose and prepare components
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a plated dessert is built from a handful of components, each chosen for the contrast it brings. One
idea holds them together; a component without a reason is left out.

**You need:** the components you know from `PST-3` and this course; seasonal Dutch fruit; paper and a pencil for
sketches; one plate and the ingredients for a test plate.

**Explanation:**

*Contrast* (`FLV-3.M4.L1`):
- **temperature:** hot and cold (a warm cake with an ice cream);
- **texture:** soft and crisp (a cream with a tuile or a crumble);
- **taste:** sweet and sharp (a rich element with a sharp fruit or a sorbet), with a touch of salt or bitterness.

*The components.* A main element, a sauce, a frozen element, a crisp element and a fresh element: choose three to five,
each with a reason.

*The idea first* (`CR-COMP`). Write one sentence that says what the dessert is about, for example "autumn apple: warm,
sharp and crisp". Every component serves it, or goes.

*Choosing from what you know.* Components from `PST-3` and this course; seasonal Dutch fruit; a modern element from
`MOD-4` if it serves the idea.

*Timing.* Decide what is made ahead (frozen elements, sauces, crisps in an airtight box) and what is done at service
(warm elements, the last plating).

A worked example, "autumn apple: warm, sharp and crisp":

| Component | Contrast | Made |
|---|---|---|
| Apple baked with butter and sugar | Warm, soft | At service |
| Apple and cider sorbet | Cold, sharp | Ahead |
| Seed brittle | Crisp | Ahead, in a jar |
| Salted caramel sauce | Rich, a touch of salt | Ahead, warmed |

**Practice:**
1. **Designing and testing (90 min).** Sketch two dessert ideas with their components and contrasts. Test one as a
   single plate, and adjust the components.

**Look for:** each component named with the contrast it brings and the idea it serves.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A crowded plate | Too many components, no idea | Write the idea; drop what doesn't serve it |
| A flat, heavy dessert | Everything soft and sweet | Add a crisp and a sharp element |
| The crisp goes soft on the plate | It touches a wet sauce | Put it on a dry spot; add it last |

**Safety:** components kept within their times (assessment §9); a raw-egg element marked, and offered to risk groups
only in its pasteurised form; allergens listed for each component.

**Record:** both sketches; the test plate photo; what you changed and why.

### PST-4.M5.L2 — Building and plating

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-4-m5-l2 -->
*3.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `PST-4.M5.L1` · moves TS-PLT→4*

*Objectives:* [S] plate four identical desserts; [J] timing at service
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-4-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** guests judge a plated dessert first by its look, and by whether every plate matches. Plating four
alike, with the warm element warm and the frozen one firm, takes a sketch, a line and a plan for timing.

**You need:** four matching plates; the components of `PST-4.M5.L1`'s dessert; the plating tools (two spoons, an offset
spatula, a squeeze bottle); the sketch; a timer; a camera.

**Explanation:**

*The sketch.* Draw the plate from above: where each component goes, its size and the space left empty.

*Plating several alike.* Line the plates up and add each component to all plates in turn, as an assembly line, with the
same spoon, nozzle and portion. The frozen element goes on last, just before the plates leave.

*Timing at service.* Desserts are plated after the main course is cleared, so you need a plan:
- warm the oven for the warm element during the main course;
- temper the ice cream in the fridge for 10 minutes so it scoops;
- bring the sauces to temperature.

*Portions.* Weigh or measure each component for the first plate and repeat the same amount: one scoop size, one spoon
of sauce, one piece of crisp. Keep the plate rims clean; wipe drips with a damp cloth before the plate leaves.

*Checking the plates.* Photograph the four together and compare them with the sketch. Fix any plate that differs before
it leaves.

**Practice:**
1. **A practice plating (60 min).** Plate four of a simple dessert to a sketch; photograph them together.
2. **A timed service (90 min).** Plate the full dessert for four after a household main course, timed from clearing to
   serving.

**Look for:**
- four plates that match the sketch and each other;
- the warm element warm and the frozen element firm when served.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The first plate waits and melts | Each plate finished before the next | Plate in a line, component by component |
| Melted ice cream | Scooped too early | Scoop last, just before serving |
| A crowded plate | No empty space | Follow the sketch's empty space |

**Safety:** the oven and hot pans handled with dry cloths; plates wiped with a clean cloth, not with fingers.

**Record:** the sketch next to the photo of the four plates; the times from clearing to serving.

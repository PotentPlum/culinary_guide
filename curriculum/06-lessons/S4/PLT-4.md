**Before you start.** Until now the program asked for food that is cooked well and served hot. This course adds intent
to how it looks:
- **composition:** colour, height, negative space, a focal point, odd numbers; the plate's size, shape and colour;
  portions that fit the course;
- **saucing and garnish:** pools, lines and dots, sauce at the table, and garnish that is edible and adds flavour;
- **styles:** classical, family-style and modern, and which suits which occasion;
- **photography for evidence:** a photo corner that makes every later photo reliable for AI review and for your own
  record.

Plating serves the food: a plate is judged hot and good to eat first, then composed. The dishes are your own repertoire,
cooked with Dutch seasonal produce, so the course costs little beyond household meals.

Plan about two weeks at 6.5 hours a week, or run it alongside another S4 course: each lesson is plated on household
meals, with two guest dinners.

**Have ready:** plain white plates in three sizes, and a bowl; one dark plate if you have one; a platter and serving
bowls with a spoon for each; two spoons, a small offset spatula and a squeeze bottle; a damp cloth for rims; the
instant-read probe; paper and a pencil for sketches; for the photo corner, a window, a plain matt grey or white board,
a white card, a phone stand, a ruler or the grid board.

## PLT-4.M1 — Visual composition

### PLT-4.M1.L1 — Composition

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-plt-4-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · moves TS-PLT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] colour, height, negative space, focal point, odd numbers; [S] plate one dish three ways
<!-- END generated:lesson-plt-4-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a few principles decide whether a plate reads clearly or looks crowded. Sketching the plate first,
and plating it hot and fast, lets you choose the look instead of finding it by accident.

**You need:** a household main course with at least three components (for example a fish, a seasonal vegetable purée
and glazed vegetables); three warm plates of the same kind; the probe; two spoons and an offset spatula; paper for three
sketches; a phone for photos.

**Explanation:**

*The principles:*
- **colour:** contrast between the components (green against brown, white against dark). The colour comes from the
  food, not from decoration.
- **height:** a little height draws the eye and shows care. Lean the components against each other; don't build towers
  that fall.
- **negative space:** empty plate around the food, so it reads clearly; on a composed plate, leave about a third of the
  plate free.
- **focal point:** one place the eye goes first, usually the main item, often slightly off-centre.
- **odd numbers:** three or five pieces look natural; even numbers look arranged.
- **flow:** place the components so the guest can eat them together, in the order you intend.

*The sketch.* Draw the plate from above before you plate: where each item goes, how large, and the empty space.

*Hot food first.* Warm the plates to about 60 °C, in a low oven, or with hot water and then dried. Plate fast; the plate
leaves within a minute. A beautiful cold plate of hot food is a failure.

Three layouts to sketch:

| Layout | Focal point | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Centred | The main item in the middle, the rest around it | Classical dishes |
| Off-centre | The main item a third of the way across, sauce and garnish leading to it | Modern composed plates |
| Linear | The components in a line or a gentle curve | Several small elements |

**Practice:**
1. **One dish three ways (60 min).** Plate a household main course three ways from three sketches: centred, off-centre
   and linear. Photograph each from above and at 45°, and compare.

**Look for:**
- a clear focal point in each version;
- the food hot when the photo is taken.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A crowded plate | Food spread to the rim | Leave about a third free; use a larger plate |
| A stack that topples on the way | Built too high | Lean components; keep height low and stable |
| Decoration left on the plate | Inedible garnish | Only what is eaten (`PLT-4.M2.L1`) |

**Safety:** plates warmed in a low oven handled with dry cloths; hot food kept hot until plated, at 57 °C or above if it
waits (assessment §9).

**Record:** the three sketches next to the three photos; which you prefer and why.

### PLT-4.M1.L2 — Plates and portions

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-plt-4-m1-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `PLT-4.M1.L1` · moves TS-PLT→3*

*Objectives:* [J] plate size, shape and colour; portion size for the course
<!-- END generated:lesson-plt-4-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** the same food looks lost on one plate and crowded on another, and a menu of large portions tires a
guest before dessert. The plate and the portion are choices you make for the whole menu.

**You need:** a starter of your repertoire; three plates of different size and colour (a large white, a small white, a
dark or coloured one); the scale; a phone for photos.

**Explanation:**

*The plate.*
- White and plain shows food best; dark plates suit pale food.
- A wide rim frames a small portion.
- A bowl holds sauce and keeps food warm.
- Match the size to the portion: a starter on a large plate looks lost; a main course on a small plate looks crowded.

*Shape.* Round plates for most food; rectangular or oval for long items (a whole fish, a slice of terrine).

*Portion size for the course.* Judge the portion against the whole menu, not only the dish:

| Course | Main item |
|---|---|
| Starter | About 80–120 g |
| Main course | 150–200 g of protein, with vegetables and starch |
| Dessert | Small and intense |

Weigh the main item the first time, so your eye learns what 100 g looks like on a plate.

*Judging the menu.* Write the portions of every course in a column and read them together. If the starter is rich,
make the main course lighter or smaller; if the main course is large, keep the dessert small. A guest should finish
every course and still want the next.

**Practice:**
1. **Plate choices (60 min).** Put the same starter on three plates of different size and colour. Photograph each, and
   write which you would choose and why.

**Look for:** your choice of plate and portion has a reason a guest would see.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The menu feels too heavy | A large portion on every course | Plan the portions across the menu |
| The plate fights the food | A coloured pattern plate | Plain plates; dark only for pale food |

**Safety:** none beyond the kitchen routine: hot food kept hot, cold food cold (assessment §9).

**Record:** the three photos; the plate you chose and the portion weight.

### PLT-4.M2.L1 — Saucing and garnishing

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-plt-4-m2-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `PLT-4.M1.L1` · moves TS-PLT→4*

*Objectives:* [S] pools, lines, dots, sauce at the table; [J] every garnish is edible and adds flavour
<!-- END generated:lesson-plt-4-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** where the sauce goes changes how a dish eats as much as how it looks. A garnish that adds flavour
earns its place; one that only adds colour is left on the plate.

**You need:** about 300 ml of one sauce (a vegetable purée, a reduction or a fluid gel from `MOD-4.M3.L2`); ten white
plates; spoons of two sizes; a squeeze bottle; a small jug; a damp cloth; for the guest course, a course of your
repertoire for four.

**Explanation:**

*Sauce placement:*
- **pool:** under or beside the main item, spooned and tilted to spread; for sauces the item sits in;
- **line or swoosh:** a spoonful dragged with the back of the spoon; for thick sauces and purées;
- **dots:** from a squeeze bottle, for intense sauces in small amounts (a reduction, a fluid gel);
- **at the table:** poured from a jug in front of the guest, for sauces that would spoil a crisp skin or set (*beurre
  blanc*, a consommé over a garnish).

*Clean edges.* Wipe the rim with a damp cloth or paper. Never put sauce under a crisp skin.

*The amount.* Enough sauce to go with every bite, no more: about 40–60 ml for a main course.

*Every garnish is edible and adds flavour.*
- A herb leaf that belongs to the dish.
- A crisp that adds texture (`MOD-4.M5`).
- A pickle that adds acid (`PRS-3`).

No inedible decoration, and no garnish that only adds colour.

**Practice:**
1. **Saucing drill (60 min).** Place one sauce ten ways on ten plates: pools, lines, swooshes, dots, and one poured at
   the table. Photograph them together.
2. **A course for guests (60 min).** Use the best saucing and a purposeful garnish on a course for four.

**Look for:**
- clean, deliberate lines and pools;
- every garnish eaten, and mentioned on the cards.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A soggy skin | Sauce under or over the crisp skin | Sauce beside it, or at the table |
| Uneven dots | Squeezed at different pressures | Same bottle, same pressure, same height |
| Smears on the rim | Plated without wiping | Wipe with a damp cloth before it leaves |

**Safety:** sauces kept hot at 57 °C or above until plated, or cold at 5 °C or below (assessment §9); a herb garnish
washed and dried.

**Record:** the drill photo with the best three marked; the course photo; what the guests said about the garnish.

### PLT-4.M3.L1 — Classical, family-style and modern

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-plt-4-m3-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `PLT-4.M2.L1` · moves TS-PLT→4, CR-COMP→4*

*Objectives:* [K] the three styles; [S] platters and shared dishes
<!-- END generated:lesson-plt-4-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the same food can be served in three styles, and each suits a different table. Knowing them, and how
to send out a platter that guests can serve from safely, lets you choose the style for the occasion.

**You need:** a household main course (a roast or a braise with seasonal vegetables); a platter and serving bowls,
warmed; a carving knife and board; a serving spoon for every dish; a small jug for the sauce.

**Explanation:**

*The three styles:*
- **classical:** the main item at the front, the garnish arranged around it, the sauce beside or under; the plate
  ordered and symmetrical (`CLS-4.M5`);
- **family-style:** platters and bowls on the table, guests serving themselves; generous, warm and relaxed, the food
  arranged to look abundant and easy to serve;
- **modern:** a composed plate built in the kitchen, often asymmetric, with height, negative space and several
  textures; one idea per plate (`PST-4.M5`, `MOD-4`).

*Platters and shared dishes:*
- warm the platter;
- carve or portion the main item before it goes out, so guests can serve themselves easily;
- put the sauce in a jug and the garnish in its own bowl, or scatter it over;
- give every dish its own serving spoon;
- keep allergen-free dishes apart, each with its own spoon, and tell the guest which they are (`NUT-3`).

*The idea behind the plate* (`CR-COMP`). One sentence says what the dish is about, and every style keeps it.

**Practice:**
1. **A family-style platter (60 min).** Serve a main course for the household on a platter with shared bowls, and
   photograph the table.

**Look for:**
- a platter that looks generous and is easy to serve from;
- each style recognisable in the photos.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Guests struggle with the meat | A whole joint sent out uncut | Carve it in the kitchen; arrange the slices |
| Food spills on the table | The platter piled too high | A larger platter, or two |
| An allergen reaches the wrong plate | One spoon shared between dishes | A spoon per dish; allergen-free dishes apart |

**Safety:** a spoon per dish, allergen-free dishes kept apart with their own spoons and named to the guest (`NUT-3`);
food on the table eaten within the meal, and leftovers cooled and kept by the leftover rows (assessment §9).

**Record:** the table photo; the idea of the dish in one sentence.

### PLT-4.M3.L2 — One dish, three styles

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-plt-4-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `PLT-4.M3.L1` · moves CR-COMP→4*

*Objectives:* [S] plate a dish in each style; [J] which suits which occasion
<!-- END generated:lesson-plt-4-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** choosing the style is a judgment about the guests, the occasion and the cook's time. Plating one
dish three ways shows that the idea stays while the presentation changes.

**You need:** a main course of your repertoire with three or more components; a platter and bowls; two plates; the
plating tools; a sketch for the modern plate; a phone.

**Explanation:**

*One dish in each style.* Plate the same components classically, family-style and modern. For example, a braised beef
with root vegetables and a sauce:

| Style | How |
|---|---|
| Classical | Slices at the front, vegetables turned and arranged behind, sauce under the meat |
| Family-style | Sliced on a platter in its sauce, vegetables in a bowl, sauce in a jug |
| Modern | One piece off-centre, a root vegetable purée swooshed, crisp vegetable shards for height |

*Which suits which occasion:*
- a family dinner or a crowd: family-style (warmer, quicker, less washing-up, guests choose their portions);
- a formal dinner or a celebration: plated (classical or modern), each guest served the same;
- a dish whose texture or temperature must be exact (a soufflé, a seared scallop): plated in the kitchen;
- the cook's time: plating for eight takes help or practice (`MNU-4.M3`).

*The idea stays the same.* Whatever the style, the dish's idea and flavours are the same; only the presentation
changes. Write the idea first, then check each version against it in the photos.

**Practice:**
1. **One dish, three styles (60 min).** Plate a dish in each style, photograph each, and write the occasion it suits.

**Look for:** each version suits a named occasion, and the same idea is clear in all three.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The food waits and cools | Every course plated for a casual meal | Family-style for an informal table |
| A formal dinner feels careless | Served from pans on the table | Plate it, or use proper platters |

**Safety:** plating three versions takes time: keep the food hot at 57 °C or above while you work (assessment §9), and
serve the household the version plated last.

**Record:** the three photos with their occasions; the dish's idea in one sentence.

### PLT-4.M4.L1 — Evidence photos

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-plt-4-m4-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `ORI-1.M1.L2` · moves TS-PLT→4, JD-REFL→4*

*Objectives:* [S] daylight, angle, background, scale reference; cross-sections; a consistent set-up for AI review
<!-- END generated:lesson-plt-4-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** AI review and your own record compare photos over months. A change of light, angle or background can
look like a change in the food, so a consistent set-up makes the evidence reliable (assessment §6).

**You need:** a window with daylight and no direct sun; a plain, matt grey or white board; a white card (A4 or larger);
a phone stand; a ruler or the grid board; a sharp knife and a damp cloth; three dishes to photograph.

**Explanation:**

*The photo corner:*
- **daylight** from one side (a window), no direct sun and no kitchen lamps mixed in; a white card opposite softens the
  shadows;
- **background:** a plain, matt surface, such as a grey or white board;
- **angles:** from above (90°) for composition, at 45° for height, level for layers and cross-sections;
- **scale:** a ruler or the grid board in the frame for cuts, crumbs and portions;
- **the camera:** a phone on a stand at a fixed distance, the lens wiped, no zoom, no filters.

Photograph the set-up itself once it works, with the stand's position marked, so you can rebuild it the same way.

*Cross-sections.* Cut with a sharp, clean knife, wiped between cuts, and photograph level and at once.

*The evidence checklist.*
1. Light: daylight from one side, nothing mixed in.
2. Angle: the one the evidence asks for.
3. Background: plain and matt.
4. Scale: in the frame where size matters.
5. Focus: sharp on the food.
6. File name: the date and the exercise ID, for example `2026-11-20_PLT-4.M4.X1_1.jpg`.

*Reviewing the record.* Look through your photos since S1 for a pattern: what makes some hard to judge? Then write a
plan to fix it.

**Practice:**
1. **Setting up the corner (60 min).** Build the corner. Take three test shots and check them against the checklist.
   Photograph the set-up so you can repeat it.
2. **Re-shoots (60 min).** Re-shoot three dishes in the corner, including a cross-section.

**Look for:**
- photos that pass every point of the checklist;
- the same light and angle on different days.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Yellow or blue food | Daylight mixed with kitchen lamps | Lamps off; one window |
| The food gets lost | A busy background | A plain, matt board |
| Torn, smeared cross-sections | A blunt knife | A sharp knife, wiped between cuts |

**Safety:** hot food photographed quickly and then served or cooled by the cooling rule (assessment §9); the knife
wiped and laid flat.

**Record:** the set-up photo; the test shots with their checklist; the three re-shoots.

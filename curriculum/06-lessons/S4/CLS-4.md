**Before you start.** S2 taught the mother sauces and S3 the regional French kitchen (`CUI-3.M2`). This course teaches
the classical system behind most restaurant cooking in Europe: Escoffier's mother sauces and their derivatives, the
classical cuts and vegetable garnishes, consommé, the named preparations, and how *nouvelle cuisine* and today's cooking
grew out of it [S11][S12]. By the end you can:
- **read and write the language:** decode a classical menu item into its components, and name your own dishes
  correctly;
- **cut to the classical standard:** *tourné*, *parisienne* and a fine *brunoise*, measured;
- **make the sauces:** a home-scaled demi-glace, and derivatives of brown sauce, *velouté*, the butter emulsions and
  tomato sauce, finished to a refined standard;
- **clarify:** a consommé clear enough to read print through;
- **judge the classics:** what to keep and what to lighten.

The repertoire is cooked with Dutch produce: beef, veal and chicken bones from a butcher, North Sea sole or plaice,
Dutch eggs and butter, and seasonal vegetables for the garnishes (carrots, turnips, celeriac, pearl onions, peas and
beans in summer). Citrus for *maltaise* is a pantry good, as in S2 and S3.

Plan about six weeks at 6.5 hours a week. The knife drill and the five vegetable courses run over at least eight weeks,
so start them in the first week and let them run on beside the next S4 course. Make the brown stock for the demi-glace
and the stock for the consommé together, and freeze both in portions: the sauces of `CLS-4.M3` and the consommé of
`CLS-4.M4` draw on them for weeks.

**Have ready:** the stockpot, a fine sieve or *chinois*, muslin, a turning knife (or a curved paring knife), a melon
baller, the grid board, a heavy sauté pan, small saucepans, a balloon whisk, ramekins and small moulds, the instant-read
thermometer, freezer containers and kitchen string; the calibrated seasoning form (`TST-1`), tasting forms and tasters'
cards; the classical glossary and the reference list of menu items.

### CLS-4.M1.L1 — Escoffier's system

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SAU-2.M1.L1` · moves CK-CLAS→3, CK-TERM→4*

*Objectives:* [K] the brigade, codified recipes, mother sauces and derivatives; why the system mattered [S11]
<!-- END generated:lesson-cls-4-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a classical name is a list of components. Once you see the logic behind the names, hundreds of
sauces and dishes become a handful of bases plus additions, and every classical recipe in the rest of S4 reads faster.

**You need:** nothing; the glossary and a notebook.

**Explanation:**

*The brigade* (*brigade de cuisine*). The classical kitchen is organised into stations under a chef:
- the *saucier* makes the sauces;
- the *poissonnier* cooks the fish;
- the *rôtisseur* roasts;
- the *entremetier* cooks the vegetables and eggs;
- the *garde-manger* runs the cold kitchen;
- the *pâtissier* makes the pastry.

That is why classical recipes are built from components made by different people and put together at service. A
*tournedos chasseur* is the *rôtisseur*'s beef, the *saucier*'s sauce and the *entremetier*'s garnish on one plate. At
home you are every station, so you plan the components in the order they can wait.

*Codified recipes.* Escoffier's *Le Guide culinaire* (1903) [S11] wrote the repertoire down, so that a dish with a name
was the same dish in every kitchen. A name is a promise of components: a guest ordering *sole meunière* expects a whole
floured sole, brown butter, lemon and parsley, and nothing else.

*Mother sauces and derivatives.* There are five bases:
- ***espagnole***, the brown sauce;
- ***velouté***, a white stock thickened with a blond roux;
- ***béchamel***, milk thickened with a white roux;
- ***sauce tomate***;
- **hollandaise**, standing for the emulsion family.

Each gives many sauces by adding a reduction, a garnish or a finish. A *Mornay* is *béchamel* plus cheese; a *bercy* is
fish *velouté* plus shallot and white wine. Learn the logic and you have learned hundreds of sauces at once.

*Why the system mattered.* It made large kitchens reliable, let cooks move from one kitchen to another and work at once,
and gave a shared vocabulary that menus still use.

*What it costs at home.* A classical sauce rests on a good stock made days ahead. The home version keeps the logic and
scales the quantities down: a litre of demi-glace frozen in cubes (`CLS-4.M3.L1`) serves a season of sauces for two.

**Practice:** None: theory only.

**Look for:** you can name the five mother sauces, the base of each, and two derivatives of each, without the glossary.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Names learned as decoration | Words memorised without their components | For each name, write the components beside it |
| A derivative treated as a new long recipe | The base not recognised | Write it as mother sauce + addition + finish |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** a one-page table: the five mother sauces, the base and thickener of each, and two derivatives of each with
their additions.

### CLS-4.M1.L2 — Classical terminology

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m1-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `CLS-4.M1.L1` · moves CK-TERM→4, CK-CLAS→3*

*Objectives:* [K] garnish names (*à la …*), preparations and menu French
<!-- END generated:lesson-cls-4-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** menu French is a code. Reading it lets you cook a dish from its name alone (`CLS-4.M5.X1`); writing
it lets you name your own dishes so a guest knows what will arrive.

**You need:** the glossary; the reference list of classical menu items; a notebook.

**Explanation:**

*Garnish names* (*à la …*, *… à la façon de …*). A name stands for a fixed garnish:

| Name | Garnish |
|---|---|
| *à la bordelaise* | Red wine and shallot, often bone marrow |
| *chasseur* | Mushrooms, shallot, white wine, tomato |
| *Du Barry* | Cauliflower |
| *Crécy* | Carrots |
| *Parmentier* | Potatoes |
| *florentine* | Spinach |
| *bonne femme* | Mushrooms, onions, potatoes or a white-wine sauce, by the dish |
| *meunière* | Floured, cooked in butter, finished with brown butter, lemon and parsley |

*Preparations:* *poché* (poached), *braisé* (braised), *rôti* (roasted), *sauté*, *poêlé* (cooked covered in butter),
*en papillote* (in a parcel), *au gratin* (browned under a crust) and *glacé* (glazed).

*Menu French.* The order is main item, then method, then garnish: *filet de sole poché, sauce bercy*. The article and
the agreement follow the noun: *suprême de volaille*, *filet de sole*, *pommes parisiennes*. A modern menu uses the
French where it names something exact (*beurre blanc*, *jus*) and plain words where they work better. Never mix the
two in one line: "Sole meunière with brown butter" says the butter twice.

*Decoding.* Break a menu item into its protein, its cut, its method, its sauce and its garnish, and from those into the
techniques you need. *Suprême de volaille, sauce suprême, carottes Vichy* becomes: a chicken breast with the wing bone
(`BUT-3.M1.L1`); pan-roasted; a chicken *velouté* with cream and butter; carrot rounds glazed. Four techniques, all
known from S2 and S3.

**Practice:**
1. **Decoding practice (45 min).** Take five classical menu items from the reference list. For each, write the protein,
   cut, method, sauce and garnish, then the techniques needed. Check every line against the glossary, and mark each
   mistake with what you guessed and what is right.

**Look for:** every component and technique named; your decoding agrees with the glossary line for line.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A wrong garnish | Guessed from the sound of the word | Look it up; add it to your table |
| A muddled menu line | French and English mixed | One language per line |
| A name that misleads | A classical name on a dish without its components | Use the name only when every component is there |

**Safety:** no special risks beyond the kitchen basics (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the five decodings with your mistakes marked; any new names added to your glossary table.

### CLS-4.M2.L1 — Precision cuts

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m2-l1 -->
*3 h · 3 practical sessions · elapsed 4 wk · builds on `KNF-2.M1.L1` · moves TS-KNF→5*

*Objectives:* [S] *tourné*, *parisienne*, *brunoise fine* to tolerance at speed
<!-- END generated:lesson-cls-4-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the *tourné*, the *parisienne* and the fine *brunoise* are lessons in control. Cutting them to a
measured standard, at speed, is the knife-work of `TS-KNF` level 5, and `MS-S4` tests it again.

**You need:** a turning knife (*bec d'oiseau*) or a curved paring knife; a chef's knife; a melon baller; the grid
board; 1 kg of carrots, 1 kg of waxy potatoes, a small celeriac, 3 onions and 2 shallots; a pan for soup; the phone to
film; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*The* tourné (NL *getourneerd*, FR *tourné*). A barrel about 5 cm long, with seven even faces and flat ends:
1. Cut the vegetable into even blocks about 5 cm long and 2 cm across, so every barrel starts the same.
2. Hold the block between thumb and fingers; set the blade at the top end.
3. Draw the knife towards your thumb in one curved stroke from end to end, turning the block a seventh of a turn
   between strokes.
4. Seven strokes, seven faces, meeting at clean edges.

It is a lesson in control more than a garnish to make every day.

*The* parisienne. A ball scooped with a melon baller from potatoes, carrots, celeriac or turnips: press the scoop in
until the rim is buried, turn it a full circle, and lift the ball out.

*Fine* brunoise. Cubes of 1–2 mm, cut across a fine *julienne*: slices 1–2 mm thick, stacked, cut into strips as wide,
then across. It is the knife-work standard of `TS-KNF` level 5. The *brunoise* is only as even as the *julienne*
behind it.

*Speed with tolerance.* The onion small dice of `KNF-2` in under 90 seconds without losing size. Speed comes from the
grip (a pinch grip on the blade, the claw on the guiding hand) and from a steady rhythm, not from hurrying.

*Using the trim.* The trim of *tourné* and *parisienne* goes to purées, soups and stocks, so nothing is wasted.

*The teaching note.* Writing down how your grip and guiding hand work, for someone else, makes your own technique
explicit: you notice what you do without thinking.

**Practice:**
1. ***Tourné* (60 min).** Cut carrots and potatoes into blocks, then turn them. Measure the best five on the grid
   and photograph them. The trim goes into a soup for the evening.
2. **Parisienne and fine brunoise (60 min).** Scoop celeriac *parisienne*. Cut a carrot and a shallot into fine
   *brunoise*; measure ten pieces of each on the grid.
3. **The timed set (45 min).** At least four weeks after session 1: small-dice an onion against the clock, cut a fine
   *brunoise* and one *tourné*. Film the set.

**Look for:**
- seven faces meeting at clean edges, and flat ends that let the piece stand on the board;
- *brunoise* cubes that look like grains, with no long pieces;
- a steady sound on the timed onion: an even rhythm, not bursts.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Faces with steps | Sawing strokes | One smooth stroke per face, end to end |
| Barrels of different sizes | Blocks cut unevenly | Square the blocks to one size first |
| Long pieces in the *brunoise* | A *julienne* of uneven thickness | Check the slices on the grid before cutting strips |

**Safety:** the turning knife cuts towards the thumb: keep the stroke short and controlled, the thumb behind the edge's
path, and the blade sharp (`KNF-1`). Never film with one hand while cutting with the other.

**Record:** the grid photos with your counts, the onion time, the video, and what you would change in the grip.

### CLS-4.M2.L2 — Classical vegetable garnishes

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m2-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `VEG-3.M3.L1` · moves TS-VEG→5*

*Objectives:* [S] glazed vegetables (*glacés à blanc / à brun*), *jardinière*, *printanière*; [J] timing several garnishes together
<!-- END generated:lesson-cls-4-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a classical garnish is several vegetables cooked separately and ready at the same moment. The
glazing and the timing plan learned here serve every plated course to come, and `TS-VEG` level 5 is built on them.

**You need:** a wide sauté pan with a lid for each vegetable (or one used in turn); 500 g of carrots, 300 g of pearl
onions, 300 g of turnips, 200 g of peas (fresh in early summer, frozen otherwise) and 200 g of green beans in summer
(celeriac in autumn and winter); 75 g of butter; sugar and salt; a written timing plan.

**Explanation:**

*Glazing* (NL *glaceren*, FR *glacer*), from `VEG-3.M3.L1`:
- ***à blanc*:** the vegetables cut evenly, barely covered with water, with a little butter, sugar and salt (about 15 g
  of butter and a pinch of sugar for 250 g of carrots). Cook uncovered until tender just as the liquid reduces to a
  shiny syrup that coats them, without colour. Carrots, turnips, pearl onions.
- ***à brun*:** the same, taken on until the syrup caramelises lightly and the vegetables are rolled in it. Pearl
  onions, chestnut-coloured turnips.

*The classical garnishes:*
- ***jardinière*:** batons of carrot and turnip, peas, green beans;
- ***printanière*:** spring vegetables, turned or cut small;
- ***bouquetière*:** vegetables arranged in small bunches around the meat;
- ***Vichy*:** carrot rounds, glazed.

*Timing several garnishes together.* Each vegetable cooks at its own speed, so each is cooked on its own, held just
under done, and finished together in butter at service. Write the order of work backwards from service: the longest
first (the turnips), the quickest last (the peas), and the finish in one pan at the end.

*Seasonal choices.* Spring carrots and peas; summer beans; autumn turnips and celeriac; winter pearl onions and root
vegetables.

**Practice:**
1. **Glazing (60 min).** Glaze carrots *à blanc* and pearl onions *à brun*. Judge each glaze on a spoon: it should
   cling.
2. **A garnish for service (75 min).** Write a timing plan for a *jardinière* of three vegetables. Cook them
   separately, then finish them together in butter for a meal at a set time. Note the planned and actual times.

**Look for:**
- a glaze that clings, shiny, with no liquid left in the pan;
- each vegetable tender with a slight bite, and the colours bright;
- everything ready at the same moment.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Soft vegetables, no glaze yet | Too much water | Barely cover; pour some off and reduce |
| Some raw, some mushy | Mixed vegetables in one pan | One vegetable per pan; finish together |
| A bitter *à brun* | The sugar taken too far | Stop at light amber; deglaze with a spoon of water |

**Safety:** a caramelising glaze is hot sugar: tilt the pan away from you and keep your fingers out of it (`HEA-1`).

**Record:** the timing plan against the actual times; photos of each glaze; what you would cook earlier or later.

### CLS-4.M3.L1 — *Espagnole* and a domestic demi-glace

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m3-l1 -->
*3.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 d · builds on `STK-2.M2.L3` · moves TS-SAU→4, TS-STK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] a brown sauce and a demi-glace scaled for home; [K] the modern shortcut (reduced brown stock)
<!-- END generated:lesson-cls-4-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the demi-glace is the base of every brown derivative in this course. Made once and frozen in
portions, it serves for weeks.

**You need:** for the brown stock, about 3 kg of bones (see below), 300 g of *mirepoix* (onion, carrot, celery), 2
tablespoons of tomato purée, a bouquet garni, about 5 litres of cold water; for the *espagnole*, 50 g each of butter and
flour, a small *mirepoix*, 1 tomato; a roasting tray, the stockpot, a fine sieve, shallow containers or an ice bath, the
thermometer, ice-cube trays, labels.

**Explanation:**

*The brown stock first* (`STK-2.M2.L3`). Roast the bones dark, add the *mirepoix* and the tomato purée to colour, then
simmer them long, skimming. Adapt it to the bones you can get:
- **veal knuckle** gives the most gelatin;
- **beef shin and oxtail** work well;
- **a chicken carcass** makes a lighter brown stock.



*The* espagnole. Brown stock thickened with a brown roux, with a browned *mirepoix* and tomato, simmered and skimmed
for about two hours, then strained. Cook the roux slowly to the colour of hazelnuts.

*The classical demi-glace.* *Espagnole* and brown stock in equal parts, reduced by half, skimmed, and strained through
a fine sieve.

*Scaled for home.* One litre of each makes about one litre of demi-glace. Frozen in ice-cube portions, it lasts for
many sauces: a derivative for two needs 150 ml (`CLS-4.M3.L2`).

*The modern shortcut* (*jus*, or *fond lié*). A brown stock reduced without flour until it coats a spoon, or thickened
lightly with a little starch slaked in cold water. It is clearer and lighter, and what most kitchens use today. You make
both to taste the difference.

*Cooling.* Follow the cooling rule of assessment §9: from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C or below within 6
hours in total. Use shallow containers or an ice bath, stirred, and log the readings. Then freeze it, or keep it at most
2 days in the fridge.

**Practice:**
1. **A home demi-glace (over two days).**
   - **Day 1:** roast the bones and *mirepoix*; make the brown stock (about 6 hours at a bare simmer); strain; cool by
     the rule, logging the readings; refrigerate overnight.
   - **Day 2:** lift the fat. Make the *espagnole* from 1 litre of stock; combine it with 1 litre more and reduce by
     half; strain. Reduce a further 500 ml of stock alone to a *jus*. Cool both by the rule and log them. Taste them
     side by side; freeze in cubes.

**Look for:**
- a deep brown, clear stock;
- a demi-glace that coats the back of a spoon and holds a clean line drawn with a finger;
- a cooling log within the limits.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A weak colour | Pale, under-roasted bones | Roast them dark brown, not black |
| Cloudy, greasy stock | Boiled hard | A bare simmer; skim |
| A bitter sauce | The roux burnt | Cook it slowly; start again if it smells burnt |
| A cooling log over the limit | A big pot left on the stove overnight | Shallow containers or an ice bath, stirred |

**Safety:** the cooling rule of assessment §9 (57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, 5 °C or below within 6); stock and sauce
frozen or kept at most 2 days in the fridge. A full stockpot is heavy: ladle it out first (`SAF-1`).

**Record:** the bones and why; the cooling log for each batch; tasting notes on the demi-glace and the *jus*; the
number of cubes frozen and the date.

### CLS-4.M3.L2 — Brown sauce derivatives

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m3-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `CLS-4.M3.L1` · moves TS-SAU→5, CK-CLAS→4, CK-TERM→4*

**Why it matters:** four classic brown sauces from one frozen base, each in under twenty minutes. Once the logic is
clear (reduction, base, finish), any brown derivative on a menu can be made from its name.

**You need:** 600 ml of demi-glace (four batches of 150 ml); 4 shallots and 1 onion; 200 ml of red wine and 200 ml of
white wine; 50 ml of red-wine vinegar; 200 g of mushrooms; a tomato; tarragon, parsley and thyme; black peppercorns; a

**Explanation:**

*The derivative logic.* A reduction (wine, vinegar, shallot, herbs), plus the mother sauce, plus a finish (butter,
herbs, a garnish). The name tells you which:

| Sauce | Reduction | Base | Finish |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Bordelaise* | Red wine and shallot | Demi-glace, thyme | Butter; poached bone-marrow slices (optional) |
| *Chasseur* | Mushrooms and shallot sautéed, white wine | Demi-glace, tomato | Tarragon and parsley |

the final reduction by *nappe*: the sauce coats a spoon and a finger drawn across leaves a clean line.

*Portion.* 40–60 ml of a reduced sauce per plate. A derivative for two starts from 150 ml of demi-glace: reduce 100 ml

2. ***Chasseur* and *poivrade* (60 min).** The *chasseur* for chicken; the *poivrade* for a game cut or a beef cut.

- each sauce tastes of its addition, not only of the demi-glace;
- a glossy *nappe*, without a skin;

| A thin, raw-tasting sauce | The wine not reduced | Reduce to a syrup before the base goes in |
| A bitter *Robert* | Mustard boiled | Off the heat, at the end |
| A greasy sauce | Butter added over the heat | Off the heat; cold butter; swirl |

**Safety:** the meats to their §9 rows: chicken 75 °C; pork 63 °C for 15 seconds; a rare or medium-rare steak by the

### CLS-4.M3.L3 — *Velouté* derivatives

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m3-l3 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SAU-2.M3.L3` · moves TS-SAU→5*

*Objectives:* [S] *suprême*, *allemande*, *bercy*; [J] finish with cream, butter or liaison
<!-- END generated:lesson-cls-4-m3-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** the white sauces of the classical kitchen differ only in their finish. Choosing cream, butter or a
liaison for the dish is the judgment this lesson trains, and it carries into every plated white sauce to come.

**You need:** 1 litre of white chicken stock and 500 ml of fish *fumet* (`BUT-3.M2.L3`); 60 g of butter and 60 g of
flour for two blond roux; 150 ml of cream; 1 pasteurised egg yolk (from a carton, about 20 g); 2 shallots and 100 ml of
white wine; parsley; a lemon; white pepper; 2 chicken breasts; 2 fillets of plaice or sole; a shallow pan for poaching;
the thermometer.

**Explanation:**

*The* velouté (`SAU-2.M3.L3`). A white chicken, veal or fish stock thickened with a blond roux (about 30 g of butter and
30 g of flour for 500 ml), simmered and skimmed until velvety and the taste of flour is gone.

*The derivatives:*
- ***suprême*:** chicken *velouté* reduced with cream and finished with butter;
- ***allemande*:** veal or chicken *velouté* thickened with a liaison of egg yolk and cream, with a little lemon;
- ***bercy*:** fish *velouté* with shallot and white wine reduced, finished with butter and parsley.

*The finish:*
- **cream** for body and richness, when the dish is lean (chicken, poached fish);
- **butter** for gloss and flavour, at the last moment;
- **a liaison** (yolk and cream) for a silky sauce that must not boil afterwards. Temper it with a ladle of the hot
  sauce, stir it back in, and heat it until it thickens, below the boil.

*Seasoning.* Salt, white pepper (black specks look like dirt in a white sauce), and a few drops of lemon to lift the
cream.

**Practice:**
1. ***Suprême* and *allemande* (60 min).** Make one chicken *velouté*; divide it. Finish one half as a *suprême* and
   the other as an *allemande*. Serve both with a poached chicken breast, probed to 75 °C.
2. ***Bercy* (60 min).** Make a fish *velouté* from the *fumet*; reduce the shallot and wine, combine, and finish.
   Serve with poached plaice or sole, probed to 63 °C.

**Look for:**
- a sauce that flows and coats, with no taste of flour;
- the liaison smooth, without grains;
- a fish sauce that tastes of the sea and the wine, not of cream alone.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A grainy *allemande* | The liaison boiled | Below the boil; temper first |
| A sauce that tastes only of cream | Too much cream | Reduce the stock more; less cream |
| A floury taste | Roux under-cooked | Simmer longer, skimming |

**Safety:** the liaison is brought back to at least 71 °C on the probe without boiling (the egg-thickened row of
assessment §9), as in `STK-2.M4.L2` and `SLW-3.M2.L2`. Chicken to 75 °C; fish to 63 °C for
15 seconds.

**Record:** the three sauces with photos; the liaison's probe reading; which finish you would choose for which dish,
and why.

### CLS-4.M3.L4 — Emulsion derivatives and tomato sauce

*Objectives:* [S] *béarnaise*, *choron*, *maltaise*; a classical tomato sauce; [J] precise final seasoning

**Why it matters:** *béarnaise* is the classical partner of steak, and *maltaise* of asparagus. The lesson also trains
the last step of every sauce: the final seasoning, judged with the dish.

**You need:** 4 pasteurised egg yolks (from a carton, about 80 g); 250 g of butter, clarified; 2 shallots, 60 ml of
white-wine vinegar, tarragon and chervil, black peppercorns; a tablespoon of tomato purée; an orange (a blood orange in

**Explanation:**

*The warm emulsions* (`SAU-2.M5.L1`). Yolks whisked over gentle heat to a light foam (*sabayon*), then warm clarified
butter whisked in slowly.

*The derivatives:*
- ***béarnaise*:** a reduction of shallot, tarragon, chervil, white-wine vinegar and crushed pepper (60 ml of vinegar
  reduced with the shallot and herb stalks to about a tablespoon), strained into the yolks; finished with chopped
  tarragon and chervil;
- ***choron*:** *béarnaise* with a little reduced tomato (a tablespoon of tomato purée cooked dry);
- ***maltaise*:** hollandaise with the juice (reduced by half) and the grated zest of an orange.

*Safety in the emulsion.* The yolks are only lightly cooked in the *sabayon*, so they are pasteurised egg yolk for every
guest (the raw-egg row of assessment §9). A warm emulsion is served at once, or kept briefly at 57 °C or above, so make
it close to service.

*The classical tomato sauce.* Sweat a *mirepoix* with a little bacon, add the tomatoes and a little stock, simmer, and
pass it through a sieve. The old method thickens with a roux; make the modern one, which reduces.

*Precise final seasoning.* Taste at serving temperature, not from a cold spoon. Adjust salt and acid in small steps,
comparing with the calibrated reference (`TST-1`). Then taste it with the dish: a *béarnaise* perfect on a spoon can be
too sharp beside a lean steak.

**Practice:**
1. ***Béarnaise* and *choron* (60 min).** Make the *béarnaise* for a steak; turn a third of it into *choron*. Adjust
   the seasoning in three steps, tasting with the steak, and record each step.
2. ***Maltaise* and a tomato sauce (60 min).** Make a *maltaise* for asparagus or a poached fish. Make the tomato
   sauce for a classical egg or meat dish. Taste both against the reference.

**Look for:**
- an emulsion that holds a ribbon, glossy and warm, not hot;
- the tarragon clear in the *béarnaise*;
- seasoning that stays balanced with the dish, not only on a spoon.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Scrambled yolks | Overheated | Lower heat; take the bowl off and on |
| A split sauce | Butter added too fast | Start again with a fresh pasteurised yolk and whisk the split sauce in drop by drop |
| Seasoning wrong on the plate | Judged on a cold spoon | Taste warm, with the dish |
| A thin, greasy sauce at service | Held warm for an hour | Make it close to service |

**Safety:** pasteurised egg yolk in every warm emulsion (the raw-egg row of assessment §9); held at 57 °C or above and
only briefly. The steak by the steak row, not served to risk groups; fish to 63 °C for 15 seconds.

**Record:** the three seasoning steps of the *béarnaise*; the *maltaise* and tomato sauce against the reference; photos.

### CLS-4.M4.L1 — Clarifying with a raft

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m4-l1 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 h · builds on `STK-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-STK→4, SC-PROT→4*

*Objectives:* [S] the *clarification* raft (lean mince, egg white, *mirepoix*), gentle simmer, straining; [K] protein trapping
<!-- END generated:lesson-cls-4-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a consommé is the test of a stock and of patience. The raft turns a good cloudy stock into a soup
clear enough to read print through, and the same protein science explains why stocks cloud and how to keep them clear.

**You need:** 2 litres of frozen brown stock (from `CLS-4.M3.L1`), thawed in the fridge and fully degreased; 250 g of
lean minced beef; 3 egg whites (about 100 g); a finely chopped *mirepoix* (1 small onion, 1 carrot, 1 celery stalk); 1
tomato; a few parsley stalks and a sprig of thyme; a tall, heavy pan; a ladle; a sieve lined with damp muslin; kitchen
paper; the thermometer; a clear glass and a printed page.

**Explanation:**

*The clarification mix.* Lean minced beef (or chicken for a chicken consommé), egg whites, a finely chopped *mirepoix*,
tomato and herbs, mixed cold into cold, fat-free stock.

*Protein trapping.* As the stock heats slowly, the proteins of the egg white and the meat unfold and set into a mesh.
The mesh rises and forms a raft, and the fine particles that cloud the stock are caught in it. The mince also adds
flavour, and the *mirepoix* aroma, so a consommé tastes stronger than the stock it came from.

*Method:*
1. The stock fully degreased and cold.
2. Stir the mix in. Bring it up slowly while stirring gently on the bottom, so nothing catches, until the raft begins
   to form (around 50–60 °C). Then leave it alone.
3. Make a hole in the raft with a ladle so the liquid can bubble through gently. Simmer at a bare tremble for about an
   hour.
4. Ladle the consommé out through the hole, and strain it through the muslin-lined sieve. Drag kitchen paper across
   the surface to lift the last fat.

*Why it fails.* Boiling breaks the raft; stirring after it has formed clouds the stock again; fat left in the stock
dulls the shine.

**Practice:**
1. **A beef consommé (3 h with simmering).** Clarify the frozen brown stock by the method. Photograph the consommé in a
   clear glass held over a printed page.

**Look for:**
- a raft that holds together and rises;
- liquid bubbling through the hole that is clear and amber;
- print legible through a glass of the consommé.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No raft, a cloudy liquid | Warm or greasy stock at the start | Start cold, fully degreased |
| A broken raft | A rolling boil | A bare tremble; lower the heat |
| Cloudy again | Stirred after the raft formed | Leave it alone once it sets |

**Safety:** the mince is raw meat: its own board, and hands washed after (`SAF-1`). Serve the consommé hot, or cool it
by the rule of assessment §9 (57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, 5 °C or below within 6) and keep it as leftovers, at most 2
days in the fridge. Discard the raft.

**Record:** the stock used; the temperature when the raft formed; the simmering time; the clarity photo.

### CLS-4.M4.L2 — Gelatin clarity and aspic introduced

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m4-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `CLS-4.M4.L1` · moves SC-PROT→4*

*Objectives:* [K] gelatin strength and clarity; [S] a small aspic or jelly
<!-- END generated:lesson-cls-4-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** gelatin decides whether a stock sets, a sauce has body, and a jelly holds its shape. Predicting it
from the bones, and dosing sheets by weight, lets you make a clear jelly that melts in the mouth.

**You need:** 500 ml of consommé (from `CLS-4.M4.L1`); gelatin sheets (weigh them: brands differ); a bowl of cold water;
seasonal vegetables cut small and blanched (a carrot *brunoise*, peas or beans in summer, celeriac in winter); chervil
leaves; four small moulds or ramekins; a cold plate; the fridge thermometer.

**Explanation:**

*Gelatin strength and clarity.* Gelatin comes from bones, where long cooking dissolves their collagen, or from sheets.
It sets a liquid as it cools: more gelatin makes a firmer set, and a clear liquid gives a clear jelly. A consommé that
sets softly in the fridge already has natural gelatin, so it needs less added.

*Sheets.* Soak them in cold water for 5 minutes, squeeze them, and dissolve them in warm, not boiling, liquid. A soft
set needs about 1.5–2 sheets per 250 ml; a firm one for unmoulding needs more. The exact strength depends on the brand
(its bloom), so note the weight of your sheets and test the set.

*Aspic* (NL *aspic*, FR *aspic* or *gelée*). Clarified stock set with gelatin, used to coat or hold cold preparations.
Today it is mostly a small jelly or a glaze.

*Testing the set.* Put a spoonful on a cold plate in the fridge for 10 minutes. If it does not hold, add a little more
dissolved gelatin; if it is rubbery, add a little more consommé.

**Practice:**
1. **A small aspic (60 min active, set overnight).** Test the set of the consommé on its own. Dissolve the sheets in a
   little warm consommé and stir it into the rest; test the set again. Arrange the vegetables and chervil in the
   moulds, pour over the cool liquid, and set in the fridge overnight. Unmould by dipping each mould briefly in warm
   water; photograph.

**Look for:**
- a set that holds its shape but melts in the mouth;
- the vegetables visible through the jelly.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A weak set | Gelatin boiled | Dissolve in warm liquid, off the heat |
| A rubbery jelly | Too much gelatin | Fewer sheets; test the set first |
| A cloudy jelly | A cloudy stock | Clarify first (`CLS-4.M4.L1`) |

**Safety:** an aspic is a cooked, chilled food: set in the fridge, kept at 5 °C or below (the fridge set to 4 °C), and
eaten within 2 days (assessment §9).

**Record:** the weight of gelatin per 250 ml; the set tests; a photo of the unmoulded aspic; whether the natural
gelatin of the consommé matched what you expected from its bones.

### CLS-4.M5.L1 — Classical fish and egg dishes

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m5-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `FSH-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-PRO→4, CK-CLAS→4*

*Objectives:* [S] *sole meunière*, *poisson poché* with a sauce, *œufs en cocotte*
<!-- END generated:lesson-cls-4-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** *sole meunière*, a poached fish with its sauce and *œufs en cocotte* are three classics a guest
recognises at once. Each is simple, and each is exact: the standard is every component present and the cooking right.

**You need:** a whole sole, plaice or dab for every two people (MSC-labelled or a seasonal North Sea fish), skinned by
the fishmonger or by you (`FSH-2`); flour; 50 g of clarified butter and 60 g of fresh butter; a lemon and parsley; for
the poached fish, fillets of plaice, 300 ml of *fumet* and 100 ml of white wine; 4 eggs, 100 ml of cream, 200 g of
spinach or mushrooms; four buttered ramekins in a roasting tin; a kettle; the thermometer.

**Explanation:**

*Sole meunière* (the miller's wife's sole, for the flour). The fish is skinned, floured at the last moment, and cooked
in clarified butter on both sides until golden. Then fresh butter is cooked in a clean pan until nut-brown (*beurre
noisette*), lemon juice and parsley added, and the butter poured over at the table. The fish is cooked to 63 °C at the
backbone (assessment §9), checked by the flesh lifting cleanly from the bone.

*Poisson poché with a sauce.* Fillets folded or rolled, poached in a shallow *cuisson* of *fumet* and wine below the
simmer, then lifted out and kept warm. The *cuisson* is reduced and turned into the sauce (a *bercy*, `CLS-4.M3.L3`, or
a *vin blanc*: the reduced *cuisson* with cream and butter).

*Œufs en cocotte.* Eggs baked in a buttered ramekin in a water bath, with a spoon of cream and a garnish (spinach,
mushrooms, ham). Take them out when the white is just set and the yolk runny, which takes about 10–15 minutes in a
moderate oven. For risk groups the yolk is cooked firm (the risk-group row of assessment §9).

*The classical standard.* The dish recognisable from its name, every component present, and the cooking exact.

**Practice:**
1. ***Sole meunière* (60 min).** One fish per two people. Flour, cook, probe at the backbone, and finish with brown
   butter, lemon and parsley. Judge the butter by colour and smell.
2. **Poached fish and *œufs en cocotte* (60 min).** One poached fish with its sauce; four *cocottes*, timed from the
   moment they go in.

**Look for:**
- a golden, even crust on the fish; the brown butter nutty, not burnt;
- poached fish just opaque and moist;
- *cocotte* whites set, yolks shining.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A gluey coating | Floured too early | Flour just before the pan |
| Broken fish | The poaching liquid boiling | Below the simmer |
| Rubbery *cocotte* edges | No water bath | Bake in a water bath |

**Safety:** fish to 63 °C for 15 seconds (assessment §9). A runny yolk is a soft egg: not served to risk groups; for
them, cook the *cocotte* until the yolk is firm. Brown butter is very hot: pour it away from you.

**Record:** the probe readings; photos of each dish; the *cocotte* time against the set.

*Objectives:* [S] *suprême de volaille*, *tournedos* with a classical sauce and garnish

**Why it matters:** the *suprême de volaille* and the *tournedos* are the classical main courses, each a protein, a

**You need:** 2 chicken supremes (`BUT-3.M1.L1`); 2 rounds of beef fillet, about 4 cm thick (*tournedos*); kitchen

**Explanation:**

*Suprême de volaille.* The supreme is seasoned, browned skin-side down in butter, and finished in the oven to 75 °C at

*Tournedos.* A thick round of beef fillet, tied with string so it keeps its shape, seared and cooked to the chosen

- the chicken probed at the target, the skin crisp;

- the garnish and sauce matching the name on the card.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A spread, unevenly cooked *tournedos* | Not tied | Tie it round the middle before searing |
| Pale, soft chicken skin | A cool pan, or moved too soon | Hot butter; leave it until it releases |
| A garnish that doesn't fit the name | The name not decoded | Decode the card first |

**Safety:** chicken to 75 °C, or the low-temperature poultry table; the *tournedos* by the steak row (seared top and

### CLS-4.M6.L1 — Lightening the classics

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-cls-4-m6-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `CLS-4.M5.L2` · moves CK-CLAS→4, FL-ARCH→4*

*Objectives:* [K] *nouvelle cuisine* and after; [J] what to keep and what to change in a classic dish
<!-- END generated:lesson-cls-4-m6-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** most of what you eat in restaurants today is classical cooking made lighter. Knowing what changed,
and what was kept, lets you modernise a classic without losing it, which `PLT-4` and `MNU-4` build on.

**You need:** the ingredients of one classical dish of the course, twice: for example 2 chicken supremes, the *suprême*
sauce, and for the light version 300 ml of brown chicken stock reduced to a *jus*, 400 g of a seasonal vegetable for a
purée (celeriac in autumn and winter, peas in early summer), a little butter and lemon; tasting forms.

**Explanation:**

*Nouvelle cuisine and after* [S12]. From the 1960s and 1970s, cooks such as Bocuse, the Troisgros brothers and Guérard
turned away from flour-thickened sauces and long cooking. They moved towards reductions, shorter cooking, fresh
produce, a menu written daily, and the plate composed in the kitchen. Later came the regional, seasonal and technical
kitchens of today, which still use the classical logic.

*What changed:*
- **sauces:** the roux-thickened *espagnole* gave way to reduced stocks, *jus*, butter sauces and vegetable sauces;
- **cooking:** shorter for fish and vegetables, precise for meat;
- **plates:** one plate composed by the cook, instead of platters served at the table;
- **produce:** the season and the place named on the menu.

*What to keep and what to change.* Keep the logic: the stock, the reduction, the balance of sauce and garnish, and the
dish's identity. Change the weight (less flour and cream, more acidity and vegetables), the portion and the plate. A
modern version is still recognisably the classic.

*A Dutch modern classic.* A sole with brown butter, sea vegetables (*zeekraal*, in season in summer) and a few seasonal
potatoes is a *meunière* with the season named.

**Practice:**
1. **A lightened classic (90 min).** Cook one classical dish of the course twice: classically, and in a lighter
   version. For example, a *suprême de volaille* with its cream sauce, and the same supreme with a reduced *jus* and a
   vegetable purée. Taste them side by side with a taster, and write what was kept and what changed.

**Look for:** the lighter version is still recognisable as the classic, and the taster can say what was kept.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A different dish, not a lighter one | Too much changed | Keep the protein, the method and the sauce's role |
| A thin, mean plate | Flavour removed (less sauce) rather than weight | Keep the sauce; lighten its base |

**Safety:** chicken to 75 °C, or the low-temperature poultry table (assessment §9); the stock cooled by the rule if made
ahead.

**Record:** both versions photographed; the taster's notes; a list of what was kept and what changed.

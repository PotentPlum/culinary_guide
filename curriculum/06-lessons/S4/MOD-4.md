### MOD-4.M1.L1 — Modernist cooking in context

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mod-4-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SLW-3.M5.L3` · moves JD-CHSE→4*

*Objectives:* [K] history and principles; [J] technique in service of flavour and texture, not novelty
<!-- END generated:lesson-mod-4-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** modern techniques are easy to copy and easy to misuse. Knowing where they came from, and asking
what each one adds, keeps you from putting a foam on a plate because you can.

**You need:** a notebook; access to a book, menu or video showing modern dishes; [S19] if you can borrow it.

**Explanation:**

*History.* Food science entered restaurant kitchens from the 1980s, when Hervé This and Nicholas Kurti spoke of
"molecular gastronomy". In the 1990s and 2000s chefs such as Ferran Adrià (elBulli) and Heston Blumenthal used foams,
gels, spherification and low-temperature cooking on the plate. *Modernist Cuisine* [S19] gathered the techniques for
cooks in 2011. Many are now ordinary: sous vide, a xanthan-thickened dressing, a fluid gel.

*Principles.*
- Control of temperature and texture through physics and chemistry.
- Ingredients used as tools: hydrocolloids that thicken and set, emulsifiers that hold oil and water together.
- Measuring instead of guessing.
- Testing, and writing the results down.

*Criticism.* The same foam on every plate; technique for show; food that surprises but doesn't satisfy. A backlash
followed, towards produce and fire.

*Technique in service of flavour and texture.* A modern technique earns its place when it:
- gives a texture you couldn't get otherwise (a fluid gel that holds on a plate, a light air of a strong flavour);
- improves control or consistency (an egg at exactly 64 °C every time);
- concentrates or clarifies a flavour;
- and the dish is better for it, as the tasters can say.

Three questions test it: what does it add? Would the classic way do as well? Would a guest miss it?

A worked example. A beetroot dish with a beetroot fluid gel:

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What does it add? | A sharp, glossy sauce that stays where it is put, so each bite gets some |
| The classic way? | A reduced beetroot *jus*: it runs across the plate and into the other elements |
| Would a guest miss it? | Test it: serve both, ask which they prefer and why |

**Practice:** None: theory only.

**Look for:** for any modern element, you can name what it adds and what the classic alternative would be.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A technique praised because it is new | Judging by novelty | Ask the three questions |
| An element on the plate without a reason | Added because it is possible | Write its reason, or leave it out |

**Safety:** none specific; the safety rules of the techniques are in the lessons that teach them.

**Record:** for one modern dish you know, the three questions and your answers.

### MOD-4.M2.L1 — Time, temperature and texture

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mod-4-m2-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 4 h · builds on `SLW-3.M5.L2` · moves TS-MOD→2, KM-EQPT→4*

*Objectives:* [S] eggs and vegetables at precise temperatures; [S] calibrate the circulator; [K] texture tables
<!-- END generated:lesson-mod-4-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a precise bath gives the same texture every time, but only if the bath is right and you know which
temperature gives which texture. A tested table and a calibrated circulator turn a guess into a choice.

**You need:** the circulator and a deep container; the calibrated probe; a glass of ice water; six or more eggs; 500 g
of carrots or celeriac, peeled and cut evenly; zip-top bags; a slotted spoon; small bowls for opening the eggs; a pan
for boiling; labels; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Texture tables.* For eggs and vegetables, the texture depends on the temperature reached and the time held:
- **eggs in the shell:** the white and yolk proteins set at different temperatures, so a 45–60 minute bath gives a
  distinct texture at each degree between about 60 and 70 °C: a barely set, custardy white and a runny yolk at the low
  end; a fudgy yolk around 64–65 °C; firm at the high end;
- **vegetables:** the pectin in the cell walls softens only above about 80–85 °C, so root vegetables cooked at 83–85 °C
  become evenly, densely tender, and the flavour stays in the bag.

Tables in [S19] and similar sources give the starting points; your own table follows from your tests.

*Safety.* Eggs held below 71 °C are lightly cooked when served: not for risk groups (assessment §9). Pasteurised eggs
exist for them.

*Calibrating the circulator.*
1. Check the probe in ice water: it should read 0 °C (`ORI-1`).
2. Set the bath to a temperature, for example 64 °C, and let it settle.
3. Read the water with the probe next to the circulator's sensor.
4. Note the offset (the probe minus the display) and correct the setting by it.
5. Check the water level and that the water circulates; descale as the manual says.

*Workarounds.* Without a circulator, use a pan on the hob with a probe and frequent adjustment (`SLW-3.M5.L1`). Without
a vacuum sealer, use a zip-top bag by water displacement: lower the open bag into the water so the pressure pushes the
air out, then seal it just above the surface.

**Practice:**
1. **Calibration and eggs (90 min).** Calibrate the circulator and log the offset. Hold eggs at five temperatures, one
   bath after another, each for the same time. Open them in a row and photograph the textures.
2. **Vegetables (75 min).** Cook carrots or celeriac in a bag at 84 °C, and the same cut boiled. Taste and compare.

**Look for:**
- a calibration log with the offset;
- a row of five eggs with a visible, graded texture;
- vegetables tender and bright, dense rather than soggy.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Textures that don't match the table | Display trusted without a check | Calibrate; correct by the offset |
| Uneven eggs in one batch | Bath too crowded, so it dropped | Fewer eggs; wait until the bath recovers |
| Vegetables still crunchy after hours | Bath at 70 °C | Above 80 °C; 83–85 °C for roots |

**Safety:** soft eggs not for risk groups; for them, pasteurised eggs or an egg cooked until fully set (assessment §9).
The circulator's water changed after use.

**Record:** the calibration log; the bath temperature and time for each egg; the photos; your carrot comparison.

### MOD-4.M3.L1 — Gelatin and agar

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mod-4-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `PST-3.M4.L3` · moves SC-EMUL→4*

*Objectives:* [K] strength, setting and melting temperature, acid tolerance; [S] set gels by percentage
<!-- END generated:lesson-mod-4-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** gelatin and agar both set a liquid, but they behave in opposite ways. Knowing which melts in the
mouth and which can be served warm, and dosing each by percentage, lets you choose the set before you start.

**You need:** 1 litre of apple or beetroot juice (Dutch, or pressed yourself); gelatin sheets of known bloom strength
(`CLS-4.M4.L2`); agar powder; the 0.01 g scale; two small pans; a whisk; three small trays or tins lined with clingfilm;
a ruler; a knife.

**Explanation:**

*Two gelling agents compared:*

| | Gelatin | Agar |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Animal collagen (pork or beef) | Red seaweed (vegetarian) |
| Dissolves | In warm liquid (about 50 °C), never boiled | Only when boiled (about 1–2 minutes) |
| Sets | Slowly, in the fridge (below about 15 °C) | Quickly, at about 35–40 °C |
| Melts | At about 30–35 °C, in the mouth | Only above about 80–85 °C, so it can be served warm |
| Texture | Elastic, melting | Firm, brittle, clean |
| Acid | Weakened by strong acid | Tolerates acid better |

Raw kiwi and pineapple also weaken gelatin, but they are not in this curriculum's produce.

*Strength by percentage.* The dose is a percentage of the liquid's weight:
- **agar:** about 0.5–1 % for a sliceable gel, less for a soft one;
- **gelatin:** by sheets of known bloom strength, or about 1.5–2 % for powder in a soft set.

For 400 g of juice, 1 % agar is 4.00 g; 0.5 % is 2.00 g.

*Set gels by percentage.*
1. Weigh the dose on the 0.01 g scale.
2. Disperse it in the cold liquid (agar whisked in; gelatin sheets soaked in cold water and squeezed).
3. Heat as the agent needs: agar boiled for 1–2 minutes; gelatin dissolved in warm liquid, never boiled.
4. Pour into a tray or mould; let it set.
5. Cut into cubes or shapes.

**Practice:**
1. **Gelatin against agar (60 min).** Set the same juice with each. Serve a cube of each warm and cold, and compare.
2. **Strength by percentage (60 min).** Set agar at three doses (for example 0.4, 0.7 and 1 %). Cut them and photograph
   them side by side.

**Look for:**
- doses logged to 0.01 g;
- the agar gel holding its shape warm while the gelatin melts.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Agar doesn't set | Not boiled | Boil 1–2 minutes, whisking |
| A weak gelatin set | Gelatin boiled | Dissolve it warm, off the boil |
| Results you can't repeat | Doses by teaspoon | Weigh on the 0.01 g scale |

**Safety:** boiling agar spits: a deep pan and a long whisk. Gels made from a stock or a dairy base are kept like
leftovers (assessment §9).

**Record:** the doses in grams and percent; the photos; which agent you would use for a warm plate and why.

### MOD-4.M3.L2 — Fluid gels and sheets

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mod-4-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `MOD-4.M3.L1` · moves TS-MOD→2*

*Objectives:* [S] fluid gels by shearing; gel sheets
<!-- END generated:lesson-mod-4-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a fluid gel and a gel sheet carry a strong flavour in a shape a sauce or a purée can't hold. Both
start from the set gel of `MOD-4.M3.L1`; the blender or the tray decides what it becomes.

**You need:** 500 g of a seasonal juice or a well-seasoned stock (apple, beetroot, celeriac or mushroom); agar; the
0.01 g scale; a pan; a blender; a fine sieve; a spatula; a flat tray or a sheet pan lined with clingfilm; a ruler; a
squeeze bottle.

**Explanation:**

*The fluid gel.* A firm agar gel (about 0.8–1 %), blended to a smooth, glossy purée. It pours and spreads when stirred,
but holds its place on the plate, carrying a strong flavour: an apple, a beetroot, a herb or a stock.
1. Make the gel as in `MOD-4.M3.L1`; let it set firm.
2. Cut it up and blend it until smooth, adding a spoon of the liquid if it is stiff.
3. Pass it through a fine sieve.
4. Remove the air bubbles by resting it or tapping the container.
5. Fill a squeeze bottle.

*Gel sheets.* Pour a thin layer (2–3 mm) of a stronger gel onto a flat tray, let it set, cut it and drape it over a
dish: a stock or vegetable sheet over a vegetable dish or a piece of fish. A level tray gives an even sheet.

*Flavour first.* Season the base before gelling. Gels mute flavour slightly, so make the base a little stronger than
you would serve it.

**Practice:**
1. **A fluid gel (60 min).** Make an agar fluid gel from a seasonal juice or stock, blend and sieve it, and use it as a
   sauce.
2. **A gel sheet (60 min).** Pour a sheet, cut it and drape it over a dish; measure its thickness.

**Look for:**
- a fluid gel that flows when stirred and stands on a plate with a glossy surface;
- a sheet that lifts without tearing.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A grainy fluid gel | Too little blending, or the gel too firm | Blend longer; lower the dose next time |
| A sheet that tears at one side | Poured unevenly | Level the tray; pour from the centre |
| A bland gel | The base seasoned for drinking | Season it a little stronger |

**Safety:** a gel made from a stock is kept like leftovers: cooled by the cooling rule, in the fridge, used within 2
days (assessment §9). The blender lid held on with a cloth when blending warm.

**Record:** the dose; the photos of the gel on a plate; the sheet's thickness; where you used each.

### MOD-4.M4.L1 — Foams and airs

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mod-4-m4-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M4.L2` · moves SC-EMUL→4, TS-MOD→2*

*Objectives:* [S] lecithin airs; whipped foams; a siphon (optional); [K] foam stability
<!-- END generated:lesson-mod-4-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a foam carries a flavour in a light texture, but most foams collapse within minutes. Knowing what
holds a foam up, and how long each kind lasts, decides which one reaches the table intact.

**You need:** 300 ml of a thin, well-flavoured liquid (a vegetable stock, an apple juice, the whey from making
cheese); soy lecithin; gelatin; 200 ml of cream; the 0.01 g scale; a stick blender; a wide, shallow container; a mixer
or whisk; a timer; the siphon and the maker's chargers if you have them.

**Explanation:**

*Foam stability.* A foam is gas held in a liquid by a film. It lasts when the film is strengthened (proteins, fat
crystals, lecithin, gelatin) and when the liquid is thick enough (sugar, xanthan) to slow drainage. Without them,
bubbles merge and collapse within minutes.

*The lecithin air.*
1. Weigh soy lecithin at about 0.3–1 % of the liquid: for 300 g, 0.90–3.00 g.
2. Blend it into the liquid.
3. Hold the stick blender at an angle at the surface, half in and half out, so it draws in air.
4. Let the air settle for a minute; spoon it off the top just before serving.

It lasts a few minutes.

*Whipped foams.* A base stabilised with gelatin or with cream, whipped in a mixer: firmer, and it lasts longer.

*The siphon (optional).* A warm or cold base (a purée, a cream-based mixture, or a liquid with gelatin) goes into the
siphon, is charged with N₂O, shaken and dispensed. Use it only as its maker's instructions say:
- the maker's chargers only;
- never fill above the fill line;
- screw the head on straight;
- release the pressure before opening;
- never inhale the gas;
- buy chargers for food use, a few at a time (assessment §9 [S50]).

**Practice:**
1. **Airs (60 min).** Make a lecithin air from a stock or juice. Time its life; photograph it at 0, 2 and 5 minutes.
2. **Whipped or siphon foam (60 min).** Whip a stabilised foam (or make one in the siphon) and time its stability
   against the air.

**Look for:**
- an air of fine, even bubbles with a clear flavour;
- a stabilised foam that holds its shape for the time you stated.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No air forms | The base too thick | Thin it, or strain it |
| A soapy taste | Too much lecithin | Stay within 0.3–1 % |
| The siphon sprays or leaks | Filled above the line, or opened under pressure | Fill to the line; release the pressure first |

**Safety:** the siphon rules above; a hot base handled as hot liquid, the siphon held with a cloth. A foam made from a
cream or stock base is kept like leftovers (assessment §9).

**Record:** the doses; the timed photos of both foams; which lasted and why.

### MOD-4.M4.L2 — Stabilised emulsions

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mod-4-m4-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `MOD-4.M4.L1` · moves SC-EMUL→4, FL-TEXT→4*

*Objectives:* [S] xanthan in dressings and sauces; [J] dose by texture
<!-- END generated:lesson-mod-4-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a pinch of xanthan keeps a vinaigrette from splitting on the plate and gives a *jus* body without
butter or flour. It is so strong that the dose is judged in tenths of a percent.

**You need:** for the vinaigrette, 150 g of oil, 50 g of cider vinegar, mustard and salt; for the *jus*, 300 g of a
reduced vegetable or mushroom stock; xanthan gum; the 0.01 g scale; a stick blender; a tall jug; spoons; white plates;
a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Xanthan gum.* A polysaccharide made by microbes that thickens cold or hot liquids at tiny doses (about 0.1–0.5 % of
the liquid's weight) and keeps emulsions from separating. It gives a clinging, pourable body, slightly slick at higher
doses.

*Dispersing it.* Sprinkle it into the liquid while blending, or mix it with sugar or oil first, so it doesn't form
lumps. It thickens at once.

*Uses:*
- a vinaigrette that doesn't split on the plate;
- a vegetable *jus* with body without butter or flour;
- a purée that doesn't weep.

*Dose by texture.* Start low and add in steps:

| Step | Dose | For 300 g |
|---|---|---|
| Start | 0.1 % | 0.30 g |
| Then | +0.05 % | +0.15 g |

Judge each step by the coat on a spoon and the line it leaves on a plate. Stop before it turns slick. Blend for half a
minute after each step, then wait a moment: the body you see then is the body on the plate.

**Practice:**
1. **Two stabilised sauces (60 min).** Make a vinaigrette and a vegetable *jus*, dosing each in steps. Plate each
   step, and log the doses and textures.

**Look for:**
- a vinaigrette that stays emulsified on the plate for 15 minutes;
- a *jus* that coats a spoon and holds a clean line, without slickness.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Lumps | Xanthan dumped in | Sprinkle it into the vortex while blending |
| A slimy sauce | An overdose | Start again lower; add in 0.05 % steps |

**Safety:** a *jus* from a stock kept like leftovers (assessment §9); the blender started below the surface, so hot
liquid doesn't spray.

**Record:** the dose at each step; the plate photos; the dose you chose and why.

### MOD-4.M4.X1 — A foam or stabilised sauce where it improves a dish

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-mod-4-m4-x1 -->
*brief, brief* — Add one foam or stabilised sauce to a dish where it improves it, with a rationale.
<!-- END generated:exercise-mod-4-m4-x1 -->

**Task:** add one foam or stabilised sauce to a dish where it makes the dish better, with a rationale, and prove it.
Make both a stable foam and a xanthan-thickened sauce from formulas, with doses weighed to 0.1 g or better: the foam
with its target texture and ratio stated in writing and a timed stability check. Serve the dish with a texture contrast
named in advance and confirmed on the guests' cards.

**You need:** soy lecithin, xanthan, gelatin; the dish's ingredients (seasonal); the siphon if you have one; the 0.01 g
scale; tasters' cards.

*Procedure:*
1. Write the rationale, the target foam texture and ratio, and the named contrast.

## MOD-4.M5 — Dehydration and crisps

**Knowledge check:** none for this module. It has no knowledge or judgment objective; the safe use of the mandoline was
checked in `SAF-3.M2`.

### MOD-4.M5.L1 — Crisps, tuiles and powders

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-mod-4-m5-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 1 d · builds on `PRS-3.M5.L1` · moves TS-MOD→2, FL-TEXT→4*

*Objectives:* [S] dehydrate in a low oven; make tuiles and vegetable crisps; fat powders (optional)
<!-- END generated:lesson-mod-4-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a crisp element gives a soft dish the contrast it lacks. Drying, baking thin and grinding turn
seasonal vegetables and fruit into crisps, tuiles and powders that keep for days, if they are kept dry.

**You need:** two vegetables or fruits (beetroot, parsnip, carrot, celeriac, apple, pear), about 300 g each; the
mandoline with its guard; sheet pans with parchment or a silicone mat; for tuiles, 50 g each of soft butter, sugar, egg
white and flour; a tuile stencil; an offset spatula; a rolling pin to shape them; the scale; airtight boxes with a bag
of dry rice; a spice grinder (optional).

**Explanation:**

*Dehydrating in a low oven* (`PRS-3.M5.L1`). Slice vegetables or fruit thin and even, with the mandoline and its guard
(`SAF-3.M2.L2`). Dry them at about 60–90 °C with the door slightly open, or in a dehydrator, until brittle. Weigh them
before and after: the loss shows how much water left. Vegetables, fruit and starches only; no meat in this course.

*Tuiles.*
1. Mix a thin batter of equal weights of butter, sugar, egg white and flour (or a savoury version with cheese or seeds).
2. Spread it thin through the stencil onto the mat.
3. Bake until golden at the edges.
4. Shape while warm, over a rolling pin; they set as they cool.

*Vegetable crisps.* Thin slices, fried or baked; or a purée spread thin and dried into a sheet, then broken into
shards.

*Powders (optional).* Grind a dried vegetable fine. A fat powder is made from a flavoured oil and tapioca maltodextrin:
it turns the oil into a powder that melts in the mouth.

*Keeping crisp.* In an airtight box with a little dry rice or a desiccant sachet; added to the plate at the last moment.

**Practice:**
1. **Dried crisps (60 min active, a day of drying).** Slice and dry two vegetables; log their weights before and after.
2. **Tuiles and a shard (60 min).** Make a tuile and a purée shard; one powder if you like.

**Look for:**
- crisps that snap, with even colour;
- tuiles thin and even, shaped without cracks;
- a clear flavour in each crisp element.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Some burnt, some leathery | Uneven slices | The mandoline at one setting; turn the trays |
| A soft tuile | Spread too thick | Scrape it level with the stencil |
| Soft crisps within an hour | Left out | Airtight, with rice; add at the last moment |

**Safety:** the mandoline with its guard (`SAF-3.M2.L2`); hot trays with dry cloths; no meat dried.

**Record:** the weights before and after; photos of each element; how long each stayed crisp.

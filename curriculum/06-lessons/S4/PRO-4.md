**Before you start.** S2 taught the everyday proteins and S3 the long cooks, the grill and home butchery. This course
adds the proteins a home cook meets less often, and asks for precision with all of them:
- **duck and game birds:** a duck breast with rendered skin and a pink centre; pigeon, pheasant and partridge cooked
  without drying out;
- **game:** farmed venison cooked precisely, and wild game (wild boar, wild venison, hare) braised;
- **offal:** calf's liver, kidneys and sweetbreads, where the window between right and ruined is narrow;
- **whole fish:** roasted or salt-baked, filleted at the table, with doneness judged per species;
- **shellfish:** crab and lobster (dispatched by the fishmonger, or bought cooked), scallops and squid;
- **a doneness map:** every protein and method you know, with your own prediction record.

Several of these foods have their own safety rules, and the course follows them exactly (assessment §9):
- **Duck and game birds are poultry.** Their "pink" comes from the low-temperature poultry table (for example 60 °C
  held for 35 minutes at the core), never from a pan-only rare breast.
- **Farmed game is meat; wild game is not.** Farmed venison is cooked like intact meat (63 °C for 15 seconds); hunted
  game (wild boar, wild deer, hare) to 75 °C, which suits braising. Wild boar comes only from a supplier of game
  examined for *Trichinella*.
- **Offal is not whole muscle.** Liver and kidneys are cooked as non-intact meat (for example 66 °C held for 1 minute),
  which still leaves calf's liver rosy. No liver in pregnancy (the risk-group row [S46]).
- **Fish with a translucent centre** below 60 °C is fish that was frozen first by the fish row, and is not served to
  risk groups.
- **Live crabs and lobsters are not dispatched at home.** The welfare sources [S40][S41] say manual dispatch may fail in
  untrained hands. They are bought cooked, or dispatched by a trained fishmonger and cooked without delay.

Meat and fish are Dutch or North Sea: duck from a Dutch farm; game birds and wild game from a poulterer in autumn and
winter; farmed venison from a Dutch deer farm or a butcher; calf's liver and kidneys from a butcher; North Sea fish (sea
bass, brill, gurnard, plaice); Oosterschelde lobster in its spring and summer season; North Sea brown crab;
MSC-labelled scallops and squid.

Plan about five weeks at 6.5 hours a week, cooking the dishes as household and guest meals. The five `TS-PRO` services
stretch over at least eight weeks, so `PRO-4.M6` closes during the next course. Follow the season: game in autumn and
winter, lobster in spring and summer.

**Have ready:** the instant-read and probe thermometers; the prediction log (since `POU-2.M6.L2`); a skillet, a heavy
sauté pan, a stockpot and a *braadpan*; the circulator and bags if you have them (otherwise the stove-top water bath
of `SLW-3.M5.L1`); a roasting tin; filleting knife, fish spatula, a fish knife and serving spoon; shellfish tools;
kitchen shears; a second board for raw protein; shallow lidded containers; the cooling log.

### PRO-4.M1.L1 — Duck breast

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pro-4-m1-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BUT-3.M1.L2` · moves TS-PRO→4, IN-MEA→4*

*Objectives:* [S] score, render from a cold pan, cook pink, rest
<!-- END generated:lesson-pro-4-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a duck breast with thin, crisp skin and an even rosy centre is a restaurant dish a home cook can
match. Because duck is poultry, the pink is reached by time at temperature, and that skill serves every game bird to
come.

**You need:** 2 duck breasts from a Dutch farm (about 350 g each); salt and pepper; a cold heavy skillet; a small bowl
for the fat; the circulator and a bag, or a pan of water on the stove with the thermometer clipped in; the probe; a
board and a sharp knife; for a sauce, 200 ml of duck or chicken stock and a seasonal fruit (Dutch cherries in summer, an
apple or pear in autumn, an orange in winter).

**Explanation:**

*The breast.* A thick fat layer under the skin over lean, dark meat. The aim is skin rendered thin and crisp, and a
rosy, juicy centre.

*Scoring.* Cut a crosshatch about 1 cm apart through the skin and fat, but not into the meat, so the fat can escape and
the skin doesn't curl.

*Rendering from a cold pan.* Skin-side down in a cold, dry pan over medium-low heat for 10–15 minutes. Pour off the fat
as it runs (keep it for potatoes, `BUT-3.M1.L2`), until the skin is thin, brown and crisp.

*Pink, safely.* Duck is poultry (assessment §9). The program reaches a rosy centre with the low-temperature poultry
table:
- **with a circulator** (`SLW-3`): seal the breast and hold it in the bath until the core has been at 60 °C for 35
  minutes (or 63.3 °C for 11.5 minutes, a little less pink); then skin-side down in the pan to crisp;
- **without one:** the stove-top water bath of `SLW-3.M5.L1`, the core probed and the time counted from 60 °C;
- **a pan-only breast** is cooked to 75 °C at the core.

A pan-only rare breast (around 55 °C) has no row in §9 and is not taught.

*Resting.* 5 minutes skin-up, so the skin stays crisp; then slice across the grain.

*Seasonal sauces.* A duck *jus* (the stock reduced with a spoon of the fat's browned bits) with Dutch cherries in
summer, apples or pears in autumn, or a *bigarade*-style orange sauce from pantry citrus in winter.

**Practice:**
1. **The water-bath breast (90 min).** Score one breast and hold it by the table; log the time the core reaches 60 °C
   and the time it comes out. Crisp the skin from a cold pan; rest; slice; photograph the cross-section.
2. **Pan-only to 75 °C (45 min).** Render a second breast and roast it to 75 °C at the core. Taste both side by side.

**Look for:**
- skin thin, brown and crisp, with no white fat left under it;
- an even rosy colour from edge to edge in the bath-cooked breast;
- the probe log showing the core time at temperature.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Burnt skin, fat still thick | A hot pan at the start | Start cold, medium-low |
| Juice on the board, dry meat | Scored into the meat | Cut through the fat only |
| Pink but unsafe | Bath time counted from when the bag went in | Count from when the core reaches 60 °C |
| A flooded board | Sliced at once | Rest 5 minutes |

**Safety:** duck by the poultry row or the low-temperature poultry table (assessment §9); hot duck fat poured into a
heatproof bowl, away from you; raw duck on its own board.

**Record:** the probe log with times at temperature; the cross-section photos of both breasts; which you preferred, and
why.

### PRO-4.M1.L2 — Game birds

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pro-4-m1-l2 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · builds on `PRO-4.M1.L1` · moves IN-MEA→4*

*Objectives:* [K] pheasant, pigeon, partridge; hanging and season; [S] roast and rest without drying
<!-- END generated:lesson-pro-4-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** game birds are lean and dry out fast. Taking them apart, the breasts cooked by the table and the
legs braised, is how both parts come out right, and it is the method behind most game-bird dishes in restaurants.

**You need:** a pheasant or two pigeons from a poulterer in season; kitchen shears and a boning knife; butter; for the
legs, an onion, a carrot, 200 ml of stock, a little red wine, thyme and juniper; the circulator or stove-top water bath;
the probe; seasonal vegetables (Savoy cabbage, celeriac).

**Explanation:**

*The birds:*
- **pheasant:** mild and lean, the hen more tender than the cock;
- **partridge:** small (one per person) and delicate;
- **pigeon** (wood pigeon or farmed squab): a dark, rich breast, best rosy; the legs tough;
- all lean, so they dry out fast.

*Hanging and season.* Game birds are hung (aged) by the supplier for flavour and tenderness; the longer the hang, the
stronger the taste. Wild birds come in the autumn and winter shooting season, and the poulterer's offer shows what is
in season. Shot may be left in the meat: feel for it, and warn your guests.

*Safety.* Game birds are poultry (assessment §9): the breasts by the low-temperature poultry table for a rosy centre,
or to 75 °C; the legs braised.

*Roasting without drying:*
- barding (a thin sheet of pork fat or bacon over the breast) or basting with butter;
- a hot oven for a short time for the whole bird, then rested, with the breasts finished by the table if needed;
- or, best, taken apart: the breasts cooked separately, the legs braised or confit.

*Taking a bird apart.* Cut the legs away at the hip joint. Cut along the breastbone and lift each breast off the frame
with the knife against the bone, as in `BUT-3.M1.L1`. Roast the frame for a sauce.

*Garnishes of the season.* Cabbage, celeriac, a bread sauce without chestnuts, juniper, apples and pears.

**Practice:**
1. **A game bird two ways (2 h).** Take a pheasant or two pigeons apart. Cook the breasts by the poultry table and crisp
   them; braise the legs with the vegetables, wine and stock until tender; make a sauce from the roasted carcass and the
   braising liquid.

**Look for:**
- moist, rosy breast meat at the logged core time;
- legs tender off the bone;
- no shot found by guests.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dry breast | The whole bird roasted until the legs were done | Take it apart |
| Pink but unsafe | No time at temperature | Follow the table; log it |
| Guests surprised by a strong taste | A long hang | Ask the supplier; tell the guests |

**Safety:** breasts by the low-temperature poultry table or 75 °C; braised legs well above 75 °C; raw birds on their
own board, hands washed; shot removed and guests warned.

**Record:** the probe log; photos of the breasts in cross-section and of the plate; the hang and source of the bird.

### PRO-4.M2.L1 — Venison and wild boar

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pro-4-m2-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M3.L3` · moves TS-PRO→5*

*Objectives:* [K] lean meat and doneness; marinade myths; [S] sear and rest a venison loin
<!-- END generated:lesson-pro-4-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** venison is so lean that a few degrees decide between juicy and dry. Knowing which game is farmed and
which is wild decides the target, and the target decides the method.

**You need:** a farmed venison loin (about 500 g) from a Dutch deer farm or a butcher; a second lean cut (a venison
steak or a fillet of farmed deer); a thin flexible knife; oil, salt, pepper; a heavy skillet; the oven; the probe; a
board.

**Explanation:**

*Lean meat and doneness.* Venison has almost no marbling, so it goes from juicy to dry within a few degrees. Cook it
fast and hot, or slow and wet; never in between.

*Farmed or wild* (assessment §9):
- **farmed deer** (commercially raised): intact cuts are cooked like other intact meat, 63 °C for 15 seconds at the
  core, which leaves the loin rosy-pink. The steak row is for beef only;
- **wild game** (hunted deer, wild boar, hare): 75 °C, so the tender cuts are better used in braises, and the loin is
  for farmed deer;
- **wild boar** only from a supplier of inspected game, whose carcasses are examined for *Trichinella* as EU law
  requires [S39].

*Marinade myths.* A marinade flavours the surface; it does not tenderise lean meat deeply, and acid left long makes the
outside mushy. The "gamey" taste is tamed by trimming the silver skin and fat, not by soaking.

*Searing and resting a loin:*
1. Trim off all the silver skin: slide the knife under it, blade angled up, and pull the skin taut.
2. Dry the loin; season it.
3. Sear it hot on all sides.
4. Finish it in a low oven to just below the target, predicting the carryover; rest it until it reaches 63 °C.
5. Slice across the grain.

**Practice:**
1. **A farmed venison loin (75 min).** Sear and finish it to 63 °C after the rest. Write the pull temperature and the
   predicted carryover first; log the actual carryover; photograph the cross-section.
2. **A second lean cut (60 min).** A venison steak or a farmed-deer fillet, cooked by cue first, then probed.

**Look for:**
- a deep crust and an even rosy centre with a thin grey band;
- the probe within 2 °C of the target after the rest;
- no silver skin on the plate.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A curled, chewy loin | Silver skin left on | Trim it all off first |
| Grey and dry after resting | Taken to 63 °C in the oven, rising on the board | Pull it earlier; predict the carryover |
| Unsafe | Wild venison served rosy | Wild game to 75 °C: braise it |

**Safety:** farmed venison 63 °C for 15 seconds; wild game 75 °C; wild boar only from inspected game (assessment §9);
raw game on its own board.

**Record:** the pull temperature, predicted and actual carryover; photos; the source of the venison (farmed or wild).

### PRO-4.M2.L2 — Game stews and sauces

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pro-4-m2-l2 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 3 h · builds on `SLW-3.M2.L1` · moves FL-ARCH→4*

*Objectives:* [S] a game braise; [J] balance gamey flavours with fruit, juniper, acid
<!-- END generated:lesson-pro-4-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** wild game is cooked to 75 °C, and braising turns that rule into an advantage: the tough, flavourful
cuts become tender. Balancing the deep, gamey taste is the judgment that makes a braise taste of the season.

**You need:** 1 kg of wild-boar or wild-venison shoulder or neck from a game supplier, in large cubes; 2 onions, 2
carrots, a celery stalk; 300 ml of red wine, 400 ml of stock; 8 juniper berries, 2 bay leaves, thyme, black pepper; 2
apples or pears (or plums, red currants or cranberries in season); a tablespoon of red-wine vinegar; a square of bitter
chocolate or a spoon of *stroop*; a *braadpan*; the probe; shallow containers for cooling.

**Explanation:**

*The game braise* (`SLW-3.M2.L1`). Brown the meat in batches, sweat the vegetables, deglaze with the wine, add the
stock and aromatics, and braise at a bare simmer until tender: 2.5–3.5 hours, judged by a fork, well above the 75 °C of
wild game.

*Balancing gamey flavours:*
- **fruit** for sweetness against the depth: Dutch apples, pears, plums, red currants or cranberries in season;
- **juniper, bay, thyme and black pepper** for resinous, warm notes (crush the juniper so it gives its flavour);
- **acid** (red-wine vinegar, the wine itself) to cut the richness;
- **a little bitter chocolate or a spoon of *stroop*** (apple syrup) to round the sauce.

Add each in small steps and taste between them. Taste at three points: when the braise goes in, halfway, and at the
end.

*Sauces.* The braising liquid, strained and reduced; or a *poivrade* (`CLS-4.M3.L2`) for game loins.

*Cooling and reheating.* A braise for the next day is cooled by the rule of assessment §9 (57 °C to 21 °C within 2
hours, 5 °C or below within 6): in shallow containers, logged. Reheat it until steaming hot throughout, every part at
least 74 °C on the probe, once.

**Practice:**
1. **A wild-boar or venison braise (60 min active, 3 h elapsed).** Braise it, balancing with fruit, juniper and acid;
   taste at the three points and adjust. Cool it and log the readings for the next day.

**Look for:**
- meat tender enough to cut with a spoon;
- a sauce that is deep but bright, with no single note too loud;
- the cooling and reheating log in range.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stringy meat | The braise boiled | A bare simmer |
| A sauce that tastes of jam | Too much fruit | Add acid and pepper; less fruit next time |
| A harsh resin taste, or none | Juniper too much, or not crushed | Crush 6–8 berries for 1 kg |

**Safety:** wild game to 75 °C; the cooling rule logged; reheated to 74 °C throughout, once (assessment §9).

**Record:** the three tastings and what you added; the cooling log; the reheating reading.

### PRO-4.M3.L1 — Preparing offal

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pro-4-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `MEA-2.M1.L1` · moves IN-MEA→4*

*Objectives:* [S] trim and soak liver, kidneys, sweetbreads; [K] freshness and sourcing
<!-- END generated:lesson-pro-4-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** offal is cheap, rich and quick to cook, but only after careful preparation. Clean trimming decides
whether liver stays flat, kidneys taste mild, and sweetbreads hold their shape.

**You need:** 400 g of calf's liver, 2 veal kidneys and 400 g of veal sweetbreads, from a butcher, bought the day you
prepare them; a separate board for raw offal; a sharp thin knife; 250 ml of milk; salt; a bowl of cold water; a small
pan; two plates and a weight (a few tins) for pressing; the fridge thermometer.

**Explanation:**

*Freshness and sourcing.* Offal spoils faster than muscle meat. Buy it fresh from a butcher close to cooking (or
frozen). It should smell clean and sweet, and look glossy and firm. Keep it at 4 °C and cook it soon. Calf's liver is
milder and more tender than pig's or ox liver; veal kidneys are milder than pork.

*Liver.* Peel off the thin outer membrane: lift an edge with the knife and pull it away. Cut out any tubes. Slice it
about 1 cm thick, evenly. For a milder taste, soak the slices in milk for an hour in the fridge.

*Kidneys.* Remove the fat and the membrane, halve them lengthways, and cut out the white core with the tip of the
knife. Pork kidneys are soaked briefly in salted water.

*Sweetbreads* (the thymus; NL *zwezerik*, FR *ris de veau*):
1. Soak them in cold water in the fridge for a few hours, changing the water, to draw out the blood.
2. Blanch them in simmering salted water for 2–3 minutes; cool them in cold water.
3. Peel off the membranes and pick off fat and sinew, keeping the lobes whole.
4. Press them between two plates under a weight in the fridge overnight, so they hold their shape.

*Hygiene.* Raw offal is raw meat: its own board, hands washed after, the board washed hot (`SAF-1`).

**Practice:**
1. **Preparing three kinds (75 min).** Slice the calf's liver; clean the veal kidneys; soak, blanch, peel and press the
   sweetbreads for the next day.

**Look for:**
- liver slices of even thickness, with no membrane;
- kidney halves with the core cut out cleanly;
- sweetbreads firm and evenly shaped after pressing.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Liver slices that curl | Membrane left on | Peel it all off |
| A strong taste in the kidneys | Cores left in | Cut out every white part |
| Sweetbreads falling apart | Not pressed | Press overnight under a weight |

**Safety:** offal kept at 4 °C and cooked within a day; its own board; blanched sweetbreads cooled and kept in the
fridge, then cooked by the offal row (`PRO-4.M3.L2`).

**Record:** the weights before and after trimming; photos of each prepared offal; the butcher and date.

### PRO-4.M3.L2 — Cooking offal

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pro-4-m3-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `PRO-4.M3.L1` · moves TS-PRO→5*

*Objectives:* [S] calf's liver pink, kidneys with a sauce, crisp sweetbreads; [J] the narrow doneness window
<!-- END generated:lesson-pro-4-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** liver and kidneys turn from tender to grainy within a few degrees and a minute. Cooking them to the
offal row of §9, with the probe and the rest, keeps them both safe and rosy.

**You need:** the prepared liver, kidneys and sweetbreads (`PRO-4.M3.L1`); flour; 50 g of butter and a little oil;
4 onions for slow-cooked onions; for the kidneys, a shallot, 2 tablespoons of Dijon mustard and 100 ml of cream; for
the sweetbreads, capers and a lemon; a heavy skillet and a warm second pan; the probe; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*The rule* (assessment §9). Offal is not whole muscle, so it is cooked as non-intact meat: 68 °C for 17 seconds, or
66 °C for 1 minute, or 63 °C for 3 minutes, or 75 °C instantaneous. Chicken livers are poultry (75 °C). In pregnancy no
liver at all, however it is cooked (the risk-group row [S46]).

*Calf's liver pink.* Flour the slices lightly and sear them in hot butter for about 1–2 minutes a side. Then move them
off the heat into a warm pan and rest them until the core has held 66 °C for 1 minute: rosy, tender and moist. *Lever
met uien*, the Dutch classic, is the same liver with onions cooked slowly in butter until soft and golden (start them
30 minutes ahead).

*Kidneys with a sauce.* Sear them hot and fast in batches, so they brown instead of boiling, then rest them to the same
target. Make the sauce in the pan: the shallot softened, the cream reduced a little, the mustard stirred in off the
heat, the kidneys and their juice returned (*rognons à la moutarde*).

*Crisp sweetbreads.* Slice the pressed sweetbreads or break them into pieces, flour them, and cook them in butter until
golden and crisp, well above the targets. Serve with a caper and brown-butter sauce (`CLS-4.M5.L1`) or a *jus*.

*The narrow window.* Liver and kidneys turn grainy and rubbery within a few degrees and a minute. The probe and the
rest decide, so plan the plate with the offal cooked last: the onions, the sauce base and the garnish ready first.

**Practice:**
1. **Calf's liver (45 min).** Liver with slowly cooked onions. Log the probe readings and the minute at 66 °C;
   photograph the cross-section.
2. **Kidneys and sweetbreads (75 min).** Kidneys in mustard sauce; crisp sweetbreads; each probed and logged.

**Look for:**
- liver rosy and soft, the probe log at the target;
- kidneys tender, not bouncy;
- sweetbreads crisp outside, creamy inside.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Grey, grainy liver | Fried too long | Sear, then rest to the target |
| Kidneys boiling in their juice | A crowded pan | Small batches, a hot pan |
| A bitter, split sauce | Mustard boiled after the kidneys went in | Off the heat |

**Safety:** offal by the offal row (68 °C for 17 seconds, 66 °C for 1 minute, 63 °C for 3 minutes, or 75 °C); chicken
livers 75 °C; no liver in pregnancy (assessment §9).

**Record:** the probe log for each dish with times at temperature; the cross-section photos; what you would change.

### PRO-4.M4.L1 — Whole fish

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pro-4-m4-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BUT-3.M2.L1` · moves IN-FSH→4, TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] roast or salt-bake; fillet at the table
<!-- END generated:lesson-pro-4-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a whole fish brought to the table and filleted there is a centrepiece, and the bone and skin keep
the flesh moist. Filleting cleanly in front of guests is the flat-fish skill of `TS-FAB` level 3.

**You need:** two whole North Sea fish of 1–1.5 kg (sea bass, brill or gurnard), MSC-labelled or in season, gutted by
the fishmonger; for the roast, herbs, a lemon or a fennel bulb, olive oil; for the salt crust, 2 kg of coarse sea salt,
2 egg whites and a little water; a roasting tin; the probe; a fish knife and a large serving spoon; a warm platter; a
scale.

**Explanation:**

*Why whole.* The bone and skin keep the flesh moist and add flavour; a whole fish at the table is a centrepiece.

*Roasting.* Scale the fish, check it is gutted, and cut out the gills (they taste bitter). Slash the thick part two or
three times to the bone, so it cooks as fast as the thin tail. Season inside and out and fill the cavity with herbs and
lemon or fennel. Roast it hot until the flesh by the backbone reaches 63 °C (assessment §9) and lifts from the bone.
Probe at the thickest part, next to the backbone.

*Salt-baking.* Leave the scales on, so the skin protects the flesh. Mix the salt with the egg whites and a little water
to a damp sand; bed the fish on a layer and pack the rest over it. Bake, then crack the crust at the table. The flesh
steams in its own juice and is not salty. Probe through the crust at the thickest part.

*Filleting at the table:*
1. Peel the skin back.
2. Lift the top fillet off the bones in pieces with the fish knife and spoon, working from the centre line outwards.
3. Lift the backbone out whole from the tail, taking the head with it.
4. Serve the bottom fillet; check for pin bones.

*Choosing the fish.* A sea bass, brill (*griet*) or gurnard (*poon*) of 1–1.5 kg feeds four. North Sea, MSC-labelled or
in season.

**Practice:**
1. **A roasted whole fish (75 min).** Probe at the backbone; fillet at the table; weigh the fillets and log the yield.
2. **A salt-baked fish (75 min).** Bake it in salt; crack the crust and fillet it at the table for guests or the
   household.

**Look for:**
- flesh that lifts cleanly from the backbone at 63 °C;
- whole fillet pieces, with the backbone lifted out in one;
- no bones on the plates.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A bitter taste | Gills left in | Cut them out |
| Burnt tail, raw thick part | No slashes | Slash the thick part to the bone |
| Flakes, not pieces | Rushed serving | Lift along the centre line, in pieces |

**Safety:** fish to 63 °C for 15 seconds at the backbone (assessment §9); the knife and spoon kept for cooked fish only;
a hot salt crust cracked with the back of a heavy spoon, away from faces.

**Record:** the fish, weight and source; the probe reading; the yield (fillet weight against whole weight); photos of
the frame and the plates.

### PRO-4.M4.L2 — Doneness across species

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pro-4-m4-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `FSH-2.M3.L4` · moves TS-PRO→5, JD-CUES→4*

*Objectives:* [J] lean versus oily, firm versus delicate; translucent centres on purpose
<!-- END generated:lesson-pro-4-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** fish species differ more than cuts of meat. Knowing which are lean, oily, firm or delicate, and when
a translucent centre is allowed, lets you choose the point at which each is best, and defend it.

**You need:** three fillets cooked by one method: a lean fish (cod, whiting or plaice), an oily fish (mackerel or
herring) and a firm fish (monkfish or brill), about 150 g each; for the translucent dish, a salmon or trout portion that
was frozen first by the fish row, with its record; a skillet; the probe.

**Explanation:**

*Lean against oily.* Lean white fish (cod, whiting, plaice) firm up and dry quickly once they flake. Oily fish
(mackerel, herring, salmon) stay moist longer.

*Firm against delicate.* Firm fish (monkfish, turbot, brill) take longer and hold together. Delicate fish (sole, plaice,
whiting) need gentle heat.

*Translucent centres on purpose.* Some fish is best just short of opaque at the centre: salmon at about 50 °C, for
example. Assessment §9 allows this only for fish frozen first by the fish row, and never for risk groups:
- −20 °C or below in all parts for at least 24 hours (bought so treated, with the label or the fishmonger's word); or
- 7 days in a home freezer shown by a thermometer to hold −20 °C or below. A freezer at −18 °C does not qualify.

Otherwise, fish is cooked to 63 °C. Decide per dish, and write the reason.

*Cues and the probe.* The flesh changes from glassy to opaque and flakes under light pressure. Probe at the thickest
part. Write the cue before you probe, as in the prediction log.

**Practice:**
1. **Three species compared (60 min).** Cook a lean, an oily and a firm fish by the same method (pan-roasted). For each,
   write the cue first, then probe; note the texture at 63 °C.
2. **A translucent centre (60 min).** Cook the frozen-first salmon or trout to a chosen translucent target. Keep the
   freezing record with the log.

**Look for:**
- each species at its own best point, recorded;
- the freezing record for the translucent dish.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dry lean fish | All fish treated the same | Gentler heat; take it off as it flakes |
| A translucent centre that isn't allowed | Fresh, never-frozen fish | Cook it to 63 °C, or freeze first |
| A wrong reading | The thin end probed | The thickest part |

**Safety:** fish to 63 °C for 15 seconds, or below it only if frozen first by the fish row and not for risk groups
(assessment §9).

**Record:** the three cues, readings and textures; the freezing record; the reason for the translucent target.

### PRO-4.M5.L1 — Crab and lobster

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pro-4-m5-l1 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · builds on `FSH-2.M4.L1` · moves IN-FSH→4, SA-TEMP→5*

*Objectives:* [K] humane dispatch (by a trained fishmonger) and why it is not done at home; [S] cooking times, extracting meat; [K] storage and safety of live and cooked shellfish
<!-- END generated:lesson-pro-4-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** crab and lobster are the luxury of the North Sea coast. Knowing why they are not dispatched at
home, how to cook them the hour they are dispatched, and how to take every bit of meat out, makes them worth their
price.

**You need:** an Oosterschelde lobster (in its spring and summer season) dispatched by a trained fishmonger and
collected in a cool bag, or a cooked North Sea brown crab (*Noordzeekrab*); a large pot or a grill; the probe; shellfish
tools (a crab cracker or nutcracker, picks or a skewer); two bowls for white and brown meat; a scale; for the stock,
the shells, a little *mirepoix*, tomato purée and water; shallow containers.

**Explanation:**

*Humane dispatch, and why it is not done at home* (assessment §9) [S40][S41]:
- crabs and lobsters can feel pain, and boiling alive can take minutes to kill them;
- the methods judged most reasonable (double spiking for crabs, splitting a lobster along its whole length, ideally
  after electrical stunning) destroy all the nerve centres quickly, but may fail in untrained hands;
- boiling alive, drowning in fresh water, chilling in a freezer or on ice, and pulling off the tail or claws alive are
  inhumane;
- so the program buys crab and lobster cooked, or has a trained fishmonger dispatch them, and cooks them without delay.

*Cooking.* A dispatched lobster is split and grilled, roasted, or cut and poached, until the meat is opaque and reaches
63 °C (the fish row of §9). A cooked brown crab comes from the fishmonger ready to pick.

*Extracting the meat:*
- **lobster:** crack the claws and pull the meat out whole; take the tail meat out in one piece; note the coral (roe)
  and the green tomalley (liver);
- **crab:** twist off the legs and claws and crack them; open the body; discard the gills (the "dead man's fingers");
  pick the white meat from the body and scoop the brown meat from the shell into its own bowl.

*Storage and safety.* Cooked shellfish are kept at 4 °C and eaten within 2 days, or frozen (the leftovers row). A
dispatched lobster is cooked without delay. Mussels and clams that stay open when tapped are discarded (`FSH-2.M4`).

*The shells.* Roast them for a shellfish stock or a shellfish butter (`PRO-4.M5.X1`): nothing is wasted.

**Practice:**
1. **Crab or lobster (2.5 h).** A lobster dispatched by the fishmonger and cooked the same hour, or a cooked brown crab.
   Extract the meat and weigh it. Roast the shells for a stock or butter; cool it and log the readings.

**Look for:**
- lobster meat opaque and springy, probed at 63 °C;
- claw meat out in whole pieces; no shell in the picked crab;
- the shell stock cooled within the limits.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Tough, stringy lobster | Overcooked | Probe; take it off at 63 °C |
| Grit or a bitter taste in the crab | Gills left in | Discard all the gills |
| An unsafe lobster | Kept in the fridge until tomorrow after dispatch | Cook it the same hour |

**Safety:** no dispatch at home (the crustacean row, [S40][S41]); lobster to 63 °C; cooked shellfish at 4 °C, eaten
within 2 days or frozen; the shell stock cooled by the cooling rule (57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, 5 °C or below within
6). Crustaceans are one of the 14 allergen groups: name them for guests.

**Record:** the time from dispatch or purchase to cooking; the probe reading; the meat weights (white and brown); the
cooling log of the stock.

### PRO-4.M5.L2 — Scallops and squid

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pro-4-m5-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `FSH-2.M4.L2` · moves TS-PRO→5*

*Objectives:* [S] sear scallops; squid either very fast or very slow; [K] why
<!-- END generated:lesson-pro-4-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** scallops and squid punish hesitation: a wet scallop steams, and squid cooked a minute too long turns
to rubber. The protein science behind "very fast or very slow" applies to every quick-cooking protein.

**You need:** 6 MSC-labelled scallops (frozen ones count as frozen first, with the pack as the record); 500 g of North
Sea squid (in autumn); kitchen paper; a little neutral oil; a very hot skillet; for the braise, an onion, garlic,
400 g of tinned tomatoes, a little wine, a *braadpan*; the probe; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Scallops.* Pull off the small side muscle. Pat the scallops very dry and season them just before cooking. Sear them in
a very hot pan with a little oil, for 1–2 minutes a side, until a deep crust forms. A translucent centre is a §9
decision: allowed only for scallops frozen first by the fish row (many are sold frozen) and not for risk groups;
otherwise they are cooked to 63 °C.

*Squid.* Pull the head and tentacles from the body, with the innards. Pull out the clear quill. Peel off the skin. Cut
the tentacles off just in front of the eyes and keep them. Score the body in a crosshatch or cut it in rings.

*Why squid is either very fast or very slow.* Squid muscle tightens and turns rubbery as its proteins contract with
heat. In under 2 minutes at very high heat it is cooked but still tender. After that it toughens, and only a long
braise (45–60 minutes) softens it again, as the connective tissue breaks down. In between, it is chewy.

*Sourcing.* MSC-labelled scallops; North Sea squid in autumn.

**Practice:**
1. **Scallops (45 min).** Sear six scallops. Photograph one in cross-section, crust and centre. Write your decision on
   the centre and its reason (the freezing record, the guests).
2. **Squid two ways (75 min).** Sear half the squid in 90 seconds. Braise the other half in tomato and wine for an hour.
   Taste each at 3 minutes and at the end.

**Look for:**
- a deep golden crust on a scallop with a moist centre;
- fast squid tender and white; slow squid soft.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A pale, steamed scallop | Wet scallops | Dry them on paper; a hotter pan |
| Rubbery squid | Five minutes in a pan | Under 2 minutes, or braise it |
| No crust on anything | A crowded pan | Cook in batches |

**Safety:** scallops below 63 °C only if frozen first and not for risk groups (the fish row); squid to 63 °C; molluscs
are one of the 14 allergen groups: name them.

**Record:** the scallop decision and reason; the cross-section photo; the squid tastings at 3 minutes and at the end.

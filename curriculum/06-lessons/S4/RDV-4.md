**Before you start.** Until now you cooked from recipes, frameworks and briefs. This course turns you into someone who
makes recipes:
- **ratios:** the proportions behind doughs, batters, custards, stocks, vinaigrettes, brines and cures [S14], so you no
  longer need a recipe for the basics, and can read any recipe as a ratio;
- **deconstruction:** taking a restaurant or cookbook dish apart into components, techniques and flavour logic, and
  recreating it from a menu description or a memory;
- **substitution and adaptation:** replacing what an ingredient *does*, and adapting a recipe to another oven, pan,
  quantity, season or diet;
- **testing:** one variable at a time, controls, blind panels and versions, until the dish is better or the test says
  stop [S17];
- **writing:** a recipe someone else can cook to the same result, and a first original dish.

The test kitchen is your household kitchen, and the tasters are the household and guests, as in every course. Produce
follows the Dutch seasons: adapting a recipe "to the season" means to what is grown here now.

Plan about four weeks at 6.5 hours a week. The test series of `RDV-4.M4` and the original dish of `RDV-4.M5` need
several rounds a few days apart, so they overlap with the next course.

**Have ready:** the 1 g scale and the 0.1 g scale; a notebook or spreadsheet for numbered versions; tasting cups and the
tasting form (`TST-1`); the oven thermometer; labels and a pen; your recipe file; the receipts of a week's shopping.

### RDV-4.M1.L1 — The core ratios

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-rdv-4-m1-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M1.L1` · moves RD-RATO→4*

*Objectives:* [K] doughs, batters, custards, stocks, vinaigrettes, brines, cures [S14]; [S] cook three from ratios alone
<!-- END generated:lesson-rdv-4-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a handful of ratios sits behind most basic preparations. Knowing them by heart lets you cook any
quantity without a recipe, see at once what a recipe is doing, and vary the flavour without breaking the structure.

**You need:** flour, water, salt, yeast; milk and eggs; oil and vinegar; a cut of meat or poultry for a brine (or a
piece of pork belly for a cure); the 1 g scale; bowls; a container with a lid for the brine; labels.

**Explanation:**

*The core ratios* [S14], by weight:

| Preparation | Ratio | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Bread dough | 5 flour : 3 water (60 % hydration) | Salt 2 % of the flour; yeast to taste (`BRD-2`) |
| Pasta | 3 flour : 2 egg | `CUI-3.M3` |
| Pie dough (*pâte brisée*) | 3 flour : 2 fat : 1 water | |
| Pound cake | 1 flour : 1 butter : 1 egg : 1 sugar | |
| Crêpe batter | 1 flour : 2 liquid : 1 egg | |
| Custard | 2 milk or cream : 1 egg | A set custard; a pouring custard uses yolks only |
| Stock | 3 water : 2 bones | |
| Vinaigrette | 3 oil : 1 acid | |
| Roux | 1 flour : 1 fat | About 60–100 g roux per litre for a *velouté* or *béchamel* |
| Brine | 5–6 % salt by weight of the water | For meat and poultry (`SLW-3`, `GRD-3`) |
| Dry cure | Salt about 2.5–3 % of the meat's weight | The equilibrium method; nitrite only where a tested recipe needs it (assessment §9) |

*Scaling.* Choose the amount of the base ingredient, then work out the rest. For a crêpe batter for four with 125 g of
flour: 250 g of milk and 125 g of egg (two to three eggs).

*Ratios are starting points.* Flours, eggs and fats differ, so adjust by feel and write the change down.

**Practice:**
1. **Three from ratios (60 min).** Make a bread dough, a crêpe batter and a vinaigrette from the ratios alone, scaled
   to your household. Write the weights before you cook.
2. **Two more (60 min).** Make a custard and a brine or a cure from the ratios alone, and photograph the results.

**Look for:**
- each preparation within standard without a recipe;
- the scaled weights written before cooking.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dough or batter far off | Measured by volume | Weigh everything |
| A wet dough or batter | The fruit's or vegetable's own water forgotten | Count it as liquid |

**Safety:** brined and curing meat kept covered in the fridge at 4 °C, as the Dutch home advice says (assessment §9);
nitrite curing salt only where a tested recipe needs it, at most 80 mg of nitrite per kg of meat, weighed from the
label (§9). A custard baked until set.

**Record:** the five sets of scaled weights; the photos; any adjustment and why.

### RDV-4.M1.L2 — From recipe to ratio

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-rdv-4-m1-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `RDV-4.M1.L1` · moves RD-RATO→4, KM-MATH→4*

*Objectives:* [S] turn five recipes into ratios; spot outliers
<!-- END generated:lesson-rdv-4-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** once a recipe is written as a ratio, you can compare it with the core and see whether it is a
different preparation or a mistake. This is how you read a new recipe before you trust it.

**You need:** five recipes from books, websites or your own file, at least one in cups and spoons; a conversion table
for the ingredients in cups (by ingredient, since a cup of flour and a cup of sugar weigh differently); a calculator;
the ratio sheet of `RDV-4.M1.L1`.

**Explanation:**

*Converting a recipe.*
1. Turn every ingredient into grams; convert cups and spoons with the table.
2. Divide each by the base ingredient: flour for doughs, liquid for custards, meat for brines.
3. Write the result as parts or percentages.

A worked example, a recipe for a "pound cake":

| Ingredient | Grams | Parts (flour = 1) |
|---|---|---|
| Flour | 200 | 1 |
| Butter | 100 | 0.5 |
| Egg | 200 | 1 |
| Sugar | 200 | 1 |

The butter is half the core ratio: either a lighter cake by design or a mistake. You decide which, from the method and
the result.

*Spotting outliers.* A recipe far from the core (a "pound cake" with half the butter, a vinaigrette at 1:1) is either a
different preparation or a mistake. Not every difference is a mistake.

*Baker's percentages* (`BRD-2`). Every ingredient as a percentage of the flour. The same idea works for other bases:
salt as a percentage of the meat, sugar as a percentage of the fruit.

*Scaling with a reason.* Salt, spices, leavening and cooking times don't always scale in a straight line (`KM-MATH`):
a doubled stew needs less than double the salt at first, and a thicker loaf takes longer, not twice as long.

**Practice:**
1. **Five recipes into ratios (60 min).** Convert five recipes from books or your file. Mark the outliers and explain
   each.

**Look for:** each recipe written as a ratio, compared with the core, and its outliers explained.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Ratios that make no sense | Volumes converted without a table | Use a table per ingredient |
| Every recipe looks wrong | Every difference called a mistake | Ask what the difference does first |

**Safety:** none beyond reading: where a converted recipe has a cure, a brine or a safety figure, check it against
assessment §9 before you cook it.

**Record:** the five ratio tables; the outliers and your verdict on each.

### RDV-4.M2.L1 — Analysing a dish

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-rdv-4-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `FLV-3.M5.L1` · moves JD-CHSE→4, FL-ARCH→4*

*Objectives:* [J] components, techniques and flavour logic of a restaurant or cookbook dish
<!-- END generated:lesson-rdv-4-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** every dish you eat out is a lesson, if you can take it apart. A grid for components, techniques and
flavour logic turns a good meal into a plan you can cook.

**You need:** a restaurant meal (notes and photos taken at the table) or a cookbook photo with its title; the analysis
grid below; the flavour notes of `FLV-3`.

**Explanation:**

*The analysis grid.* For a dish eaten out or read in a book:

| Line | Ask |
|---|---|
| Components | The main item, sauce, garnish, texture elements |
| Techniques | How each was cooked, how it was cut, what was made ahead |
| Flavour logic (`FLV-3`) | The base, the contrast, the acid, the fat, the aromatics, the season |
| Contrasts | Texture and temperature |
| Intent | What the cook wanted the guest to notice |

*Reading from taste.*
- The tongue and nose tell you salt, acid, sweetness, bitterness, umami and the aromatics.
- The texture tells you about the technique: a confit is silky, a quick sear firm at the edge.
- The colour tells you about browning.

*Uncertainty.* Mark each line as certain, likely or a guess, and write how a test would decide. "The sauce is thickened
with butter (likely: glossy, rich, split slightly as it cooled); a test: mount a reduction and compare."

**Practice:**
1. **Two dishes analysed (60 min).** One from a restaurant meal, with notes and photos taken at the table; one from a
   cookbook photo and its title. Write each in the grid, with the certainty marked on every line.

**Look for:** each component, technique and flavour role named, with the certainty marked.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A list of ingredients | Their role not named | Write what each does |
| A plan built on a wrong guess | A guess treated as a fact | Mark certainty; test the guesses small |

**Safety:** a dish recreated from a restaurant follows the safety rows of assessment §9 at home, whatever the restaurant
did; note anything served raw or rare, and who you would not serve it to.

**Record:** both grids; the photos; the guesses you would test first, and how you would test each one small.

### RDV-4.M2.L2 — Recreating from a description

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-rdv-4-m2-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `RDV-4.M2.L1` · moves CR-IMPV→3*

*Objectives:* [S] recreate a dish from a menu description or a tasting
<!-- END generated:lesson-rdv-4-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** recreating a dish turns analysis into cooking, with the risk that the uncertain part fails at the
table. A plan with a fallback, and the habit of improvising from what is at hand, make you a cook who doesn't depend on
a recipe.

**You need:** one of the analysed dishes of `RDV-4.M2.L1` with its grid; seasonal produce; your repertoire of frameworks
and ratios; for the improvised meal, only what is in the fridge and pantry; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*From analysis to plan.* Turn each component into a method from your repertoire (a framework, a ratio). Test the
uncertain parts small first.

*Fallbacks.* For the riskiest step, have a simpler alternative ready: a pan sauce if the emulsion splits; a purée if
the gel doesn't set.

*Working from what's at hand.* A recreation often meets a missing ingredient: choose the substitute by function
(`RDV-4.M3`). You can improvise a whole meal from the fridge and pantry the same way:
1. Photograph what is there.
2. Choose one main item and one idea.
3. Add one contrast (acid, crunch, freshness) and stop.
4. Cook within 45 minutes.

*Side-by-side notes.* Compare the recreation with the memory or description: what matched, what didn't, and why. Write
them the same evening, while the taste of both is fresh, and mark which differences you would test next.

**Practice:**
1. **A recreation (60 min).** Cook one of the analysed dishes from your plan.
2. **An improvised meal (45 min).** Photograph what is in the fridge and pantry, then cook a meal within 45 minutes
   without a recipe.

**Look for:**
- a dish the tasters recognise from the description;
- an improvised meal on the table within 45 minutes.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The dinner fails at one component | The most uncertain part cooked first at the dinner | Test it small beforehand; keep the fallback ready |
| A muddled improvised meal | Everything added | One idea, one contrast |

**Safety:** leftovers used in an improvised meal only within their 2 days and reheated until steaming hot, once
(assessment §9); a missing ingredient replaced with its allergens checked.

**Record:** the plan with its fallback; the side-by-side notes; the fridge photo and the meal photo with the time.

### RDV-4.M3.L1 — Function-first substitution

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-rdv-4-m3-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `NUT-3.M2.L1` · moves RD-ADPT→4*

*Objectives:* [J] identify what an ingredient does (structure, moisture, fat, flavour) and replace that function
<!-- END generated:lesson-rdv-4-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** an ingredient is replaced well only if you know what it does. Naming its functions first, and
covering each one, gives a substitute that works and doesn't bring a new allergen to the table.

**You need:** a batter, a crumble and a cream sauce from your repertoire, with their ingredients twice; for the
substitutes, ground flaxseed and water, a dairy-free spread or a neutral oil, and an oat or soy cream; the labels of
every substitute; the scale.

**Explanation:**

*What an ingredient does.* Before you replace it, name its functions:
- **structure:** gluten (flour), egg proteins, starch, gelatin;
- **moisture:** water, milk, fruit and vegetable purées;
- **fat:** tenderness, richness, carrying flavour, browning;
- **flavour:** taste, aroma, acidity, sweetness;
- **other:** leavening, emulsifying (egg yolk, mustard), colour, binding.

*Replacing the function.* A substitute must do the same job. If it does only part, cover the rest with another
ingredient: an egg in a cake replaced by a flax "egg" for binding, plus a little extra leavening for lift.

A worked example, butter in a crumble:

| Function of butter | Replaced by |
|---|---|
| Fat: binds the crumbs, makes them tender | A dairy-free spread or oil |
| Browning and flavour | A little extra sugar; a longer bake if pale |
| Water (butter is partly water) | Check the spread's label; adjust if the crumbs are dry |

*Allergens and diets* (`NUT-3`).
- Gluten-free flour blends need a binder (xanthan, psyllium).
- Dairy-free butter substitutes differ in water content.
- Nuts are replaced by seeds.
- The 14 allergen groups are checked on every label: a substitute carries allergens of its own.

*Predicting the change.* Write it before cooking; check it against the result.

**Practice:**
1. **Three substitutions by function (60 min).** For example egg in a batter, butter in a crumble, cream in a sauce.
   Predict each, cook it against the original, and compare.

**Look for:** the prediction names the function lost and how it is replaced; the result is close to the original.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A crumbly or flat result | Swapped by name (any flour for any flour) | Name the function; replace each part |
| A new allergen on the table | The substitute's label not read | Read every label against the guest's allergies |

**Safety:** every substitute's label read for the 14 allergen groups and the guest's other allergies (`NUT-3`); the
substitute's label kept with the dish.

**Record:** the three predictions and results; the labels you checked.

### RDV-4.M3.L2 — Adapting to equipment, scale and season

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-rdv-4-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SLW-3.M4.L2` · moves RD-ADPT→4*

*Objectives:* [S] adapt a recipe for a different oven, pan, quantity or season; [J] predict what changes
<!-- END generated:lesson-rdv-4-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a recipe is written for one oven, one pan, one quantity and one season. Predicting what changes when
any of these changes is what lets you cook the recipe anywhere, for anyone, all year.

**You need:** a household dish you know well, with twice its ingredients; a dish from the other half of the year and
your notes or memory of it; the oven thermometer; a larger pan or a second one.

**Explanation:**

*Another oven.* Check it with the oven thermometer (`EQP-1`). Fan ovens run about 20 °C lower than conventional ones for
the same result. Turn the trays around if the oven has hot spots.

*Another pan.* A wider pan reduces faster and browns more; a smaller pan crowds. Cast iron holds heat; stainless steel
responds faster.

*Another quantity.*
- Scale the weights.
- Adjust the salt and spices by taste.
- Judge cooking times by thickness rather than weight.
- Split batches when the pan or oven can't take them (`KM-MATH`).

*Another season.* A summer dish moved to winter keeps its idea with the vegetables of the season (courgette becomes
celeriac; fresh herbs become dried, or parsley root), and its cooking adjusts (roasting rather than raw).

*Predicting what changes.* Write it before cooking (time, texture, browning, seasoning) and check it after. For a
doubled stew in the same pan, for example: deeper liquid, so a longer simmer; less browning per batch, so sear in two
rounds.

**Practice:**
1. **Twice the quantity (60 min).** Double a household dish. Write the predicted changes and check them.
2. **Another season (60 min).** Adapt a dish from the other half of the year to the current season, and taste it
   against your notes or memory of the original.

**Look for:** predictions that hold, or a clear reason why not.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too salty, overcooked | Salt and time doubled | Salt by taste; time by thickness |
| Hard, raw winter roots | A summer dish's raw method kept | Roast or braise them |

**Safety:** a doubled batch cooled by the cooling rule, in shallow containers (assessment §9); the core temperatures of
§9 checked with the probe, not taken from the original time.

**Record:** the predictions next to what happened; photos of both dishes.

### RDV-4.M4.L1 — Designing tests

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-rdv-4-m4-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M1.L1` · moves RD-TEST→4*

*Objectives:* [S] one variable at a time, controls, blind panels, scoring forms
<!-- END generated:lesson-rdv-4-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a dish improves only if you know which change made the difference. One variable, a control and a
blind panel turn tasting into a test whose result you can trust [S17].

**You need:** oil, vinegar, mustard and salt for two small vinaigrettes; the scale; tasting cups labelled with
three-digit codes; the tasting form (`TST-1`); two or three tasters; plain bread or lettuce to taste on.

**Explanation:**

*One variable at a time.* Each round changes one thing (the salt, the time, the fat), so the result can be traced to it.

*Controls.* Cook the unchanged version alongside, so day-to-day differences (the oven, the ingredients) don't hide the
effect.

*Blind panels* (`TST-1`, [S20]).
- Code the samples with three-digit numbers.
- Serve them in a random order.
- Tasters score on a form, without talking.
- A triangle test (two the same, one different; which is the odd one?) checks whether a difference can be tasted at
  all.

*Scoring forms.* List the attributes that matter for the dish (texture, seasoning, aroma), each on a fixed scale, with
room for comments.

*Small batches.* Test at a small scale to save food; cook the final version at full size.

A test plan for a vinaigrette:

| Line | Entry |
|---|---|
| Aim | A sharper vinaigrette for a winter salad |
| Variable | Acid: 3:1 (control) against 2.5:1 |
| Coded samples | 418 (control), 762 (test) |
| Form | Sharpness, balance, would you use it? (1–5), comments |

**Practice:**
1. **A test design (60 min).** Write a test plan for one variable with a control and a blind panel, and run it once on
   a small batch (for example a vinaigrette at two acid levels).

**Look for:** a plan with one variable, a control, coded samples and a form; results that can be read.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Results you can't explain | Recipe and oven changed together | One variable per round |
| A biased panel | Tasters know which is which | Codes and a random order |

**Safety:** tasting cups and spoons used once per taster; tasters asked about allergies before the panel.

**Record:** the test plan; the forms; what the panel found.

### RDV-4.M4.L2 — Iteration

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-rdv-4-m4-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 2 wk · builds on `RDV-4.M4.L1` · moves RD-TEST→4, RD-WRIT→3*

*Objectives:* [S] document versions; [J] decide what to change next and when to stop
<!-- END generated:lesson-rdv-4-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a dish improves over versions, and versions are easy to lose. A log, and a rule for what to change
next and when to stop, keep the work going forward instead of in circles.

**You need:** the results of `RDV-4.M4.L1`; the dish's ingredients for two more rounds; the test log (a notebook with
numbered versions, or a spreadsheet with a tab per round); the panel; a camera.

**Explanation:**

*Version control.* Write each version in full, with a number and date, what changed, why, and the result. Keep the log
with photos.

*Deciding what to change next.* Sort the panel's comments by how often they appear and how much they matter. Test the
largest problem first.

*When to stop.*
- The panel can't tell the new version from the last (a triangle test fails).
- The remaining comments contradict each other.
- The dish meets its aim.

*Writing so it repeats.* The final recipe has weights, cues and timings. Cook it again a week later to check it gives
the same result. If it doesn't, the recipe is missing something you did without writing it down: find it, add it, and
cook it once more.

*Spacing the rounds.* Leave a few days between rounds, so the panel tastes fresh and you have time to read the forms.

A log line:

| Version | Date | Changed | Why | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 14 Nov | Acid 3:1 → 2.5:1 | Panel: "flat" (3 of 3) | Sharper; 2 of 3 prefer it |

**Practice:**
1. **Round two (60 min).** Make the second version from round one's results; run the panel; log it.
2. **Round three and the write-up (60 min).** Make the third version; write the decision; write the recipe in full.

**Look for:** a log that shows why each change was made and what it did.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The dish drifts to the cook's taste | The panel ignored | Follow the panel, or write why not |
| You can't go back to a better version | Earlier versions lost | Write every version in full |

**Safety:** test batches kept like leftovers (assessment §9); food that waits for a panel kept hot or cold, not warm.

**Record:** the full log with photos; the decision to stop and why.

## RDV-4.M5 — Writing recipes and a first original dish

**Knowledge check:** none for this module. Its judgment objectives (testing with another cook, what makes a dish your
own) are shown by the other cooks' results and your note in `RDV-4.M5.X1`.

### RDV-4.M5.L1 — Writing for others

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-rdv-4-m5-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `KMG-3.M5.L1` · moves RD-WRIT→4*

*Objectives:* [S] precise quantities, ordered steps, cues, yields, timings; [J] test with another cook
<!-- END generated:lesson-rdv-4-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a recipe works only if someone else can cook it to your result. Writing for another cook, and
watching them cook from your text in silence, shows every gap in it.

**You need:** one of your recipes; a household member who will cook it; the ingredients; a notebook for the silent
test.

**Explanation:**

*The recipe's parts:*
1. **Title and yield:** portions and weight.
2. **Ingredients** in the order of use, by weight, with their state (diced, softened), and the allergens marked.
3. **Equipment** that matters: the pan size, a thermometer.
4. **Steps** in order, one action each, with the cue that ends the step (golden, coats a spoon, 63 °C at the core) and
   the time as a range.
5. **Make-ahead and storage notes** within assessment §9.

*Writing for another cook.* No unexplained terms (`CK-TERM`); cues before times; the safety figure where there is one.

Before and after:

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Season to taste | Add 4 g of salt; taste, and add more a pinch at a time |
| Cook the onions, add garlic and spices and simmer the sauce | 1. Cook the onions over medium heat until soft and golden, 10–12 minutes. 2. Add the garlic; stir for 1 minute. 3. … |
| Bake for 30 minutes | Bake until golden and a skewer comes out clean, 25–35 minutes |

*Testing with another cook.* A household member cooks from the text alone while you watch silently and note every
hesitation. Rewrite the recipe where they hesitated.

**Practice:**
1. **A recipe rewritten (60 min).** Write one of your recipes for another cook.
2. **The silent test (60 min).** A household member cooks it; note the hesitations; revise the recipe.

**Look for:** another cook's result matches yours, and their hesitations led to clear changes.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Under- or over-seasoned | "Season to taste" without a starting amount | Give a starting amount |
| Overcooked or raw | Times without cues | Cue first, time as a range |
| Steps missed | Three actions in one step | One action per step |

**Safety:** every safety figure (core temperatures, cooling, storage) written in the recipe as assessment §9 gives it;
the allergens marked in the ingredient list.

**Record:** the first and revised versions; the hesitation notes; photos of both results.

### RDV-4.M5.L2 — An original dish

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-rdv-4-m5-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 2 wk · builds on `RDV-4.M4.L2` · moves CR-ORIG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] idea, test plan, iterations, final recipe; [J] what makes it yours
<!-- END generated:lesson-rdv-4-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** an original dish brings together everything in this course: an idea, a test plan, rounds of
tasting and a recipe someone else can cook. What makes it yours is a choice you can explain, not novelty.

**You need:** Dutch produce in season as the starting point; the test log; the panel; two rounds' ingredients; the
recipe format of `RDV-4.M5.L1`.

**Explanation:**

*The idea.* One sentence (`CR-COMP`): a season, a memory, a technique or an ingredient pairing. For example, "late
autumn: smoked celeriac with the sharpness of pickled red onion and the crunch of toasted seeds".

*The test plan* (`RDV-4.M4`). Write what is uncertain about the idea and how two or more rounds will settle it.

*Iterations.* Cook the dish, have tasters judge it, change one thing at a time, and log it. Two rounds is the minimum;
stop when the dish meets its idea, by the rules of `RDV-4.M4.L2`.

*Starting from the season.* Begin with what is at its best now in the Dutch harvest, then choose the technique and
balance that show it. An idea that needs an imported ingredient to work is the wrong idea for this course.

*The final recipe.* Written for others (`RDV-4.M5.L1`).

*What makes it yours.* Not novelty for its own sake, but a choice of ingredients, technique or balance that reflects
your taste and place. Write it in a short note: what you chose, and why it is yours.

**Practice:**
1. **Idea and first round (60 min).** Write the idea and the test plan; cook the first version; have it judged.
2. **Second round (60 min).** Cook the second version from the results; have it judged; write the recipe.

**Look for:** a logged development, a final recipe and a note on what makes it your own.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dish that doesn't come together | An idea with too many parts | Cut the idea to one sentence; drop what doesn't serve it |
| You can't tell what helped | Everything changed between rounds | One change per round |

**Safety:** the dish's safety figures from assessment §9 written into its recipe; tasters asked about allergies first.

**Record:** the idea; the test log; the recipe; the note on what makes it yours.

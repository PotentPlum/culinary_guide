**Before you start.** A professional kitchen runs on plans, lists, costs and storage. A household kitchen runs on the
same things, usually without writing them down. This course writes them down, so that cooking well every day fits your
time and your money:
- **weekly planning:** a balanced, seasonal week of meals around your real schedule, with one prep day;
- **purchasing:** quantities per person, one consolidated shopping list, and where to buy what (Good or Best);
- **cost and yield:** cost per portion from the yields of `BUT-3`, and a budget week that keeps its quality;
- **leftovers and the freezer:** cooked food turned into new dishes within the safety limits, and a freezer that is used
  rather than filled;
- **house recipes:** the household's best dishes written so that anyone can cook them.

The safety figures of [assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment) run through every
module:
- leftovers into the fridge within 2 hours, and kept at most 2 days;
- the fridge at 4 °C, the freezer at −18 °C or colder;
- reheated until steaming hot throughout, and only once;
- batches cooled by the standard: from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C or below within 6 hours in total.

The course runs on the household's real weeks: plan about four weeks, each module one week of planning, shopping,
cooking and logging. Keep every plan, list, costing and log: `MS-S3` asks for the same documents.

**Have ready:** the planning, list, costing and comparison templates (a notebook, or a spreadsheet with them linked);
the balance checklist (`CR-MENU`); your yield log from `BUT-3.M4`; the 1 g scale; shallow containers with lids; freezer
boxes and flat freezer bags; labels and a marker; the fridge and freezer thermometers; the journal.

### KMG-3.M1.L1 — A week of meals

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-kmg-3-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 wk · builds on `WRK-1.M2.L3` · moves CR-MENU→2, KM-PLAN→4*

*Objectives:* [S] balance, variety, season and time across a week; [J] plan around a busy schedule
<!-- END generated:lesson-kmg-3-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a plan decides the week before the week decides for you. Without one, the busy evenings become
takeaway and the vegetables go limp in the drawer.

**You need:** the planning template; the household's calendar for the coming week; the balance checklist (`CR-MENU`);
the seasonal calendar (`VEG-1`, `VEG-3.M1`); your house recipes and frameworks so far.

**Explanation:**

*Balance across a week.* Check the week against the balance checklist:
- a range of proteins: legumes, fish twice, poultry, meat, eggs;
- vegetables at every meal;
- whole grains;
- no main technique, colour or dominant taste repeated two days running.

*Season.* Start from what is best and cheapest now (`VEG-3.M1`), not from a fixed rotation. In November that means
cabbages, leeks, roots, pumpkin and *witlof*; in June, the first peas, broad beans and lettuces.

*Around a busy schedule.* Read the calendar first, then fill in the meals:
- **busy days:** quick meals of 15–30 minutes, built from prepared components;
- **free days:** a longer cook;
- **cook once, eat twice:** a braise on Sunday, its second portion on Tuesday, within the 2-day limit, or frozen;
- **a fallback:** one meal from the pantry, for the evening the plan breaks.

*One dinner as a menu.* Once a week, plan a three-course dinner as a menu and check it against the balance checklist:
the courses differ in technique, colour and richness, and the dessert doesn't repeat the main's dominant taste.

*Lunches.* Write down which lunches come from which dinners: a portion of soup, a grain salad from Monday's grains.
Those portions go on the shopping list too.

*An example week, in November:*
- Monday (busy): soup from the stock made on Sunday, with smoked-mackerel toast;
- Tuesday (busy): the second portion of Sunday's beef braise, with mash;
- Wednesday: a white-bean and leek stew with a crisp crumb;
- Thursday (busy): an omelette with *witlof* salad;
- Friday: baked mackerel with beetroot and barley;
- Saturday: a three-course dinner, a roast chicken as the main;
- Sunday: the braise, and a pot of grains for the week.

**Practice:**
1. **Plan the week (60 min).** Seven dinners and the lunches that come from them, on the planning template, checked
   against the balance checklist and the household's calendar.

**Look for:** every busy day has a quick meal; every long cook feeds two days; the checklist passes.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A week of pasta | Planned from habit | Run the balance checklist; vary the base |
| A two-hour recipe on the busiest evening | The calendar not read first | Calendar first; long cooks on free days |
| Takeaway on Thursday | No fallback | One pantry meal in every plan |
| The second portion goes off | Eaten too late | Within 2 days, or frozen the day it is cooked |

**Safety:** a second portion planned within 2 days of cooking, or frozen the same day (§9).

**Record:** the plan, the checklist, and a note on the calendar's busy days.

### KMG-3.M1.L2 — A prep day

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-kmg-3-m1-l2 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · builds on `KMG-3.M1.L1` · moves KM-PLAN→4, TS-MEP→4*

*Objectives:* [S] batch components (grains, sauces, roasted vegetables, stock) for the week
<!-- END generated:lesson-kmg-3-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** an afternoon of batch cooking makes the busy evenings quick. It only works with a written task list
and the fridge's 2-day limit in mind.

**You need:** the week's plan; the ingredients for the components; the oven; large pans; shallow containers with lids;
labels and a marker; a timer; a phone for timed photos; the probe.

**Explanation:**

*What to batch:* components that keep, and that save time later:
- a pot of grains or legumes;
- a stock;
- a sauce base;
- roasted vegetables;
- washed and dried leaves;
- a dressing, a marinade, a dough.

*The task list.* Write it in a reasoned order:
1. the longest tasks and the oven tasks first;
2. the stock in the background;
3. washing and cutting while things cook;
4. cold tasks last.

Where you can, share one oven temperature for everything that roasts.

*Clean as you go.* A bin bowl on the board; a clean board between tasks; the sink emptied between stages. Take a photo
of the station at set times (every 30 minutes); they show whether you kept it clean.

*Storage:*
- every component cooled by the standard: shallow containers, smaller portions, an ice-water bath for a stock;
- labelled containers (name, date);
- at most 2 days in the fridge, unless frozen (§9);
- the plan says which day each component is used. What is needed after day 2 goes into the freezer today.

*The fridge limit.* Prepping for seven days does not work when the fridge allows 2. Prep for the first half of the
week, freeze for the second, or run two short prep sessions.

**Practice:**
1. **The prep day (2.5 h).** The components of the week's plan batched from a written task list; timed station photos;
   everything labelled and stored.

**Look for:** no forgotten item; a clean station at each timed photo; every container labelled with name and date.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Thursday's grains are out of date | Prepped for seven days | Fridge for days 1–2; freezer for the rest |
| A warm, crowded fridge | Warm containers stacked | Cool first, in shallow containers; then the fridge |
| An item forgotten | The task list in the head | Write it; tick it off |
| A cluttered station | No bin bowl, no reset between tasks | Bin bowl; clean board between tasks |

**Safety:** batches cooled from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours and to 5 °C or below within 6 hours (§9); probe a large
batch to check; the fridge at 4 °C.

**Record:** the task list with actual times, the station photos, and the labels' contents.

### KMG-3.M2.L1 — Quantities per person

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-kmg-3-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `WRK-1.M3.L2` · moves KM-PURC→4, KM-MATH→4*

*Objectives:* [S] standard portion sizes; quantities from plans; a consolidated shopping list
<!-- END generated:lesson-kmg-3-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** buying per recipe gives three half-used bunches of parsley and no onions on Thursday. One list for
the week, from portions you have checked, buys what you will use.

**You need:** the week's plan and its recipes; your yield log from `BUT-3.M4`; the pantry inventory; the list template;
a calculator.

**Explanation:**

*Working portions.* Start from standard amounts per main portion, adjust them to your household's appetites, record them
once and reuse them:
- raw boneless meat or fish: about 125–150 g;
- dry pasta or rice: 75–100 g;
- vegetables: 200–250 g;
- dry legumes: 60–80 g.

*From plan to list:*
1. Scale every recipe's quantities to the household.
2. Add them up across the week, item by item.
3. Subtract what is already in the pantry (the inventory).

*Yield in purchasing.* A whole bird, a vegetable with its trim, a fish on the bone are bought by the as-purchased weight
that gives the usable portion (`BUT-3.M4`). Divide the usable weight you need by the yield factor in your log. For
example, if your log shows that leeks give 0.6 usable, 600 g of trimmed leek needs 600 ÷ 0.6 = 1,000 g bought.

*Not everything scales in a straight line.* Seasoning, braising liquid, oven times and frying oil change less than the
number of portions. A double batch of stew in a wider pot needs less than double the liquid; the salt is added to
taste, not doubled. Write the reason beside each item you don't scale straight.

*The consolidated list.* Grouped by shop and by section (vegetables, butcher, dairy, dry goods), with quantities and
units. Check it against the plan, item by item, before you leave.

**Practice:**
1. **The list (60 min).** The coming week's plan turned into a consolidated list, scaled, with the yields applied and
   the pantry subtracted.

**Look for:** a list that matches the plan item by item.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Half-used bunches and missing staples | Buying per recipe | Add up across the week, by item |
| Too little for the dish | The trim forgotten | Divide by the yield factor |
| An over-salted double batch | Salt doubled with the portions | Salt to taste; note the non-linear items |
| Two jars of cumin | The pantry not checked | Subtract the inventory first |

**Safety:** the cold items grouped on the list so they are bought last and go straight into the fridge.

**Record:** the working portions, the scaled list with yields, and the non-linear items with reasons.

### KMG-3.M2.L2 — Where and how to buy

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-kmg-3-m2-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `VEG-1.M2.L1` · moves KM-PURC→4, IN-SEL→4*

*Objectives:* [J] supermarket, market, specialist, online; seasonal buying; Good/Best decisions
<!-- END generated:lesson-kmg-3-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** where you buy changes what you pay and what you cook with. A reasoned choice puts the money where it
changes the result.

**You need:** the consolidated list; a budget; a notebook or phone for notes; bags, and a cool bag for fish and meat.

**Explanation:**

*Where:*
- **the supermarket** for staples and convenience;
- **the market and farm shops** for seasonal produce at its peak;
- **specialists** (butcher, fishmonger, cheese shop, an Asian or Turkish grocer) for quality, advice and particular
  items;
- **online and vegetable-box schemes** for regular deliveries.

*Seasonal buying.* The cheapest and best produce is the produce in season. The seasonal calendar (`VEG-1`) and what the
market has this week set the plan, not the other way round.

*Good or Best.* Where the better product changes the result, it is worth paying for:
- fish freshness;
- bread flour;
- a cheese eaten on its own.

Where it doesn't (cooking onions, canned tomatoes), Good is good enough. Write each decision with its reason.

*Judging at the shop.* Look, smell, firmness, dates, origin (`IN-SEL`). When something is not good (limp leeks,
dull-eyed fish), make a reasoned change: another vegetable from the same family, another fish of the same type, or the
dish moved to another day. Note the change.

*Receipts.* Keep them. They are the data for `KMG-3.M3`, where the costing is checked against them.

**Practice:**
1. **The shopping round (60 min).** The week's list bought across at least two kinds of shop. Note the Good or Best
   decisions with reasons, and make one reasoned change at the shop.

**Look for:** every main item's quality, price and purpose noted.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Poor fish bought because it was on the list | Buying the plan whatever the quality | Judge first; change the item or the day |
| Over budget | Best prices for Good uses | Best only where it changes the result |
| No costing possible later | No receipts kept | Keep every receipt with the week's list |
| Warm fish at home | The cold items bought first | Cold items last; a cool bag |

**Safety:** fish, meat and dairy in a cool bag and into the fridge at 4 °C as soon as you are home; dates checked at the
shop.

**Record:** the shops, the decisions and their reasons, the change made, and the receipts.

### KMG-3.M3.L1 — Cost per portion

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-kmg-3-m3-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `BUT-3.M4.L1` · moves KM-MATH→4*

*Objectives:* [S] cost a recipe with yield factors; [K] where the money goes
<!-- END generated:lesson-kmg-3-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** knowing what a portion costs tells you where the money goes, and which changes would save it. A
costing that ignores the trim, the oil and the waste is wrong in the same direction every time: too cheap.

**You need:** three of the week's recipes; the receipts; your yield log (`BUT-3.M4`); the costing sheet; a calculator.

**Explanation:**

*Costing a recipe:*
1. Each ingredient's quantity **as purchased**, with its yield factor from `BUT-3.M4`.
2. Its price per unit, from the receipt.
3. Its cost: quantity × price.
4. The total for the recipe.
5. Divided by the number of portions.

Small items (spices, oil, salt) are added as a flat percentage of the total rather than weighed one by one. Choose the
percentage once, from a few recipes you have weighed, and write it on the sheet.

*Yield factors:*
- **trim:** usable weight ÷ purchased weight;
- **cooking loss** for protein served by weight: cooked weight ÷ raw weight.

*A worked example.* A recipe costs €14.40 in total and serves six: €14.40 ÷ 6 = €2.40 per portion. If the leeks in it
were costed at their trimmed weight, the cost is too low: 600 g of trimmed leek at a yield of 0.6 is 1,000 g bought, and
at €3.00 per kg that is €3.00, not €1.80.

*Where the money goes.* In most home menus the protein and the dairy are most of the cost. Seasonal vegetables, grains
and legumes are cheap. Waste is a hidden cost: food bought and thrown away is paid for too.

*The costing sheet.* Keep one for each house recipe, and update it when prices change. It becomes the costing line of
the house recipe in `KMG-3.M5`.

**Practice:**
1. **Cost three dishes (60 min).** Three of the week's dishes costed per portion with yield factors, on the sheet;
   checked against the receipts.

**Look for:** a costing within 10 % of the receipts.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The costing is too low | Trimmed weight costed at the whole price | Cost the as-purchased weight |
| A few percent short every time | Oil and butter forgotten | Add them, or the flat percentage |
| The week costs more than the dishes | Waste ignored | Log the waste; it is part of the cost |
| A meat portion short | Cooking loss ignored | Use the cooked-weight factor |

**Safety:** cost cutting never changes a §9 figure; a cheaper cut is still cooked to its figure.

**Record:** the three costing sheets, the receipts, and the difference between them.

### KMG-3.M3.L2 — Budget menus

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-kmg-3-m3-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 wk · builds on `KMG-3.M3.L1` · moves KM-PURC→4*

*Objectives:* [J] cheaper cuts, seasonal produce and pantry staples; [S] a budget week without losing quality
<!-- END generated:lesson-kmg-3-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a cheaper week can still be a good week. The skill is knowing which savings cost nothing in quality,
and which two things are never cut.

**You need:** the costing sheets; the seasonal calendar; the planning template; the pantry and preserves; the household
cards.

**Explanation:**

*Cutting cost without cutting quality:*
- **cheaper cuts cooked well** (`SLW-3`): shin, cheek, shoulder, chicken legs, braised slowly;
- **whole birds and fish,** with every part used (`BUT-3`): the carcass for stock, the trim for a filling;
- **legumes and grains as the base,** protein as the accent;
- **seasonal produce** at its peak;
- **the pantry and preserves** used (`PRS-3`);
- **leftovers planned in** (`KMG-3.M4`);
- **Best only** where it changes the result.

*Where quality must not be cut:*
- **freshness of fish and meat:** a cheap fish that is not fresh is not a saving;
- **safety:** a cheap cut is still cooked to its §9 figure, and a leftover still kept within 2 days.

*A budget week:*
1. Set an amount per person per day.
2. Design the menu to it.
3. Cost it in advance, dish by dish.
4. Check it against the receipts.

*Variety on a budget.* A budget met by eating the same thing every day fails the household cards. Vary the base and
the technique: a lentil soup, a chicken-leg braise, a vegetable gratin, an egg dish, a whole mackerel. Keep the
vegetables: they are among the cheapest items on the list.

**Practice:**
1. **A budget week (60 min of planning, then the week).** A week of dinners designed to a set budget and costed in
   advance.

**Look for:** within budget, and the household cards don't drop.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Tough, cheap meat | A braising cut cooked fast | Slow, moist heat until tender (`SLW-3`) |
| The household tires of it | The same dish every day | Vary the base and the technique |
| Few vegetables | Skipped to save money | Seasonal vegetables are cheap; keep them |
| Over budget by Thursday | Not costed in advance | Cost every dish before shopping |

**Safety:** cheaper cuts cooked to their §9 figures; whole birds and offal by their rows; leftovers within 2 days.

**Record:** the budget, the costed menu, the receipts, and the cards.

### KMG-3.M4.L1 — Transforming leftovers

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-kmg-3-m4-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SAF-3.M1.L1` · moves CR-IMPV→3, KM-WAST→4*

*Objectives:* [S] re-use cooked food in new dishes (soups, fritters, gratins, fried rice, salads); [J] safety limits
<!-- END generated:lesson-kmg-3-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a leftover reheated on a plate is yesterday's dinner. Turned into a new dish, it is a quick meal
that nobody minds, and less food in the bin. Both only within the limits of §9.

**You need:** the week's leftovers, each labelled with its date; the pantry; eggs and flour for binding; fresh herbs,
lemons, vinegar; the frameworks from `FLV-3`; a timer; a phone for the "before" photo; the probe.

**Explanation:**

*New dishes, not reheated plates:*
- **soups:** roasted vegetables blended with stock;
- **fritters and croquettes:** mash, grains or vegetables bound with egg and flour;
- **gratins and bakes:** vegetables, pasta, a sauce, a crust;
- **fried rice and grain bowls:** cold cooked rice fries best;
- **salads:** grains, legumes, roasted vegetables, a sharp dressing;
- **fillings:** a braise in a pie, a roast in a sandwich;
- ***stamppot*** and hash.

*Changing texture and flavour* (`FLV-3`). A crisp element, a fresh herb, an acid and a new sauce turn yesterday's dish
into a new one. Roasted roots become a soup with a spoon of yoghurt and toasted seeds; a braise becomes a pie with a
crisp crust.

*Safety limits* (assessment §9):
- leftovers into the fridge within 2 hours of cooking, cooled quickly, covered;
- at most 2 days in the fridge, or frozen;
- reheated until steaming hot throughout, **and only once**: a leftover that was already reheated is not reheated again;
- cooked rice cooled quickly and kept cold (*Bacillus cereus*, `SCI-2.M6`);
- when in doubt, throw it out.

A fritter made from a leftover counts as its reheating: it is eaten that meal, not kept and reheated again.

*Improvising quickly.* Photograph what is at hand first. Choose a framework (soup, fritter, gratin, fried rice, salad).
Put a meal on the table in 45 minutes.

**Practice:**
1. **Two transformations (90 min).** Two leftovers from the week turned into new dishes from frameworks, each within 45
   minutes.
2. **A pantry meal (45 min).** A meal from what is at hand, photographed first, without a recipe.

**Look for:** a new dish, not a reheated plate; within 45 minutes; every leftover within its 2 days.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dish reheated twice | Leftovers of leftovers kept | Reheat once; eat it that meal |
| A leftover "until it smells" | No date on the box | Label every box; at most 2 days |
| A fritter falls apart | Too little binder | More egg and flour; chill before frying |
| It tastes like yesterday | Nothing changed | Add a crisp element, an acid, a fresh herb |

**Safety:** the dates checked before cooking; reheated until steaming hot throughout, once; rice cooled fast and kept
cold; anything past 2 days or of unknown age thrown out (§9).

### KMG-3.M4.L2 — Freezer management

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-kmg-3-m4-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 wk · builds on `SAF-1.M3.L3` · moves IN-STO→4, KM-WAST→4*

*Objectives:* [K] what freezes well and what doesn't; [S] portion, label, rotate first in, first out
<!-- END generated:lesson-kmg-3-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a freezer is a store of quick meals, or a box of unlabelled ice. Freezing well, and an inventory you
actually use, decide which.

**You need:** the freezer; the freezer thermometer; flat freezer bags and shallow boxes; labels and a marker; a cool box
for the audit; the inventory template (on paper on the door, or on the phone).

**Explanation:**

*What freezes well:*
- stocks, soups, braises, sauces;
- cooked legumes and grains;
- doughs and baked bread;
- raw meat and fish;
- blanched vegetables;
- herbs in oil or butter, kept frozen;
- fruit for cooking.

*What freezes badly:*
- raw salad vegetables and cucumber: they go limp;
- cooked potatoes in large pieces: grainy;
- dairy-heavy sauces: they split;
- mayonnaise;
- fried foods: soggy;
- cooked egg whites: rubbery.

*Freezing well:*
- cooled first, by the standard; never hot into the freezer;
- flat bags or shallow containers, the air pressed out (against freezer burn);
- portioned for one meal;
- labelled with the content, the date, and a use-by date for quality.

*The freezer at −18 °C or colder* (§9). Check it with a thermometer, not the dial.

*Thawing.* In the fridge, or cooked from frozen. Never on the counter.

*Rotation.* Keep an inventory on the door or on the phone: first in, first out, checked monthly. Give each item a
quality limit (for example, most cooked dishes within a few months). The limit is for quality: over the months, frozen
food dries and loses flavour.

**Practice:**
1. **Freezer audit (45 min).** Everything taken out into a cool box, listed, labelled and dated. What is past its
   quality limit is used first or discarded. The inventory is set up.

**Look for:** an inventory that matches the freezer; every item labelled and dated.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Unlabelled tubs of "brown stew" | No label at freezing | Content, date, use-by on every item |
| Ice crystals, dry patches | Air left in the bag | Press the air out; flat bags |
| A warm freezer and a thawed edge | Hot food put in | Cool by the standard first |
| A stew thawed on the counter | No plan for thawing | Into the fridge the day before |

**Safety:** the freezer at −18 °C or colder (§9); food cooled before freezing; thawed in the fridge or cooked from
frozen; the audit done quickly, with the food in a cool box.

**Record:** the freezer temperature, the inventory, and what was used or discarded.

### KMG-3.M5.L1 — Writing house recipes

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-kmg-3-m5-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `WRK-1.M4.L2` · moves RD-WRIT→3, JD-REFL→4*

*Objectives:* [S] clear batch recipes with yields, storage and reheating notes; [J] review what worked this course; [S] have a household member cook one of them from the page
<!-- END generated:lesson-kmg-3-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a dish the household loves is lost if only you can cook it, and only on a good day. Written well, it
comes out the same every time, and someone else can cook it from the page.

**You need:** three dishes from S3 that the household wants again; the house-recipe template and checklist; the 1 g
scale; the costing sheets (`KMG-3.M3`); a household member willing to cook; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*A house recipe:*
- **a title** and one line on what it is;
- **the yield:** portions, or a batch size;
- **ingredients by weight,** in the order of use;
- **numbered steps with cues,** temperatures and times: "until the onions are soft and golden, about 10 minutes";
- **storage:** fridge days within §9, freezing;
- **reheating notes:** until steaming hot throughout, once;
- **a costing line** (`KMG-3.M3`).

*Cues, not just times.* A time depends on your pan and your stove; a cue doesn't. Give both: the cue to judge by, the
time as a guide. Where a probe decides (a chicken, a minced filling), give the §9 figure.

*Seasoning.* "Season to taste" helps nobody who doesn't know the dish. Give a starting amount by weight, and say what to
taste for: "6 g of salt; taste, and add more until the broth tastes full, not salty".

*Testing it:*
1. Cook it again a week later from the page only. Note every place where you needed your memory.
2. A household member cooks it from the page while you watch without helping. Write down every question they ask.
3. Write the gaps into the next version.

*Reviewing the course.* Which planning, buying and storage habits saved time and money; which didn't stick, and why.
Look for a pattern across the logs (the prep day that always ran over; the item always thrown away), and set a practice
plan for S4.

**Practice:**
1. **Write and repeat (75 min).** Three house recipes written; each cooked again from the page a week later and
   compared.
2. **Someone else cooks (75 min).** A household member cooks one recipe from the page; you note every question they ask.

**Look for:** a recipe another person can follow without asking; the repeat matches the first.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The cook asks "how much salt?" | "Season to taste" | A starting weight and what to taste for |
| Different every time | Cups and handfuls | Weights, from the scale |
| A leftover kept too long | No storage notes | Fridge days, freezing, reheating on every recipe |
| The cook stalls at a step | A time without a cue | Add the cue you judge by |

**Safety:** every house recipe carries its §9 figures (core temperatures, fridge days, reheating once); allergens listed
under the ingredients.

**Record:** the three recipes, the side-by-side photos, the questions asked, and the revised versions.

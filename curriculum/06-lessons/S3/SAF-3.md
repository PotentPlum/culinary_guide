**Before you start.** S1 taught the rules of a safe kitchen; S2 applied them to stocks, poultry, meat and fish. S3 adds
processes where those rules are not enough on their own:
- **large batches,** which stay warm for hours;
- **food kept without oxygen:** oil, vacuum bags, jars;
- **food preserved by acid, salt or sugar:** pickles, ferments, cures;
- **food served below the usual core temperatures:** sous vide, raw fish.

In each of them the hazard can't be seen, smelled or tasted, and a mistake can be serious: botulism is rare, and deadly.
That is why this course comes first in the stage. `SLW-3`, `GRD-3`, `PRS-3`, `FIR-3` and `CUI-3` lean on it.

*Two ideas run through it:*
- **some processes need a tested method:** you learn to recognise them, to find the method in a trustworthy source, and
  to refuse a recipe that improvises where it must not;
- **control points:** the idea behind HACCP, the system professional kitchens use. Find the steps where a hazard is
  controlled, name the limit, check it and log it.

*Every figure in this course comes from
[assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)*, which was extended for this stage, each
row with a checked source. Where the Dutch home advice is stricter than the professional rule, the stricter one is
taught. Learn where each figure lives, not only the figure: from here on, when in doubt, you look it up.

Plan about a week. The cooling exercise needs a day: the stock is made, cooled and logged, then checked the next
morning. All sixteen knowledge-check items are safety items, so in practice both checks are passed in full.

**Have ready:** the probe; a fridge-and-freezer thermometer; a stockpot; two shallow containers or trays about 5 cm

### SAF-3.M1.L1 — Cooling, holding and reheating batches

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-3-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `SCI-2.M6.L2` · moves SA-TEMP→4, SA-HRPR→2*

*Objectives:* [S] cool large volumes fast (shallow trays, ice bath, dividing); hold hot and reheat safely
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-3-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a small pan of soup cools in minutes; 4 litres in a deep pot can stay warm for hours. S3 cooks in
batches (stocks, braises, a week's production), so cooling becomes a measured step rather than something that happens
by itself.

**You need:** the stock for `SAF-3.M1.X1`; the probe; two shallow containers; a large bowl or the sink with ice and
water; labels; the cooling-log template; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Why batches are different.* Spores that survive cooking germinate and grow while food sits warm (`SCI-2.M6`). A deep
pot loses heat only through its surface, so its centre stays in that range for a long time. Time in that range is the
hazard.

*The standard* (assessment §9): from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C or below within 6 hours in total. The
first stage is the critical one, because growth is fastest there.

*The methods* (Food Code §3-501.15):
- divide the batch into shallow containers, a few centimetres deep;
- stand the pot in an ice-water bath and stir; top the water up with ice as it melts;
- use ice as an ingredient: after reducing a stock, replace part of its water with ice;
- leave lids off until the food is cold, then cover;
- never put a hot pot in the fridge, which warms everything else in it.

*Logging.* Probe the centre of each container, not the edge, every 30 minutes until 21 °C, then every hour until 5 °C.
Write the time beside each reading. A log of only the end temperature can't show whether the first stage met its
2 hours.

*Holding hot:* at 57 °C or above, checked with the probe, in a low oven or a bain-marie. A pan switched off on the hob
is not holding: it is cooling slowly.

*Reheating:* until steaming hot throughout, and once only. For food that is then held hot, every part reaches 74 °C for
15 seconds within 2 hours (§9). Reheat only what will be eaten: take one bowl's worth out, not the whole batch.

*Storing:* at most 2 days in the fridge at 4 °C, covered, labelled with the contents and the date; freeze what will be
kept longer.

**Practice:**
1. **Cool a batch (2 h active over a day).** Make the 4 litres of stock for `SAF-3.M1.X1`; split it; cool the halves
   two ways and log both. Next morning, check the fridge thermometer, and reheat one portion to 74 °C.

**Look for:** readings logged every 30 minutes, with the time; 21 °C reached within 2 hours; containers shallow and
uncovered while cooling.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Still warm at bedtime | One deep pot in the fridge | Shallow containers or an ice bath |
| Slow cooling, condensation | The lid on while hot | Lids off until cold |
| No proof the limit was met | Logging only the end temperature | Every 30 minutes, with the time |
| A batch reheated again and again | Reheating the whole batch for one bowl | Reheat one portion |

**Safety:** this lesson is the cooling, holding and reheating rule of assessment §9. A batch that misses the 2-hour or
6-hour limit is thrown away, not served.

**Record:** both cooling logs and the reheating reading.

### SAF-3.M1.L2 — Botulism, acidity and oxygen

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-3-m1-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M6.L2` · moves SA-HRPR→3*

*Objectives:* [K] why low-acid, low-oxygen storage is dangerous (garlic in oil, home canning); pH 4.6; [J] only tested methods [S27]
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-3-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** garlic in oil on the counter, or beans canned the way a grandmother did it, look harmless and can
kill. This lesson teaches you to see where the risk hides, and the one rule that handles it: only a tested method.

**You need:** the journal; a list of ten foods to sort; a preserving or flavoured-oil recipe from a magazine or website,
for `SAF-3.M1.X1`.

**Explanation:**

*Clostridium botulinum* forms spores that survive boiling. Where there is no oxygen, low acid, enough moisture and
warmth, they can germinate and make a toxin that kills in tiny amounts. The food can look, smell and taste normal, so
none of your senses will warn you.

*The pH line* (assessment §9, NCHFP [S27]):
- **acid foods,** at pH 4.6 or below: fruit, pickles, sauerkraut, jams;
- **low-acid foods,** above 4.6: meat, fish, poultry, milk, and all fresh vegetables except most tomatoes.

Mixtures are usually low-acid, unless a tested recipe adds enough acid. A jar of pickled onions is acid; a jar of
vegetables in oil is not.

*Where the risk hides at home:*
- **garlic, herbs or vegetables in oil:** no oxygen and low acid. Kept in the fridge, used soon, never kept on the
  counter (§9);
- **home canning of low-acid foods:** only a pressure canner reaches the temperature needed to destroy the spores, and
  the program doesn't teach it. A boiling-water canner is for acid foods only;
- **vacuum bags and sous vide:** no oxygen, so the cook-chill rules apply (`SAF-3.M1.L3`);
- **foil-wrapped baked potatoes left warm:** the foil keeps the air out, and the potato is low-acid.

*The judgment: only a tested method.* A tested recipe comes from a source that measured its acid, its processing time
and its jar size: the NCHFP, or an equivalent authority. "My grandmother did it" is not a test. A canned recipe can't
be changed, because its acid and processing time were measured for exactly those ingredients and that jar.

*Signs of an unsafe recipe:*
- low-acid vegetables or beans in a boiling-water bath;
- "adjust the vinegar to taste" in a canned recipe;
- oven canning, or no processing time at all;
- garlic oil kept in the cupboard.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): sort ten foods into acid and low-acid, with the reason for each; then read the
preserving or oil recipe you found against the four signs above, and note what you find for `SAF-3.M1.X1`.

**Look for:** you can sort ten foods into acid and low-acid, and explain why a canned recipe can't be changed.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A jar kept because it smells fine | Trusting the smell | The toxin has no smell; follow the method |
| A tested pickle made unsafe | "Topping up" a tested pickle with extra vegetables | Follow the recipe exactly |
| A deadly jar | Water-bath canning beans or a vegetable soup | Low-acid foods are not canned at home in the program |
| Toxin in a bottle of oil | Garlic oil on the shelf | In the fridge, used soon |

**Safety:** the acidity and home-canning rows of assessment §9: acid foods only, in a boiling-water canner, by a tested
recipe followed exactly; garlic or herbs in oil kept in the fridge.

**Record:** the sorted list, and your notes on the recipe.

### SAF-3.M1.L3 — Curing salts, fermentation and sous vide rules

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-3-m1-l3 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SAF-3.M1.L2` · moves SA-HRPR→4*

*Objectives:* [K] nitrite dosage, salt % for ferments, time and temperature for pasteurisation; when to throw away
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-3-m1-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** cures, ferments and sous vide each have a figure that keeps them safe. This lesson gathers them in
one place.

**You need:** the journal; assessment §9 open beside it; a label from a pack of curing salt (*nitrietpekelzout*), or its
photo.

**Explanation:**

*Curing salts* (§9). Nitrite stops *C. botulinum* in cured meats that are not cooked through or are kept long
(air-dried, cold-smoked). It is toxic in excess, so:
- it is used only where a tested recipe needs it;
- the added nitrite stays at or below 80 mg per kg of meat (the EU limit, as the nitrite ion);
- the dose is calculated from the nitrite content on the salt's label, and weighed on a 0.01 g scale;
- curing salt is labelled and kept apart from table salt.

The cures of `PRS-3` use plain salt; this rule lets you read a recipe that asks for nitrite.

*Fermentation* (§9). Salt at 2.25–2.5 % of the vegetables' weight, the vegetables under the brine: sauerkraut ferments
in about 3–4 weeks at 21–24 °C. Fuzzy or coloured mould, a slimy texture or a rotten smell means discard. `PRS-3.M3`
practises it.

*Sous vide and low-temperature cooking* (§9):
- poultry follows the poultry table: for example 60 °C for 35 minutes or 65.6 °C for 5 minutes at the core; nothing
  below 60 °C;
- whole meat roasts follow the roast table, and intact beef steaks the steak row; other intact meat and fish reach 63 °C
  for 15 seconds;
- fish below 60 °C at the core is treated as raw (below);
- the time starts when the core reaches the bath temperature, not when the bag goes in;
- food kept for later is cooled by the cooling standard, and kept 2 days or frozen.

*Raw and lightly cooked fish* (§9): frozen first to −20 °C or below in all parts for at least 24 hours (fish sold with
that treatment qualifies), or in a home freezer shown by a thermometer to hold −20 °C or below, for 7 days. A freezer at
−18 °C does not qualify. Salt, sugar and acid do not kill the parasites; heat to 60 °C at the core for 1 minute does.

*Risk groups* (§9): pregnant people, young children, older people and people with weakened immunity are not served raw
or lightly cooked fish, rare meat or soft eggs from raw eggs. Raw-yolk sauces and raw-egg desserts are made with
pasteurised egg for every guest.

*When to throw away:* a bulging lid or a jar that spurts when opened; a broken seal; sous vide food kept beyond 2 days;
a cooling log that missed its limit.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): for each process above, write the figure that controls it and its §9 row; then
calculate the curing salt for 1 kg of meat from the label.

**Look for:** each process with its figure and its source.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Undercooked poultry | Starting the sous vide timer when the bag goes in | Time from when the core reaches the bath |
| Chicken held too low | Chicken at 57 °C "because it's on the internet" | The poultry table; nothing below 60 °C |
| A toxic or useless cure | Curing salt guessed by the spoon | Calculate from the label; weigh to 0.01 g |
| Parasites in a raw dish | Ceviche from fresh, unfrozen wild fish | Frozen first, as §9 says |

**Safety:** the assessment §9 rows named above.

**Record:** the table of processes, figures and rows; the curing-salt calculation.

**Framework:** cooling a batch.

| Part | What it is | Your decisions |
|---|---|---|
| The batch | 4 litres of stock, strained at the boil | — |
| Split | Two equal halves by weight | — |

### SAF-3.M2.L1 — Critical control points

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-3-m2-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAF-3.M1.L1` · moves SA-HYG→5*

*Objectives:* [S] find the hazards and control points in a three-course menu; [K] the HACCP idea
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-3-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** cooking for guests means many steps, and a few of them decide whether anyone gets ill. A
control-point plan picks those few out in advance, so you check them while you cook instead of hoping.

**You need:** a three-course menu: a cold starter, a poultry main and a custard dessert (for example a beetroot salad
with goat's cheese, a roast chicken with root vegetables, and a *crème caramel*); the model plan; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The HACCP idea,* in three questions for each step of a menu:
1. **What can go wrong here?** A hazard: bacteria growing or surviving, cross-contamination, an allergen, or a physical
   hazard (glass, bone, a blade).
2. **Is this the step that controls it?** If so, it is a control point. What is the limit: a figure from §9, or a rule?
3. **How is it checked and logged, and what happens if the limit is missed?** That is the corrective action.

*The usual control points at home:*
- **receiving and storing:** the fridge at 4 °C, raw protein on the bottom shelf;
- **preparing:** raw protein last, separate boards, hands washed;
- **cooking:** core temperatures, the poultry and roast tables;
- **cooling:** the 2-hour and 6-hour limits;
- **holding and reheating:** 57 °C; 74 °C;
- **allergens:** the guest's dish first, with clean tools;
- **high-risk processes:** the tested method, the log.

*Not every step is a control point.* Peeling carrots isn't; roasting the chicken is. A plan with twenty control points
won't be kept. Aim for the few steps where a hazard is actually stopped.

*Corrective actions* are decided in advance, so there is no debate at the stove: cook longer; cool faster in smaller
containers; discard.

*A plan is only as good as its log.* A tick without a reading is not a log. Write the figure: "chicken thigh
75 °C, 19:10".

*An example row:*

| Step | Hazard | Limit | Check | If missed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roast chicken | Bacteria survive | 75 °C at the thickest part (§9) | Probe, logged | Back in the oven; probe again |

**Practice:**
1. **A plan for a three-course menu (60 min).** List every step of the menu; mark the control points with their limits,
   checks and corrective actions; compare with the model plan and correct yours.

**Look for:** every control point has a figure or a rule, a check and an action; not every step is a control point.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A plan nobody follows | Twenty control points nobody can keep | The few steps that stop a hazard |
| An unreliable limit | A limit with no source | Every figure from §9 |
| Panic when a limit is missed | No corrective action | Decide the action in advance |
| A plan that controls nothing | A plan written after the meal | Write it before you shop |

**Safety:** the plan uses only the figures of assessment §9, and its corrective actions include "discard".

**Record:** the plan, corrected against the model.

### SAF-3.M2.L2 — Equipment hazards

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-saf-3-m2-l2 -->
*1 h · 1 practical session · builds on `HEA-2.M4.L1` · moves SA-PHYS→5*

*Objectives:* [S] mandoline, pressure cooker, blowtorch, deep fryer, immersion circulator: risks and safe use
<!-- END generated:lesson-saf-3-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** S3 brings sharper and hotter tools: the mandoline, the pressure cooker, the blowtorch, the frying
pot and the immersion circulator. Each has one or two habits that prevent nearly all of its accidents.

**You need:** two pieces of equipment you own or will use in S3, for example the mandoline and the pressure cooker;
their manuals; a cut-resistant glove; a vegetable to slice; the phone and a stand for filming; the safety checklist.

**Explanation:**

*Mandoline:*
- always the guard, or a cut-resistant glove;
- stop before the last few centimetres of the vegetable, and use the stub for stock;
- clean the blade with a brush, never a cloth.

*Pressure cooker:*
- as most manuals say, never more than two-thirds full, and half for beans and grains, which foam;
- check the valve before use;
- release the pressure as the manual says, never by forcing the lid;
- replace the gasket when it cracks.

*Blowtorch:* used away from the body and from anything that burns, on a stable surface, the gas closed after use. It is
refilled away from flames and never pointed at a person (§9, tier 3 equipment).

*Deep fryer or frying pot:* as in `HEA-2.M4`: a third full, the thermometer in the oil, a lid ready, never water.

*Immersion circulator:*
- clipped to a stable container on a heat-resistant surface;
- the water level within its marks;
- the cable kept dry;
- unplugged before it is lifted out.

*A safety check before use,* read aloud the first few times:
1. The manual: have I read the part about this use?
2. The setup: is it stable, and assembled as shown?
3. The space around: nothing burnable, no cables, no children within reach.
4. If it fails: what do I do? (Lid on the fryer; the circulator unplugged; the torch closed.)

**Practice:**
1. **Film the setups (45 min).** Set up two pieces of equipment and use each briefly, filming the setup and the use with
   the checklist read aloud.

**Look for:** the guard always on; the valve checked aloud; nothing burnable near the torch.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A cut fingertip | Slicing the last stub of a vegetable | Stop early; the stub goes to stock |
| A scald from steam and food | Forcing a pressure-cooker lid | Release the pressure as the manual says |
| A burn on the hand | A torch pointed towards the hand | Point it away; hold the dish on a stable surface |
| Hot water on the counter | A circulator clipped to a wobbly pan | A stable container, a heat-resistant surface |

**Safety:** the equipment rules above, and the tier 3 rule of assessment §9: tools used only as their maker's
instructions say.

**Record:** the two videos and the ticked checklists.

**Before you start.** Preserving turns a glut into a pantry: late-summer cucumbers into pickles, autumn cabbage into
sauerkraut, plums into jam, duck legs into confit. It is also where a home cook's mistake can be most dangerous, because
the worst hazard, botulism, cannot be seen, smelled or tasted. So this course works by one rule: **every method is a
tested method**, from a named source, followed exactly where safety depends on it. The course has six parts:
- **principles:** the hurdles behind each method, and how to spot an unsafe recipe;
- **pickling:** quick fridge pickles by ratio, and water-bath canning of an acid pickle by a tested recipe;
- **lacto-fermentation:** sauerkraut at a weighed salt percentage, a mixed ferment, and telling normal from spoiled;
- **salt curing and confit:** a cure by weight, and confit stored safely;
- **drying and sugar preserves:** a low oven, a jam and a chutney;
- **cooking with preserves:** the pantry used, labelled and rotated.

*The figures.* They come from
[assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment):
- the pH line of 4.6 between acid and low-acid foods;
- home canning of acid foods only, in boiling water, by a tested recipe;
- sauerkraut salt at 2.25–2.5 % with its temperatures and times;
- nitrite curing salt only where a tested recipe needs it, at most 80 mg of nitrite per kg of meat;
- garlic and herbs in oil kept in the fridge;
- the fridge at 4 °C, leftovers kept at most 2 days, the cooling standard.

The tested recipes come from the National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP) [S27], named in each lesson. The
Netherlands lies at sea level, so a tested recipe's lowest-altitude processing time is the one to use.

The produce follows the Dutch harvest: cucumbers, beans, courgettes and beetroot in late summer; cabbage, carrots,
onions and pumpkin in autumn; rhubarb and strawberries in June, plums in August, apples and pears in autumn. Plan about
five weeks of active work over two to three months, following the harvest; the ferments and the confit run in the
background.

**Have ready:** a 2-litre preserving jar or crock with a weight (a plate and a water-filled bag will do); preserving
jars with new two-piece lids; a deep pot with a rack or a folded tea towel under the jars; a jar lifter and a wide
funnel; the 1 g scale and, if a tested cure needs curing salt, the 0.01 g scale; a room thermometer; pH strips
(optional); a heavy saucepan; labels and a marker; non-iodised salt; the processing log; the pantry inventory; the
journal.

### PRS-3.M1.L1 — Hurdles and tested methods

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m1-l1 -->
*2 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SAF-3.M1.L2` · moves SA-HRPR→4, SC-MICR→3*

*Objectives:* [K] acid, salt, sugar, drying, fat, cold; [J] recognise an untested or unsafe recipe [S27]
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** every preserve here is safe because a few hurdles together stop the organisms that matter. Knowing
them tells you what you may change in a recipe and what you may not.

**You need:** the hurdle table below; a few preserving recipes; the NCHFP site [S27]; the journal.

**Explanation:**

*The hurdles.* Each method takes away one or more things microbes need:

| Method | Main hurdles | Storage |
|---|---|---|
| Quick pickle | Acid, cold | Fridge |
| Water-bath pickle | Acid (pH 4.6 or below), heat, a sealed jar | Cupboard until opened |
| Lacto-ferment | Salt, then the acid the bacteria make, no air | Cool, then the fridge |
| Salt cure | Salt (less free water), cold | Fridge, then cooked |
| Confit | Cooking, fat (no air), cold | Fridge or freezer |
| Drying | Little free water | Airtight, dry |
| Jam, chutney | Sugar, acid, heat, a sealed jar | Cupboard if processed; fridge once opened |

*Why combinations matter.* One hurdle alone is often not enough; together they hold. Removing one (less vinegar,
sugar or salt) can break the combination, though the jar looks the same.

*What only a tested method can make safe.* *Clostridium botulinum* grows without air in moist, low-acid food, and its
toxin gives no warning. Foods above pH 4.6 are low-acid: meat, fish, milk and all fresh vegetables except most tomatoes
(§9). A boiling-water bath is enough only for acid foods; low-acid foods need pressure canning, which the program does
not teach (§9). So a tested method is needed for:
- anything canned;
- low-acid foods kept without the fridge;
- garlic, herbs or vegetables kept in oil;
- cured meats eaten without cooking.

*Recognising an untested or unsafe recipe.* Ask seven questions:
1. Is the source an authority that tested it (NCHFP or equivalent), or does it name one?
2. Does a canned recipe give a processing time and a jar size?
3. Is a low-acid food being water-bath canned?
4. Does it say "to taste" for vinegar, sugar or salt where they are hurdles?
5. Is something kept in oil at room temperature?
6. Does it use oven canning, open-kettle canning (filled hot and sealed without processing) or a wax seal?
7. Does a cure for meat eaten raw lack a nitrite dose (§9)?



**Practice:** theory only (2 h): for each preserve you plan to make, write its hurdles and whether it needs a tested
method; then read three recipes against the seven questions, as the warm-up for `PRS-3.M1.X1`.

**Look for:** for any preserve, you name its hurdles without the table, and whether it needs a tested recipe.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Grandma always did it this way" | Tradition taken as a test | Ask the seven questions; a habit that worked is not a tested method |
| Halving the sugar in a canned jam | Sugar seen only as taste | Sugar is a hurdle; choose a tested low-sugar recipe, or keep the jam in the fridge |
| Garlic oil in the cupboard | Oil seen as a seal | Oil keeps air out, which botulism likes; keep it in the fridge (§9) |
| Water-bath canning beans | Beans taken for an acid food | Beans are low-acid; pickle them by a tested recipe, or freeze them |

**Safety:** a preserve without a tested method is refused or replaced by a tested one; a doubtful jar is thrown away
unopened (`SAF-3.M1.L3`).

**Record:** the hurdles of each planned preserve, and the tested source you will use for it.

### PRS-3.M2.L1 — Quick pickles

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 1 d · builds on `PRS-3.M1.L1` · moves TS-PRS→2, RD-RATO→3*

*Objectives:* [S] brine ratios of vinegar, water, salt and sugar; [J] vary aromatics
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a quick pickle is the fastest preserve there is, ready in hours, and the easiest way to bring acid
and crunch to a plate. It is also the base on which water-bath canning builds.

**You need:** red onions, pickling cucumbers and one more vegetable in season; white wine or cider vinegar of 5 %
acidity; water; non-iodised salt; sugar; mustard seed, dill, bay, chilli, peppercorns, juniper or fennel seed; clean
jars with lids; a saucepan; the scale; labels; the tasting form.

**Explanation:**

*A quick (fridge) pickle* is vegetables in a hot or cold brine of vinegar, water, salt and sugar, kept in the fridge.
It is not processed, so it is not shelf-stable: the fridge is one of its hurdles.

*The brine.* No NCHFP recipe is written for the fridge, so the program borrows the pickling solution of NCHFP's
*Quick Fresh-Pack Dill Pickles* [S27]: 1.5 quarts of 5 % vinegar to 2 quarts of water, which is **3 parts vinegar to 4
parts water by volume**. That is the floor: more vinegar is allowed, more water never, and vinegar of unknown or lower
acidity is never used (NCHFP: "do not alter vinegar, food, or water proportions") [S27]. Salt and sugar are flavour
here, weighed against the liquid: a starting point is about 4 % salt and 1.5 % sugar, converted from the recipe's half
cup of salt and quarter cup of sugar. For a 700 ml brine: 300 ml vinegar, 400 ml water, 28 g salt, 10 g sugar.

*Varying the aromatics.* Mustard seed, dill, bay, chilli, garlic, peppercorns, juniper or fennel seed; the acid as
wine, cider or rice vinegar of the same 5 %; sugar up for sweet-sour, down for sharp. What changes is flavour; the
vinegar-to-water floor stays.

*The vegetables.* Firm and fresh: cucumbers, beans, carrots, red onions, beetroot, radishes, cauliflower and courgettes
in summer; cabbage and roots in winter. Cut them evenly and pack them tight, so the brine covers everything.

*The method.* Bring the brine to the boil with the salt, sugar and spices. Pack the vegetables into hot-washed jars.
Pour the hot brine over to cover, close the jar, cool it, and put it in the fridge. Red onions are ready in an hour;
cucumbers taste best after a day.

*Jars.* Clean, hot-washed, labelled with the content and the date. A fridge pickle keeps its crunch for some weeks, but
no tested keeping time exists for it; you set a use-by date for quality in your inventory (`PRS-3.M6.L1`).

**Practice:**
1. **Two quick pickles (60 min).** Red onions and cucumbers in a hot brine by the ratio, weighed; tasted after an hour
   and after a day.
2. **A third with varied aromatics (45 min).** A seasonal vegetable with different spices and acid; compared with the
   first two on the form.

**Look for:**
- crisp after a day;
- sour, salt and sweet in balance;
- every jar labelled and dated.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A flat, weak pickle | Vinegar watered down | Never below 3 parts vinegar to 4 parts water; start again |
| Soft vegetables | Cooked in the brine, or old produce | Pour the brine over raw, fresh vegetables |
| A jar you can't place | No date | Label every jar as you fill it |

**Safety:** quick pickles live in the fridge at 4 °C, never in the cupboard. Mustard is an allergen (`SAF-1.M4`): label
a pickle that contains mustard seed.

**Record:** the brine weighed, the three pickles, and the tastings after an hour and a day.

### PRS-3.M2.L2 — Water-bath canning

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m2-l2 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `PRS-3.M2.L1` · moves TS-PRS→3, SA-HRPR→4*

*Objectives:* [S] jars, headspace, processing time, seal check; tested recipes only
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a sealed jar on the shelf has only its acid and its seal between it and botulism, so every step of
the tested recipe matters. Done exactly, it is simple; changed, it is guesswork.

**You need:** the tested recipe of `PRS-3.M2.X2` printed; preserving jars with new two-piece lids; a deep pot with a
rack (or a folded tea towel) so the jars sit off the bottom; a jar lifter; a wide funnel; a ruler for headspace; US
measuring cups and spoons (NCHFP recipes use them); a timer; labels; the processing log.

**Explanation:**

*When.* Only acid foods, at pH 4.6 or below, by a tested recipe and its processing time, followed exactly; low-acid
foods need pressure canning, which the program does not teach (§9).

*The steps of a tested recipe:*
1. jars washed and kept hot;
2. food prepared exactly as written: the cut, the amounts, the vinegar strength;
3. jars filled to the stated **headspace** (the gap between the food and the lid), air bubbles released with a clean
   spatula, rims wiped;
4. lids on, bands finger-tight;
5. processed in boiling water for the stated time, **timed from when the water returns to the boil**, with the jars
   covered by water as the recipe says;
6. lifted out upright and left undisturbed to cool.

Measure in the recipe's US cups and spoons; use its 0–1,000 ft time.

*The seal check (NCHFP)* [S27]. Cool the jars at room temperature for 12 to 24 hours; do not retighten the lids. Then
take off the bands and test:
- press the centre of the lid: if it springs up when you release it, it is not sealed;
- or tap it with a teaspoon: a ringing note means sealed, a dull one not;
- or look across it: a sealed lid curves down.

A jar that didn't seal goes into the fridge and is eaten within several days, or is reprocessed within 24 hours with
the same time.

*Storage (NCHFP)* [S27]: labelled and dated; in a clean, cool, dark, dry place, best at 10–21 °C, never above 35 °C,
near a stove or in sunlight; stored without the bands; used within a year for best quality.

*Spoiled jars.* A bulging lid, a broken seal, spurting liquid, mould or an odd smell: **do not taste it** (NCHFP).
Throw the jar away unopened, sealed in a heavy bin bag (`SAF-3.M1.L3`).

*Not recommended (NCHFP)* [S27]: open-kettle canning (filled hot, sealed, not processed), and jars processed in an
oven, microwave or dishwasher. Nor does the program use wax seals.

**Practice:**
1. **A canned pickle (2 h).** The pickled beetroot of `PRS-3.M2.X2`, processed by its tested recipe; the seals checked
   and logged the next day.

**Look for:**
- the headspace measured, the time counted from the return to the boil;
- every lid sealed and curved down after a day.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Under-processed jars | Timer started when the jars went in | Start it when the water boils again |
| Lids that don't seal | Old flat lids reused, or a rim not wiped | New lids every time; wipe each rim |
| Jars that leak or don't seal | Extra vegetables packed in | Fill to the stated headspace, no more |
| Sickness risk | Tasting from a bulging jar | Never taste it; throw it away sealed |

**Safety:** acid foods and tested recipes only; hot jars moved upright with the lifter; a doubtful jar never tasted.

**Record:** the processing log: recipe and URL, headspace, the time from the return to the boil, and the seal check.

### PRS-3.M3.L1 — Sauerkraut

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 wk · builds on `SCI-2.M6.L1` · moves TS-PRS→3*

*Objectives:* [S] salt by weight (2.25–2.5 %), packing, weighting, temperature; [K] the bacterial succession
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** sauerkraut is the Dutch preserve of the cabbage harvest, and the clearest example of a ferment: you
add only salt, and the bacteria make the acid that keeps it. Get the salt and the temperature right, and the rest
follows.

**You need:** about 2 kg of white cabbage (autumn); non-iodised salt; a 2-litre preserving jar or crock; a weight (a
plate and a water-filled bag, or glass weights); a large bowl; the 1 g scale; a room thermometer; the fermentation log.

**Explanation:**

*Salt by weight* (§9, [S36]): **2.25–2.5 % of the shredded cabbage's weight**, weighed, never measured by the spoon.
For 2,000 g of cabbage, that is 45–50 g of salt. Too little salt softens the kraut; too much slows the fermentation and
can turn it pink. Use non-iodised salt.

*Packing and weighting.* Remove the outer leaves and the core; shred the cabbage finely and weigh it. Mix it with the
salt and leave it for 10 minutes, then squeeze and press it hard, in handfuls, into the jar, until its own juice rises
and covers it. Keep it under the brine with a weight. Air is kept out: the cabbage stays submerged.

*Temperature and time* (§9, [S27]):
- at 21–24 °C, about 3–4 weeks;
- at 16–18 °C, 5–6 weeks;
- below about 16 °C it may not ferment; above about 24 °C it can turn soft.

Put the jar where the room thermometer shows the right range: not beside the heating. A dish under the jar catches any
overflow.

*The bacterial succession.* First, salt-tolerant bacteria (*Leuconostoc*) make gas and some acid. As the acid builds,
*Lactobacillus* species take over and bring the pH lower. The acid and the salt together keep spoilage organisms and
pathogens out.

*Reading it daily.* Look at the brine level (topped up if it falls), the bubbling, the surface and the smell, and log
each. Bubbles appear in the first days; the smell turns cleanly sour.

*Done.* The bubbling has stopped and the flavour is pleasantly sour. Fully fermented kraut keeps for several months in
the fridge, tightly covered [S27].

**Practice:**
1. **Make it (60 min, then weeks).** The sauerkraut of `PRS-3.M3.X1`: weighed, salted, packed and weighted; the log
   started with the cabbage weight, the salt weight and the room temperature.

**Look for:**
- brine above the cabbage;
- bubbles in the first days;
- a clean, sour smell.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Soft, mushy kraut | Too little salt, or too warm | Weigh the salt; move the jar to 21–24 °C or lower |
| Cabbage above the brine | Not pressed, no weight | Press harder; weigh it down; add brine at the same salt % |
| Slow or no fermentation | Too cold, below about 16 °C | Move it somewhere warmer within the range |

**Safety:** the salt is weighed to the §9 percentage; the cabbage stays under the brine; mould means the ferment is
thrown away (`PRS-3.M3.L2`).

**Record:** the cabbage and salt weights, the salt %, the room temperature, and the daily log.

### PRS-3.M3.L2 — Fermented vegetables and troubleshooting

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 2 wk · builds on `PRS-3.M3.L1` · moves TS-PRS→3, JD-CUES→4*

*Objectives:* [S] a kimchi-style mixed ferment; [J] kahm yeast versus mould; when to discard
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a ferment changes daily, and most of what you see is normal. Telling normal from spoiled, and kahm
yeast from mould, saves good ferments and throws away the dangerous ones.

**You need:** a Chinese or savoy cabbage; radishes or carrots; spring onions; garlic; ginger; chilli flakes; fish sauce
or soy sauce; non-iodised salt; a jar with a weight; gloves for the chilli; the scale; the log; eight ferment photos
(from the course's photo set or your own).

**Explanation:**

*A kimchi-style mixed ferment.* Salt the cabbage pieces by weight and leave them until limp; rinse and drain them. Mix
them with a paste of chilli, garlic, ginger and spring onion, and fish sauce or soy sauce, and with carrots or radishes.
Pack it tightly into the jar, pressed under its own liquid. Leave it at room temperature until it bubbles and smells
sour, logging it daily, then keep it in the fridge. Dutch cabbage, radishes and spring onions suit it. It has no tested
NCHFP recipe, so it is a fridge ferment, not a cupboard one.

*Brine ferments.* Whole or cut vegetables (cucumbers, carrots, beans) are fermented in a weighed brine, at the brine
strength a tested recipe gives [S27], kept under it.

*Normal or spoiled:*
- **normal:** bubbling, cloudy brine, a sour smell, a little white sediment at the bottom;
- **kahm yeast:** a flat, white, wrinkled film on the surface. Harmless but off-tasting: skim it off; the vegetables
  under the brine are fine;
- **mould:** fuzzy, raised spots, green, black, pink or blue. **Discard the whole ferment**: never scrape it off;
- **spoiled:** slimy or soft vegetables, a rotten or putrid smell, pink or dark discoloured kraut. **Discard it.**

*When in doubt, throw it out.* Then change the salt, the weight or the temperature next time, and note why.

**Practice:**
1. **A mixed ferment (60 min, then two weeks).** A kimchi-style ferment packed and logged daily.
2. **Diagnose (30 min).** Eight ferment photos (normal, kahm yeast, mould, spoiled), each judged and the action written.

**Look for:**
- you name kahm yeast and mould from photos, and say what to do;
- the log shows the day the ferment went into the fridge, and why.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Mould eaten | Scraped off "and the rest is fine" | Throw away the whole ferment |
| A good ferment wasted | A little kahm yeast taken for mould | Flat and white is yeast: skim it |
| Risk of illness | Tasting a slimy ferment "to check" | Slimy or putrid is thrown away untasted |

**Safety:** fish sauce is a fish allergen, soy a soy allergen, and chilli is handled with gloves (`SAF-1.M4`); label the
jar. A doubtful ferment is never tasted.

## PRS-3.M4 — Salt curing and confit

**Knowledge check:** the 8 items of `PRS-3.M4` in the [course file](../../05-courses/S3/PRS-3.md), 5 of them safety
items. Pass mark 80 %, with every safety item correct.

### PRS-3.M4.L1 — Salt curing

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m4-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 wk · builds on `SAF-3.M1.L3` · moves TS-PRS→3*

*Objectives:* [S] equilibrium curing of a duck breast or pork belly; [K] when nitrite is needed and the dosage
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a cure turns pork belly into your own bacon, seasoned through. Curing by weight makes it
repeatable; knowing when nitrite is needed keeps it safe.

**You need:** a piece of pork belly of about 1 kg, skin on or off; non-iodised salt; sugar; black pepper, juniper, bay
and thyme; a freezer bag or a covered container that fits it; the 1 g scale; the curing log; labels.

**Explanation:**

*Equilibrium curing.* Weigh the meat; weigh the salt and sugar as a percentage of that weight; seal them with the meat
in a bag in the fridge. The meat takes up all of that salt and no more, so given enough time it reaches the same salt
level throughout and can never be over-salted. A starting point is **2.5 % salt and 1 % sugar** by the meat's weight:
for a 1,000 g belly, 25 g salt and 10 g sugar. This is a quality choice, not a safety figure: the safety comes from the
fridge and the cooking. Log the percentage you use and adjust it to taste next time.

*The cure.* Rub the salt, sugar and crushed aromatics over the meat; seal it in the bag with the air pressed out. Keep
it in the fridge at 4 °C for about a week, turning it daily, so the liquid that forms reaches every side. The belly
is ready when it feels firm throughout. Rinse it, dry it, and cook it. A duck breast can be cured the same way and then
cooked.

*Cooking it.* The program's cures are cooked before eating:
- pork belly as intact meat to 63 °C for 15 seconds at the core (§9), whether roasted or sliced and fried;
- a cured duck breast as poultry, to 75 °C (§9).

Uncooked, it stays in the fridge for the cure time; cooked, it keeps as leftovers (2 days) or is frozen.

*When nitrite is needed, and the dose* (§9, `SAF-3.M1.L3`):
- **needed** for meat eaten without cooking or kept long: air-dried hams, cold-smoked meat, fermented sausages;
- **not needed** for the program's cures, which are cooked before eating and kept in the fridge;
- where a tested recipe calls for nitrite curing salt, the added nitrite stays at **80 mg per kg of meat or below**,
  as the nitrite ion, calculated from the label and weighed on a 0.01 g scale.

An example label: 0.5 % sodium nitrite. Sodium nitrite is about two-thirds nitrite ion by weight (46 of 69), so 80 mg of
nitrite ion is 120 mg of sodium nitrite: at most 24 g of that salt per kg of meat. Your own label changes the
arithmetic.

**Practice:**
1. **A cure by weight (45 min, then about a week).** The pork belly weighed, the cure weighed by percentage and logged,
   turned daily, and cooked at the end of the cure time.

**Look for:** every weight in the log, with the percentage; the meat firm and evenly seasoned when cooked.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too salty or uneven | A cure by eye | Weigh the meat and the cure; log the % |
| An unneeded additive | Curing salt where none is needed | Plain salt for a cooked cure |
| Raw cured meat eaten | Taking the cure for cooking | Cook it to its §9 figure first |

**Safety:** a cured belly is raw meat: stored in the fridge on the bottom shelf, cooked to its §9 figure, its board and
knife treated as raw meat's.

**Record:** the meat weight, the cure weights and %, the days, and the cooked result.

### PRS-3.M4.L2 — Confit

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m4-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 d · builds on `SAF-3.M1.L2` · moves TS-PRS→3*

*Objectives:* [S] duck legs or garlic confit; [K] safe storage in fat (refrigerated, limited time)
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** confit is cooking slowly under fat, the old way to keep duck legs. The cooking still works; the
keeping does not, without the fridge. This lesson keeps the first and changes the second.

**You need:** two heads of garlic; a neutral oil or duck fat; a small saucepan; a clean jar; labels; the probe. For the
explanation: the duck legs of `BUT-3.M1.X1` (cooked in `PRS-3.M4.X1`).

**Explanation:**

*Duck confit.* Salt the legs by weight (the starting point of `PRS-3.M4.L1`), overnight in the fridge. Rinse and dry
them; submerge them in duck fat; cook them in a low oven, the fat barely trembling, until a skewer slides into the
thigh with no resistance. The core passes 75 °C (the poultry row of §9) long before the meat is tender. Cool them in
the fat.

*Garlic confit.* Peel the cloves; cover them with oil in a small pan; cook them very gently, with only small bubbles,
until they are soft and golden, not brown. Garlic in oil is a botulism risk (§9): keep it in the fridge and use it
within 2 days, or freeze it.

*Safe storage in fat:*
- fat keeps out air, and *C. botulinum* grows without air, so the fat is not a safety hurdle on its own;
- confit is cooled by the standard (57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C or below within 6 hours in total, §9),
  kept covered in its fat in the fridge, and used within 2 days, or frozen in its fat for longer;
- the traditional months under fat in a cellar have no checked figure and are not taught;
- the strained fat is reused for potatoes and further confit, kept in the fridge.

*Serving.* Crisp the legs skin-side down in a hot pan or oven until they are steaming hot throughout (§9). Serve them
with potatoes cooked in the fat and something sharp: a pickle, a chicory salad.

**Practice:**
1. **Garlic confit (30 min active, 1 h in total).** A small batch, cooled, labelled and refrigerated; used within
   2 days, on toast, in a dressing or mashed into potatoes.

**Look for:**
- the garlic soft and golden, not brown;
- the oil never more than gently bubbling;
- the jar dated and in the fridge.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Garlic confit on the shelf | Oil seen as a seal | In the fridge, within 2 days, or frozen |
| Confit kept for weeks in the fridge | The old cellar rule followed | 2 days in the fridge; freeze it for longer |
| Bitter, brown garlic | The oil too hot | Lower the heat: small bubbles only |

**Safety:** hot fat and oil are handled with dry tools and the pan kept stable; garlic in oil and confit are always
refrigerated or frozen (§9).

**Record:** the garlic batch, the date made and the day used.

### PRS-3.M5.L1 — Drying

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m5-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `SCI-2.M5.L1` · moves TS-PRS→3*

*Objectives:* [S] herbs, tomatoes, mushrooms and fruit in a low oven; [K] water activity and storage
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** drying is the oldest hurdle and needs no additives: take away the water and microbes cannot grow. A
home oven does it slowly; the skill is in knowing when the food is dry, and keeping it that way.

**You need:** woody herbs (thyme, rosemary, sage); ripe Dutch tomatoes (late summer); mushrooms; apples or pears; a
lemon or vinegar for acidulated water; oven racks or wire racks on trays; a wooden spoon to hold the door ajar; clean,
dry jars; the scale; labels; the log.

**Explanation:**

*Water activity.* Microbes need free water; drying removes it until they cannot grow. The food is dry when it is
leathery or brittle, by type, with no moist centre.

*The low oven.* The lowest setting, often 50–70 °C, with the door slightly ajar so the moisture can escape; the food in
a single layer on a rack, with air around every piece. A dehydrator does the same job; NCHFP describes dehydrators as
designed to dry foods fast at 60 °C (140 °F) [S27]. An oven that runs hotter cooks the food instead of drying it.

*What dries well at home:*
- **herbs,** woody ones best: air-dried in bunches or on a rack, or in the oven at its lowest; dry when they crumble;
- **tomatoes,** halved and seasoned: leathery, with no moist centre;
- **mushrooms,** sliced: dried until brittle;
- **apple and pear rings,** dipped in acidulated water against browning: leathery and pliable.

*When it is dry (NCHFP)* [S27]. Vegetables are dried until they are brittle or crisp. Fruit stays leathery, with about
20 % moisture, so it is **conditioned**: cooled, packed loosely in jars, sealed and left for 7 to 10 days, shaken
daily. If condensation appears, the fruit goes back for more drying.

*Storage (NCHFP)* [S27]. Cooled completely first, then in clean, dry jars or freezer containers with tight lids, in a
cool, dark, dry place, labelled. Most dried fruit keeps about a year stored cool, about half that stored warm;
vegetables keep about half as long as fruit. Dried tomatoes or herbs put into oil become an oil preserve: in the fridge,
and used within 2 days (§9).

*Weighing.* Weigh the food before and after: the loss is the water removed, and the record tells you next time how far
to take it.

**Practice:**
1. **A drying session (90 min active, a day in total).** Herbs, tomatoes and apple rings, weighed before and after;
   stored, and checked after three days for condensation.

**Look for:**
- no moisture in the centre when a piece is torn;
- no condensation in the jar after three days.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Cooked, browned food | The oven too hot, the door shut | The lowest setting, the door ajar |
| Condensation in the jar | Packed warm, or not dry enough | Cool completely; back into the oven |
| Dried tomatoes in oil on the shelf | Oil seen as a seal | In the fridge, within 2 days |

**Safety:** a door held ajar leaves a hot oven open: keep children and tea towels away, and switch the oven off when you
leave the house. Dried food in oil is kept in the fridge (§9).

**Record:** the weights before and after, the oven setting and time, and the condensation check.

### PRS-3.M5.L2 — Jams, marmalade and chutney

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-prs-3-m5-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M5.L1` · moves TS-PRS→3, IN-VEG→3*

*Objectives:* [S] sugar, pectin, acid and the set test; a seasonal chutney; [K] fruit and vegetable pectin
<!-- END generated:lesson-prs-3-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** in jam, sugar is a hurdle and a texture at once; in chutney, vegetables meet vinegar and sugar.
Both use the gluts of the Dutch summer and autumn.

**You need:** seasonal fruit (plums in August, apples in autumn); powdered pectin if the tested recipe needs it; sugar;
cider vinegar of 5 %; onions; spices; a wide, heavy pan; saucers in the freezer; the thermometer; preserving jars with
new lids; the deep pot with a rack; US measuring cups; labels; red onions for the comparison.

**Explanation:**

*How a jam sets.* Pectin from the fruit, sugar and acid together form a gel when the mixture is boiled to the setting
point. High-pectin fruit (apples, plums, redcurrants, citrus peel) sets easily; low-pectin fruit (strawberries,
rhubarb, pears) needs added pectin, lemon juice, or a high-pectin fruit mixed in.

*Vegetable pectin.* Vegetables have pectin too, which is why a chutney thickens as it reduces. Acid keeps vegetables
firm and sets their colour: red onion and red cabbage turn bright pink with acid (`VEG-1.M1` compared carrots in acid
and alkali).

*The set test.*
- **with added pectin:** the tested recipe's boil (for NCHFP's plum jam, a full rolling boil, then a hard boil for one
  minute after the sugar) is the set; follow it exactly;
- **without added pectin:** a spoonful on a saucer from the freezer wrinkles when pushed after a few minutes; or the
  temperature the tested recipe gives, on the thermometer.

*Sugar is a hurdle* (`PRS-3.M1`). A full-sugar jam by a tested recipe, water-bath processed, keeps in the cupboard until
opened; opened, it goes in the fridge and is best within a month (NCHFP) [S27]. Sweeteners are not substituted for the
sugar in a regular-pectin recipe; low-sugar spreads use a tested low-sugar recipe and its pectin, or are kept in the
fridge or freezer.

*Marmalade.* Citrus peel, pith and juice cooked long; the peel's pectin sets it. Citrus is imported, so marmalade is
taught as a method and is optional, with Seville oranges in January.

*Chutney.* Fruit or vegetables with vinegar, sugar and spices, cooked down until a spoon drawn across the pan leaves a
channel. It mellows after a few weeks. It is canned only by a tested recipe, otherwise kept in the fridge.

*Seasonal choices.* Rhubarb and strawberry in June, plums in August, apples and pears in autumn; pumpkin, beetroot, red
onion or green tomato chutney in autumn, in the fridge unless a tested recipe exists.

**Practice:**
1. **A jam (75 min).** The plum jam of `PRS-3.M5.X1` by its tested recipe; the set checked on a cold saucer; jarred and
   processed.
2. **A chutney (90 min).** The apple chutney of `PRS-3.M5.X1`, cooked to a channel and jarred; a red onion cooked with
   and without vinegar, photographed side by side for colour.

**Look for:**
- a set that wrinkles on the cold saucer;
- a chutney that holds a channel;
- every jar labelled.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A stiff, dark jam | Boiled past the set | Follow the tested boil; test early |
| A jam that may spoil | Sugar halved in a canned recipe | A tested low-sugar recipe, or the fridge |
| A wet chutney | Not reduced enough | Cook on until a spoon leaves a channel |

**Safety:** boiling jam is hotter than boiling water and sticks to skin: a deep pan, long sleeves, a long spoon, and
children away. Mustard seed is an allergen (`SAF-1.M4`): label the chutney.

**Record:** the set test, the processing log, and the onion photos.

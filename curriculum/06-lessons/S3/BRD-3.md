**Before you start.** `BRD-2` baked lean and enriched yeast breads with commercial yeast, a first shaped loaf in a Dutch
oven, and a bread log. This course adds what makes bread a lifelong craft: **time and culture**.
- **preferments** (*poolish*, *biga*) for flavour and a flexible schedule, and wetter doughs;
- **a sourdough culture:** started, kept, read and used for a first sourdough loaf;
- **shaping and scoring** of high-hydration dough, for tension and a controlled opening (an "ear");
- **whole grain and rye,** which behave differently from white wheat;
- **a schedule around a working week,** and diagnosing loaves from the crumb.

The method follows Hamelman's *Bread* [S13]: formulas in baker's percentages, dough temperatures logged, each loaf
judged by its crumb. Flour from a Dutch mill (*molenmeel*, stone-ground wholemeal, rye) suits the course and is widely
sold; Dutch *roggebrood* is the country's rye tradition.

Plan about eight weeks. The starter takes about two weeks to become reliable, and the last module is four weeks of
baking for the household alongside other courses.

**Have ready:** the heavy lidded casserole (*braadpan*) as a Dutch oven; a proofing basket; a lame or a razor blade; a
bench scraper; a 1-litre glass jar with a loose lid and a rubber band; a room thermometer; the instant-read thermometer
for the dough; a loaf tin (a heavy one for rye); a dough container; the 1 g scale; dry oven gloves; rice flour for the
basket; the bread log.

### BRD-3.M1.L1 — *Poolish* and *biga*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m1-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 16 h · builds on `BRD-2.M2.L2` · moves TS-BRD→3*

*Objectives:* [K] flavour and scheduling; [S] a *poolish* bread
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a preferment costs fifteen minutes the evening before and gives a loaf more flavour, a more open
crumb and a longer keeping time. It also splits the work over two days, which makes good bread fit a busy week.

**You need:** bread flour from a Dutch mill; water; salt; instant yeast; two bowls with covers; the 1 g scale and a
0.1 g scale for the yeast; the thermometer; the Dutch oven; the bread log.

**Explanation:**

*A preferment* is part of the flour and water, with a little yeast, mixed hours before the final dough:
- ***poolish*:** equal weights of flour and water (100 % hydration), a pinch of yeast, 12–16 hours at room temperature.
  It gives an extensible dough and a creamy, slightly nutty flavour;
- ***biga*:** a stiff preferment, about 50–60 % hydration, more yeast-forward, for a stronger dough;
- **ready at its peak:** domed, bubbly, and just beginning to recede at the centre.

*Flavour.* The long, slow fermentation makes organic acids and aromatic compounds that a straight dough has no time to
make. The bread also keeps longer.

*Scheduling.* Make the preferment in the evening and mix the final dough the next morning. Reduce the yeast in the final
dough, because the preferment already brings active yeast. The timing moves with the room's temperature.

*Baker's percentages* (`BRD-2`). Count the preferment's flour and water in the totals, so you know the overall
hydration. A worked example for one loaf, flour 100 %:

| Ingredient | Total | In the *poolish* | In the final dough |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour (100 %) | 500 g | 150 g | 350 g |
| Water (70 %) | 350 g | 150 g | 200 g |
| Salt (2 %) | 10 g | — | 10 g |
| Instant yeast | about 2 g | 0.1 g (a pinch) | 2 g |

**Practice:**
1. **The *poolish* (15 min in the evening).** Mixed, covered, left at room temperature; its peak photographed in the
   morning.
2. **The bread (2 h active over the day).** The final dough mixed from the *poolish*, fermented, shaped and baked; a
   straight dough of the same totals baked alongside for comparison.

**Look for:**
- the *poolish* domed and bubbly at its peak;
- the *poolish* loaf more aromatic, with a more open crumb, than the straight dough.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A collapsed *poolish* that smells of alcohol | Over-fermented | Less yeast, or a cooler spot; use it earlier |
| A wetter or drier dough than planned | The preferment's flour and water not counted | Count them in the totals |
| A dough that over-proofs | The same yeast as a straight dough | Reduce the final dough's yeast |

**Safety:** none beyond the kitchen basics; the Dutch oven is handled as in `BRD-3.M2.L3`.

**Record:** the formula with the preferment counted, the times and temperatures, and crumb photos of both loaves.

### BRD-3.M1.L2 — Hydration

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m1-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M1.L1` · moves TS-BRD→3, RD-RATO→3*

*Objectives:* [S] handle 70–80 % hydration doughs; [K] hydration by flour type
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** the open, glossy crumb of a good artisan loaf comes from water, and a wet dough is handled with
technique rather than more flour. Learning that changes what you can bake.

**You need:** bread flour; water; salt; instant yeast; a dough container; wet hands; a bench scraper; the thermometer;
the bread log.

**Explanation:**

*Hydration* is water as a percentage of the flour's weight. More water gives a more open, irregular crumb and a thinner,
crisper crust, but a stickier dough.

*By flour type.* Stronger white flour takes more water; wholemeal and rye absorb much more (bran and pentosans); a weak
or old flour takes less. The same hydration feels different from one flour to the next, so you judge the feel as well
as the number.

*Handling wet dough (70–80 %):*
- **wet hands and a bench scraper,** instead of extra flour;
- **stretch-and-folds or coil folds** in the container instead of kneading: lift the dough from one side, fold it over,
  turn the container, repeat; three or four sets, 30 minutes apart;
- **an autolyse:** the flour and water mixed and rested for 30–60 minutes before the salt and yeast, for extensibility;
- **bassinage:** some of the water held back and added late, once the gluten has formed.

*Dough temperature.* Aim for about 24–26 °C, set by the water's temperature, measured and logged: it sets the pace of
the fermentation. Hamelman's method calculates the water temperature from the dough temperature you want and the
temperatures of the room and the flour [S13]. In a cool Dutch kitchen in winter, that often means warm water.

**Practice:**
1. **Two hydrations (75 min).** The same formula at 68 and 76 % hydration, mixed with folds, logged and baked.
2. **A high-hydration loaf (75 min).** A 78 % dough handled with an autolyse and coil folds; baked; crumb photographed.

**Look for:**
- a smooth, extensible dough after the folds;
- the dough temperature in range;
- a more open crumb at the higher hydration.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dry, tight dough and crumb | The bench floured heavily | Wet hands and a scraper instead |
| A torn, sticky mess | A wet dough kneaded on the bench | Folds in the container |
| A fermentation that runs fast or slow | Water at any temperature | Calculate the water temperature; log the dough's |

**Safety:** none beyond the kitchen basics.

**Record:** both hydrations, the dough temperatures, the folds, and the crumb photos side by side.

### BRD-3.M2.L1 — Starting and keeping a starter

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 wk · builds on `BRD-2.M1.L1` · moves SC-MICR→3*

*Objectives:* [S] feed ratios, temperature, storage; [K] wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a starter is a living culture you keep for years. Starting one takes two weeks of a few minutes a
day; keeping it takes a feed a week.

**You need:** wholemeal wheat or rye flour (stone-ground, from a Dutch mill if possible); water; a 1-litre glass jar
with a loose lid; a rubber band; a spoon; the 1 g scale; a room thermometer; the starter log.

**Explanation:**

*What lives in a starter.* Wild yeasts, which raise the bread, and lactic acid bacteria, which sour it and make flavour,
in a stable community. Their acid lowers the pH, which keeps unwanted organisms out (`SCI-2.M6`).

*Starting one.* Mix 50 g wholemeal or rye flour with 50 g water in the jar. Each day, discard all but 50 g and feed it
with 50 g flour and 50 g water. After a few days of erratic activity, often a burst of bubbles and then nothing, it
settles into a predictable rise and fall. It is usually reliable within about two weeks.

*Feeding ratios.* Starter, flour and water by weight: for example 1 : 1 : 1, or 1 : 5 : 5 for a slower rise. A stiffer
or colder starter is more sour and slower.

*Temperature.* Warm, about 24–26 °C, for activity. Between bakes, keep it in the fridge and feed it once a week.

*Storage and hygiene:*
- a clean jar with a loose lid, never sealed: the gas must escape;
- the level marked with the rubber band after each feed, so you can see the rise;
- the discard used in pancakes or crackers;
- a pink or orange tint, or fuzzy mould, means the starter is thrown away and started again.

**Practice:**
1. **Start it (20 min, then daily for two weeks).** The starter mixed and fed; the log of `BRD-3.M2.X2` begun.

**Look for:**
- a predictable doubling within 4–8 hours of feeding;
- a pleasant sour, yoghurty smell.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "It's dead" on day 3 | The early burst has passed | Keep feeding; it settles after a few days |
| A cracked jar or a burst lid | A sealed jar | A loose lid |
| A slow starter | Chlorinated or very cold water, a cold room | Water at room temperature; a warmer spot |

**Safety:** a pink or orange tint, or fuzzy mould, means the whole starter is thrown away, the jar washed, and a new one
started; it is never scraped and kept.

**Record:** the daily log: feed ratio, room temperature, the rise against the band, and the smell.

### BRD-3.M2.L2 — Reading the starter

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m2-l2 -->
*1 h · 1 practical session · builds on `BRD-3.M2.L1` · moves JD-CUES→4*

*Objectives:* [J] peak, smell, bubbles, float test; when it's ready
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a loaf made with a starter at its peak rises; one made with a hungry starter doesn't. Reading the
starter by eye and nose, then checking, is the judgment behind every sourdough bake.

**You need:** your starter, freshly fed; the jar with its rubber band; a glass of water; a spoon; the phone for photos;
the starter log.

**Explanation:**

*The cues of a ready starter:*
- at or just after its peak: domed, risen to about double, full of bubbles through the jar;
- a pleasant sour, fruity smell; a smell of nail varnish means it is hungry;
- a spoonful floats in water: a rough guide, not always reliable for wholemeal or rye starters;
- a web of gluten when you pull a spoonful away.

*Hungry or over-ripe.* Collapsed, a liquid (*hooch*) on top, an acetone smell: feed it again and wait for the next peak
before using it.

*The rise and fall.* After a feed, the starter climbs slowly, then quickly, peaks, and falls back. The peak moves with
temperature and ratio: a warm kitchen and a 1 : 1 : 1 feed bring it in a few hours; a cold kitchen and a 1 : 5 : 5 feed
can take most of a day. That is how you plan a bake around it.

*Cue, then check.* Predict "ready" from the look and the smell, write it down, then check with the band (the rise) and
the float test. Log both. Over two weeks your predictions should close in on the observed peak.

**Practice:**
1. **Read it (60 min over a day).** The starter fed, then photographed and smelled every two hours; the peak predicted,
   then checked; logged.

**Look for:** predictions within an hour of the observed peak.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A flat, dense loaf | Starter used at the bottom of its curve | Use it at or just after its peak |
| A misleading "ready" | Only the float test trusted | Combine rise, smell, bubbles and float |
| Missed peaks | No band on the jar | Mark the level after every feed |

**Safety:** a starter that shows pink, orange or fuzzy mould is thrown away, not read (`BRD-3.M2.L1`).

**Record:** the photos every two hours, the predicted and observed peaks, and the gap between them.

### BRD-3.M2.L3 — A first sourdough loaf

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m2-l3 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 1 d · builds on `BRD-3.M2.L2` · moves TS-BRD→3*

*Objectives:* [S] levain, mix, bulk, shape, retard, bake
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m2-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a sourdough loaf brings the course together: a live culture, a wet dough, judgment of fermentation
by the dough, and an overnight retard that fits the bake to your day.

**You need:** the ripe starter; bread flour and some wholemeal; water; salt; a dough container; a proofing basket dusted
with rice flour; a tea towel; a lame; the Dutch oven; baking paper; dry oven gloves; the thermometer; the bread log.

**Explanation:**

*The stages:*
1. **levain:** some starter fed and left to peak;
2. **mix:** flour and water (an autolyse), then the levain and the salt;
3. **bulk fermentation:** with folds, until the dough has grown by roughly half, is domed and jiggly, with bubbles at
   the sides. The time depends on the dough temperature: often 4–6 hours at 24–26 °C;
4. **pre-shape and shape** with tension (`BRD-3.M3.L1`);
5. **retard:** the shaped loaf in its basket in the fridge overnight, for flavour, for the schedule, and for easier
   scoring;
6. **bake:** cold from the fridge in a preheated Dutch oven, lid on, then off.

*Judging bulk by the dough, not the clock.* Volume (a straight-sided container with a mark helps), a domed surface,
bubbles at the sides and on top, and a jiggle when you shake the container. A recipe's time is a guide for its
temperature; your kitchen decides.

*The Dutch oven.* Preheat it with the lid at about 250 °C. The lid traps the loaf's steam for the first part of the
bake, so the crust stays soft and the loaf can spring and open; the lid comes off for the crust to colour.

*After the bake.* Cool the loaf on a rack for at least an hour before slicing: the crumb is still setting.

**Practice:**
1. **Mix and bulk (90 min active over a day).** Levain, mix, folds, bulk judged by the dough; shaped and retarded.
2. **Bake (60 min the next morning).** Scored and baked; the crumb photographed after cooling.

**Look for:**
- a domed, jiggly dough at the end of bulk;
- a tall loaf with an open, even crumb.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dense or over-proofed loaf | Bulk ended by the clock | Judge by volume, dome, bubbles and jiggle |
| A tight crumb with a few large holes | Under-fermented | Longer bulk, or a warmer dough |
| A gummy crumb | Sliced hot | Cool it for at least an hour |

**Safety:** the Dutch oven at about 250 °C: dry oven gloves on both hands, the lid set down on a heatproof surface, the
loaf lowered in on baking paper, never by hand (`BRD-2.M3`).

**Record:** the fermentation log (dough temperature, folds, bulk time and its cues), and the crumb photo.

### BRD-3.M2.X1 — A first sourdough loaf, baked twice

<!-- END generated:exercise-brd-3-m2-x1 -->

**Task:** bake a sourdough loaf by the guided recipe twice, on separate days, improving the second from the first. Keep
a fermentation log for each (dough temperatures, bulk judged by the dough), shape with tension, score, and retard
overnight. Photograph the crumb for AI review, and correct the second bake from what the first showed.

**You need:** for one loaf: 100 g ripe levain (at 100 % hydration); 450 g bread flour and 50 g wholemeal from a Dutch

**Recipe:** a first sourdough (overall hydration about 75 %).
1. **Levain:** feed the starter the evening before or in the morning; use it at its peak.
2. **Autolyse:** mix the flours with 340 g of the water; rest 30–60 minutes.

7. **Bake:** the Dutch oven preheated at about 250 °C; the loaf turned onto baking paper, scored, lowered in; lid on

### BRD-3.M3.L1 — Advanced shaping

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 3 practical sessions · builds on `BRD-2.M2.L3` · moves TS-BRD→3*

*Objectives:* [S] tension with high-hydration dough; *bâtard* and *boule*
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a wet dough only becomes a tall loaf if it is shaped with tension. Shaping is a movement of the
hands that improves with every bake, so this lesson is about repetition.

**You need:** a batch of dough (the *poolish* or sourdough formula), enough for three loaves, or three single batches
over the week; a bench scraper; a little flour; proofing baskets or bowls lined with a floured tea towel; rice flour;
the phone for filming.

**Explanation:**

*Tension.* A tight skin on the surface of the loaf holds its shape, directs the oven spring upwards, and helps the
scoring open. Without it, the loaf spreads.

*Pre-shape and bench rest.* Divide the dough and round each piece loosely with the scraper. Rest it, uncovered or
lightly covered, for 20–30 minutes, so the gluten relaxes and the final shape doesn't tear.

*The boule:*
1. turn the dough seam up on a lightly floured patch;
2. fold the edges in to the centre, all the way round;
3. flip it seam down onto an unfloured patch of bench;
4. drag it towards you with cupped hands, turning it a little each time, so the surface tightens against the bench.

*The bâtard:*
1. turn the dough seam up and pat it into a rough rectangle;
2. fold the top third down and the bottom third up, like a letter;
3. roll it from the top, pressing in with the thumbs at each turn;
4. seal the seam with the heel of the hand; even, with tapered or blunt ends.

*High hydration.* A lightly floured surface for the first fold and none for the tightening; the bench scraper as a
second hand; quick, confident movements. A well-floured basket (rice flour sticks least), the loaf in seam up.

*Checking tension.* Poke the shaped loaf gently: it should spring back slowly, and the surface should look taut and
smooth, without tears.

**Practice:**
1. ***Boules* (45 min).** Three pre-shaped and shaped; the tension checked by a poke and photographed.
2. ***Bâtards* (45 min).** Three shaped; filmed for AI review.
3. **Wet dough (45 min).** A 78 % dough shaped into both forms; compared.

**Look for:**
- a taut, smooth surface;
- the loaf holds its shape when turned out;
- a sealed seam.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A torn surface | Over-tightened | Stop when it is taut; rest more before shaping |
| The dough slides instead of tightening | Too much flour on the bench | An unfloured patch for the drag |
| A shape that springs back and tears | No bench rest | Rest 20–30 minutes after pre-shaping |

**Safety:** none beyond the kitchen basics.

**Record:** the poke photos, the shaping video, and one thing to change next time.

### BRD-3.M3.L2 — Scoring

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m3-l2 -->
*2 h · 3 practical sessions · builds on `BRD-2.M3.L2` · moves TS-BRD→3*

*Objectives:* [S] blade angle and depth; patterns; [K] why scoring directs the spring
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a loaf will burst somewhere in the oven; scoring decides where. A confident cut at the right angle
gives the "ear" that marks a well-made loaf.

**You need:** three shaped *bâtards* and three *boules* over the module (cold from a retard); a lame or a razor blade;
baking paper; the Dutch oven; dry oven gloves; the phone for photos.

**Explanation:**

*Why scoring directs the spring.* The loaf expands fast in the first minutes of the bake. A cut gives it a planned
place to open, instead of bursting at a weak point.

*The ear.* A single long cut along a *bâtard*, slightly off the centre line:
- the blade held at a shallow angle, about 30° to the surface;
- about 5–10 mm deep;
- in one confident stroke, from end to end.

The flap lifts in the oven and bakes into a crisp ridge.

*Patterns:*
- a cross or a square on a *boule*;
- parallel cuts;
- decorative shallow cuts, plus one deep expansion cut that does the work.

*Conditions.* A cold, retarded loaf is firmer and easier to score. Steam, here the Dutch oven's lid, keeps the surface
soft for the first minutes, so the cut can open before the crust sets.

*Holding the lame.* Lightly, between thumb and fingers, the arm moving rather than the wrist; the other hand steadying
the baking paper, not the loaf.

**Practice:**
1. **Single cut (30 min).** The ear cut on three *bâtards* at different angles; the openings compared.
2. **Patterns (30 min).** Three *boules* scored with different patterns.
3. **Combined (30 min).** The best shape and cut on one bake, photographed.

**Look for:**
- a clean cut in one stroke;
- an even, raised ear;
- the bloom where it was planned.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A ragged, torn cut | The blade sawed | One stroke, a sharp blade |
| No ear | Cut straight down | A shallow angle, about 30° |
| A cut that closes up | Too shallow on a warm, slack loaf | Score cold from the fridge; cut 5–10 mm deep |

**Safety:** a razor blade is mounted on its lame or holder and stored covered; the hot Dutch oven is handled with dry
gloves on both hands (`BRD-3.M2.L3`).

**Record:** photos of each opening, labelled with the angle and pattern.

## BRD-3.M4 — Whole grain and rye

**Knowledge check:** the 8 items of `BRD-3.M4` in the [course file](../../05-courses/S3/BRD-3.md). Pass mark 80 %.

### BRD-3.M4.L1 — Whole wheat

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m4-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 d · builds on `SCI-2.M3.L2` · moves SC-STRC→4*

*Objectives:* [K] bran and water absorption; [S] a whole wheat loaf
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** wholemeal bread tastes of the grain, but it doesn't behave like white bread. Treated on its own
terms, with more water and gentler handling, it is moist and well risen rather than heavy.

**You need:** fresh stone-ground wholemeal wheat flour from a Dutch mill; water; salt; levain or a little yeast; a
container; a loaf tin or the Dutch oven; the thermometer; the bread log.

**Explanation:**

*Bran and water.* The bran and germ absorb much more water than white flour, and the bran's sharp edges cut gluten
strands. So a wholemeal dough needs:
- **more water:** often 80 % or more, where white bread takes 65–75 %;
- **a longer autolyse,** or a soaker (the flour and water mixed hours ahead), so the bran softens;
- **gentle handling:** folds rather than long kneading.

*Flavour.* Nutty and fuller, and it pairs well with a preferment or sourdough. The germ's oils make the flour go stale
and rancid faster, so buy it fresh, in small amounts, and keep it cool and airtight. Smell it before use: rancid flour
smells bitter, like old nuts or paint.

*Expectations.* A denser, less open crumb than white bread. Good wholemeal bread is moist and well risen, not heavy or
crumbly.

*Blends.* A share of wholemeal, 20–50 %, in a white loaf is a step up, and a good weekday bread. Raise the water with
the share of wholemeal, and judge the dough by its feel: it should be soft and slightly sticky, never stiff.

*Fermentation.* Bran brings nutrients that speed the yeast and the bacteria, so a wholemeal dough often ferments
faster than a white one at the same temperature. Watch the dough, not the clock.

**Practice:**
1. **A wholemeal loaf (75 min active, 1 day in total).** 100 % wholemeal with a long autolyse at a higher hydration;
   baked; the crumb photographed.

**Look for:**
- a moist, risen crumb;
- not crumbly, not gummy.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dry, dense loaf | White-bread hydration | More water; a longer autolyse |
| A tight, tough loaf | Over-kneaded | Folds, not long kneading |
| A bitter taste | Rancid flour from the back of the cupboard | Buy fresh; store cool; smell before use |

**Safety:** none beyond the kitchen basics.

**Record:** the hydration, the autolyse time, and the crumb photo.

### BRD-3.M4.L2 — Rye

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m4-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 d · builds on `BRD-3.M2.L3` · moves TS-BRD→3*

*Objectives:* [K] pentosans and acidity; [S] a rye bread with different handling
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** rye is the bread of the Dutch and northern European tradition, and it follows different rules from
wheat. Once you work with them, a dense, moist, keeping rye loaf is one of the easiest breads to bake well.

**You need:** rye flour (wholemeal rye, *roggemeel*); a rye starter (your starter fed with rye); water; salt; seeds if
you like (sunflower, linseed); a heavy loaf tin, greased; a wet spatula; the bread log.

**Explanation:**

*Pentosans and acidity.* Rye has little gluten-forming protein. Its structure comes from pentosans, gums that hold water
and make a sticky paste. Rye flour is also rich in enzymes that break down starch, so without acid the crumb turns
gummy. A sourdough's acidity tames them, which is why rye breads are traditionally sourdough breads.

*Handling.* A paste rather than a dough: mixed, not kneaded; shaped with wet hands or simply spread into a tin with a
wet spatula; scored little or not at all. Don't look for gluten development: there won't be any.

*A starting formula.* Rye flour 100 %, with about 40 % of it pre-fermented as a rye levain; water about 80 %; salt 2 %;
seeds as you like. Proof in the tin until the surface shows small cracks.

*Baking and resting.* Baked long, and often lower than a wheat loaf. Rest it, wrapped, for a day before slicing, so the
crumb sets. Cut too soon, it is sticky inside.

*Traditions.* Dutch *roggebrood*: dark, dense and long-baked. German and Scandinavian ryes, often with seeds. A *pain de
seigle*-style mixed loaf of rye and wheat is an easier start.

**Practice:**
1. **A rye loaf (75 min active, 2 days in total).** A sourdough rye in a tin, baked, rested a day, sliced and
   photographed.

**Look for:**
- a moist but set crumb after a day;
- no gummy streak at the base.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dense, tough loaf | Kneaded like wheat | Mix to a paste; don't knead |
| A sticky crumb | Sliced warm | Rest it wrapped for a day |
| A gummy rye | Yeasted, without acid | Use a rye sourdough |

**Safety:** a long bake in a heavy tin: dry gloves on both hands, and the tin cooled on a rack before the loaf is turned
out.

**Record:** the formula, the proof cues, the bake, and the crumb photo after a day.

## BRD-3.M5 — Schedule and diagnosis

**Knowledge check:** none for this module. Its judgments, adjusting to the season and diagnosing loaves, are shown in

### BRD-3.M5.L1 — Sourdough around a working week

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m5-l1 -->
*2 h · 3 practical sessions · elapsed 6 wk · builds on `BRD-2.M5.L1` · moves KM-PLAN→4*

*Objectives:* [S] a weekly schedule with retarding; [J] adjust to the season's temperature
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** sourdough seems to demand someone at home all day. With the fridge as a pause button, it fits around
work, and a schedule written for your real week is what keeps a household in bread.

**You need:** your diary for the next four weeks; the starter; the bread log; a room thermometer; the fridge.

**Explanation:**

*The fridge as a tool:*
- the starter kept in the fridge and fed the evening before a bake;
- the shaped loaf retarded overnight, or up to a day or two, so bake day can move.

*A weekday schedule (one example):*
- **morning, before work:** feed the levain;
- **after work:** mix; autolyse; add the levain and salt;
- **evening:** bulk with folds, judged by the dough;
- **before bed:** shape; into the fridge;
- **next morning or evening:** bake from cold.

*Adjusting to the season:*
- **winter:** warmer water, a warm spot (the oven with only its light on), longer bulk;
- **summer:** cooler water, less levain, shorter bulk;
- **always:** the dough temperature logged, and the schedule adjusted from it.

*A control point on time.* If the dough is ready before you are, the fridge pauses it. If it isn't ready, the bake
moves. Never bake an over- or under-proofed loaf to keep to the clock.

*Writing the plan.* Four weeks, about two loaves a week, each bake with its control points: levain ready, dough
temperature, bulk end, retard window, bake. Leave one evening free each week for a slip.

**Practice:**
1. **Plan (30 min).** A four-week schedule written around your actual week.
2. **First week (2 h active over the week).** Baked to the schedule; deviations logged.
3. **Adjust (30 min).** The schedule changed from the first week's log.

**Look for:**
- a schedule that fits the real week;
- changes made from the log, not guessed.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A plan you can't keep | It needs someone at home at noon | Put the long steps overnight, in the fridge |
| Bulk that runs fast or slow | The room temperature ignored | Adjust water and levain by season; log it |
| Flat loaves on busy days | Over-proofed loaves baked anyway | Pause in the fridge; move the bake |

**Safety:** none beyond the kitchen basics; an oven used as a warm spot is switched off, with only its light on, and
checked before preheating.

**Record:** the four-week plan, the first week's log against it, and the changes.

### BRD-3.M5.L2 — Diagnosing loaves

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-brd-3-m5-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `BRD-3.M2.L3` · moves JD-TRBL→4, RD-TEST→3*

*Objectives:* [J] under- and over-proofing, dense crumb, flat loaf: cause and fix; [S] test one variable
<!-- END generated:lesson-brd-3-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** every loaf tells you what happened to it, if you know how to read the crumb and the shape. A
diagnosis turns a disappointing loaf into the reason the next one is better.

**You need:** a dough for two loaves; the bread log and crumb photos from your earlier bakes; a serrated knife; coded
plates for a blind tasting; one taster at least.

**Explanation:**

*Reading the crumb and the shape:*
- **under-proofed:** a dense, tight crumb with a few large irregular holes under the crust; a burst side; a pale, gummy
  feel;
- **over-proofed:** a flat, spreading loaf; a weak ear; an even, large-holed crumb that has collapsed; sour;
- **dense crumb:** too little fermentation, too little water, a weak starter, or too much whole grain without
  adjustment;
- **flat loaf:** weak shaping, over-proofing, too slack a dough, or a cold oven.

*From cause to fix.* Choose one cause from the evidence (the log and the photos), change one variable, and compare the
next loaf. Two changes at once tell you nothing.

*Testing one variable.* For example, bulk extended by an hour, or the retard shortened. Bake two loaves from the same
dough, one changed, and compare the crumbs side by side; taste them blind (`VEG-3.M5.L1`).

*Using the log.* The dough temperature and the bulk time explain most faults. A fault with no log is a guess; a fault
with a log and a photo is a lesson.

**Practice:**
1. **Diagnose and test (90 min over a bake).** Two loaves of the same dough, one variable changed; crumbs compared and
   tasted blind; the diagnosis written.

**Look for:** a cause named from the evidence, and a fix that works on the next loaf.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No idea what worked | Everything changed at once | One variable per bake |
| The same fault every week | The flour blamed every time | Read the log: temperature and time first |
| A diagnosis you can't check | No photos to compare | Photograph every crumb, cut the same way |

**Safety:** a serrated knife cuts away from the hand holding the loaf.

**Record:** the two crumbs side by side, the variable, the blind result and the diagnosis.

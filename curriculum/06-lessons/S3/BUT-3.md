**Before you start.** In S2 you cut a chicken into eight pieces following a video, filleted a round fish and portioned
meat. This course takes the video away, and adds what a home cook gains most from:
- **whole birds:** supremes, a boned leg, a fully boned chicken, and a duck broken down into breasts, legs, fat and
  carcass;
- **whole fish:** round and flat fish filleted cleanly, with the frames and heads used;
- **joints:** trimmed, tied, butterflied, stuffed and rolled for roasting;
- **yield and cost:** what a usable kilo really costs, and when buying whole pays.

Buying whole is cheaper per usable kilo, gives better quality, and leaves bones and trim for stock, fat for cooking and
skin for crisping. It also takes skill and time, which this course builds and then prices honestly in the yield tests.

*Two rules hold through every session:*
- **raw protein on its own board,** as in `POU-2.M1` and `FSH-2`: hands washed after, board and knife washed in hot
  soapy water at once, and the protein back in the fridge at 4 °C while you work on the next part;
- **every part weighed,** so the yield sheet adds up to what you bought.

Meat is from Dutch farms: a free-range chicken or duck from a poulterer, Dutch pork and lamb. Fish is whole, from a
fishmonger: North Sea round fish (mackerel, whiting) or Dutch-farmed trout, and North Sea flat fish (plaice, dab),
MSC-labelled or in season. Plan about four weeks. Each bird and fish is bought for a planned week of dishes, so nothing
waits.

**Have ready:** the boning knife, the filleting knife, fish tweezers and a scaler; kitchen string; a cut-resistant
glove; the raw-protein board on a damp cloth; the scale; the probe; a roasting tin; the phone stand; the yield sheet and
the costing template; freezer bags and labels; the journal.

### BUT-3.M1.L1 — Supremes and a *ballotine* introduced

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m1-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `POU-2.M2.L2` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] cut supremes with the wing bone; bone a leg for stuffing
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a supreme is the restaurant cut of a chicken breast, and a boned leg can be stuffed into a small
roast. Both come from the same bird you already know how to joint, with a few more precise cuts.

**You need:** a whole chicken; the boning knife; the raw board; kitchen string; mushrooms, shallot and breadcrumbs, or a
simple forcemeat, for a filling; the scale; the probe; the yield sheet.

**Explanation:**

*The supreme* (*suprême*): a breast with the first wing joint attached and the skin on.
- The wing tip and the middle joint are cut off.
- The bone that remains is cleaned (*French-trimmed*) by scraping the meat and sinew back towards the breast.

*Taking the breast off the frame:*
1. Cut along one side of the breastbone, the knife tip following the ribs.
2. Lift the flesh with your other hand as you go, in short strokes.
3. Separate the wing joint at the shoulder, and free the breast.
4. Take out the inner fillet, if it stays on the frame, and keep it.

*Boning a leg for stuffing* (a leg *ballotine*):
- free the thigh bone and the drumstick bone from the inside;
- turn the meat back like a sleeve over the knuckle, and cut the tendons;
- keep the leg whole, its skin intact, ready to be stuffed and tied.

*Hygiene* (`POU-2.M1`): raw poultry on its own board, hands washed after, the board and knife washed in hot soapy water
at once.

*Using everything:* the carcass and trim for stock, the skin for crackling or fat, and the oyster (the round piece of
meat on the back) kept with the thigh.

**Practice:**
1. **Supremes (75 min).** Cut two supremes from the chicken and French-trim the bones. Portion the rest of the bird for
   the week; weigh every part and log it.
2. **A boned leg (75 min).** Bone one leg for stuffing; fill it with the mushroom filling or forcemeat, tie it and roast
   it until the probe reads 75 °C in the centre of the stuffing (poultry and stuffed foods, assessment §9). Bone the
   other leg flat for the grill.

**Look for:** a clean breastbone with almost no meat on it; the wing bone clean and white; the boned leg whole, with no
tear through the skin.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Torn, ragged breast | Long sawing strokes | Short strokes with the tip, along the bone |
| A leg that won't hold a stuffing | Cutting through the skin when boning the leg | Work from the inside; keep the blade on the bone |
| Lost meat | Leaving the inner fillet on the frame | Check the frame; lift it off with your fingers |
| Cross-contamination | Raw chicken on the vegetable board | Raw poultry on its own board, always |

**Safety:** the raw-poultry rules of `POU-2.M1`; the stuffed leg cooked to 75 °C in the stuffing's centre
(assessment §9).

**Record:** the weights; photos of the supremes, the frame and the boned leg.

### BUT-3.M1.L2 — Boning a bird; duck

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m1-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BUT-3.M1.L1` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] bone a whole chicken; break down a duck (breasts, legs, fat, carcass)
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a boned chicken, stuffed and rolled, is a dinner-party roast that slices cleanly. A duck, broken
down, gives four things: breasts for a meal, legs for confit, fat for potatoes and a carcass for dark stock.

**You need:** a whole chicken; a whole duck (about 2–2.5 kg) for `BUT-3.M1.X1`; the boning knife; the raw board; a
stuffing; kitchen string; a heavy pan for rendering; a sieve and a clean jar; the probe; the scale.

**Explanation:**

*Boning a whole chicken from the back:*
1. Cut along the backbone.
2. Work the flesh off the frame on each side, the knife tip against the bone.
3. Free the leg and wing joints from the frame.
4. Lift out the frame without piercing the skin of the breast.

The result is a flat bird, ready to stuff and roll (a whole *ballotine*) or to grill flat.

*Duck is different:*
- a thick layer of fat under the skin, which is rendered, not thrown away;
- the breasts taken off the frame like a chicken's, the skin scored without cutting into the meat;
- the legs taken off at the hip, kept for confit (`PRS-3.M4`);
- the carcass roasted for a dark stock.

*Rendering the fat:* trim all loose fat and skin, cut it small, and render it slowly in a heavy pan with a little water,
until the cracklings are golden. Strain the fat into a clean jar and keep it in the fridge. Too much heat browns the
fat, and it tastes burnt.

*Planning a duck:* the two breasts for one meal, the legs for confit, the fat for potatoes, the carcass for stock and
soup.

**Practice:**
1. **A boned chicken (75 min).** Bone a whole chicken from the back; stuff, roll and tie it, and roast it until the
   probe reads 75 °C in the centre of the stuffing. The frame goes to stock.
2. **A duck (90 min).** Break down the duck into breasts, legs, fat and carcass for `BUT-3.M1.X1`; weigh each part, and
   render the fat.

**Look for:** the boned chicken in one piece, with no holes in the skin; the duck fat clear and white when cold; the
cracklings crisp.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A stuffing that leaks out | Piercing the breast skin from inside | Keep the edge turned towards the bone |
| Money and flavour lost | Throwing away the duck fat | Render it; it keeps for months in the fridge |
| Brown, bitter fat | Rendering the fat too hot | Low heat, a little water, patience |

**Safety:** duck is poultry: the rules of `POU-2.M1`, and cooked to the poultry figures of assessment §9. Rendering fat
is hot fat: the pan at the back of the hob, the handle turned in, and the fat cooled before it is poured into the jar.

**Record:** the duck's weights by part; photos of the boned chicken and of both frames.

### BUT-3.M2.L1 — Filleting a round fish

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `FSH-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] mackerel, trout or sea bass: two clean fillets, minimal waste
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** in `FSH-2` you filleted a round fish with the video beside you. Doing it fluently, with a frame you
can see through, is what makes a whole fish worth buying.

**You need:** two mackerel or trout; a whiting or a farmed trout of 600–800 g; the filleting knife; tweezers; the
scaler; the raw board; salt; a cold plate and cling film; the scale.

**Explanation:**

*Before filleting:*
- scale the fish from tail to head, under water or in a bag, so the scales don't fly;
- gut and rinse it, and scrape out the bloodline along the backbone;
- dry it.

*Two fillets from a round fish:*
1. Cut behind the head and the gill flap, down to the bone.
2. Turn the knife flat along the backbone, and work from head to tail in long strokes, the blade pressed against the
   bones.
3. Go over the top of the rib cage, then free the fillet at the tail.
4. Turn the fish over, and do the same on the other side.

*Trimming:*
- trim the belly edge and the fins;
- feel for the pin bones with a fingertip, and pull them with tweezers towards the head;
- portion the fillet.

*Skinning:* the fillet skin-down, the tail end held with salt on your fingers for grip. Angle the knife slightly down,
and pull the skin back and forth against it rather than pushing the knife.

*Keeping it cold:* fillet the fish straight from the fridge, put the fillets on a cold plate, covered, and back in the
fridge at 4 °C (assessment §9) while you work on the next fish.

**Practice:**
1. **Mackerel or trout (60 min).** Fillet two fish, pin-bone them, and weigh fillets and frames. Skin one fillet.
2. **A larger round fish (60 min).** Fillet the whiting or trout, and portion the fillets for a meal.

**Look for:** a frame with very little flesh, held up to the light; smooth fillets with no ragged steps; no pin bones
under the fingertip.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A torn fillet | Sawing | Long, even strokes |
| Flesh left on the frame | The knife angled up into the flesh | The blade flat, pressed against the bones |
| A torn line where the bones were | Pin bones pulled away from the head | Pull towards the head |
| Warm, soft fish | Fish left on the counter while working | Back in the fridge between fish |

**Safety:** the raw-fish rules of `FSH-2`; the filleting knife moving away from the holding hand; scales and guts bagged
and out of the kitchen the same day.

**Record:** the weights; photos of the frames against the light.

### BUT-3.M2.L2 — Filleting a flat fish

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BUT-3.M2.L1` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] plaice or sole: four fillets; skinning
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a flat fish gives four thin fillets instead of two, and its bones lie in a different plane. It is a
new technique, and the `TS-FAB` row asks for it.

**You need:** two plaice; a dab or a third plaice; the filleting knife; the raw board; salt; a cold plate; the scale;
the reference video for the first fish only.

**Explanation:**

*Four fillets from a flat fish* (plaice, dab, sole):
1. Cut down the centre line, along the backbone, from head to tail.
2. With the knife flat, work outwards over the bones in long strokes, lifting the fillet as you go.
3. Free it at the edge along the fins; do the same on the other side of the line.
4. Turn the fish over, and take two more fillets from the white side.

*Skinning:* dark-skinned fillets are usually skinned, the same way as a round-fish fillet. Sole can be skinned whole:
nick the skin at the tail and pull the dark skin off towards the head.

*Whole or filleted.* Whole flat fish are often cooked on the bone (plaice in butter), which keeps them moist. Filleting
is for dishes that need a fillet: rolled, stuffed, or served in a sauce.

*Yield.* A flat fish yields less than a round fish of the same weight, because its head and frame are heavier; the yield
sheet will show it.

**Practice:**
1. **Plaice (60 min).** Fillet two plaice into four fillets each; skin them; weigh fillets, skins and frames.
2. **A second flat fish (60 min).** Fillet the dab or another plaice without the video, and cook the fillets that
   evening.

**Look for:** four whole fillets with no ragged edges; a frame with very little flesh on it.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Fillets in pieces | Starting at the edge instead of the centre line | Start at the centre line, work outwards |
| Bones in the fillet | Cutting through the bones | Let the blade ride over them, flat |
| A ragged tail | Tearing the thin tail end | Short strokes at the tail; hold it down with salted fingers |

**Safety:** as in `BUT-3.M2.L1`; the fillets cooked to the fish figure of assessment §9 (63 °C for 15 seconds at the
thickest part).

**Record:** the weights; photos of the fillets and the frames.

### BUT-3.M2.L3 — Frames and heads

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l3 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 1 h · builds on `STK-2.M2.L4` · moves KM-WAST→4*

*Objectives:* [S] *fumet*, soup or crisp skin from the trim; [J] what to keep and what to discard
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m2-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** the frame and head of a fish are a third of what you paid for. A good *fumet* or a crisp skin turns
them into a meal, and knowing what to discard keeps a stock from turning bitter or oily.

**You need:** the plaice frames from `BUT-3.M2.L2` (or frozen frames); onion, leek and celery or celeriac; white wine if
you use it; a pot; a fine sieve; the skins; a baking tray and paper; the probe; the cooling log.

**Explanation:**

*The fumet* (`STK-2.M2.L4`):
- white-fish frames and heads, with the gills removed and the blood rinsed out;
- sweated with the aromatics, then covered with water (and wine);
- simmered for no more than 30 minutes, then strained;
- cooled by the standard of assessment §9: from 57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, and to 5 °C within 6 hours.

*Other uses:*
- a fish soup from the frame and the scraps scraped off it;
- fish skin, dried and baked or fried crisp, as a garnish;
- the cheeks, cut from larger heads.

*The judgment: what to keep and what to discard?*
- **keep** white-fish frames and heads; flat-fish frames make an excellent *fumet*;
- **discard** the gills (bitter), the guts and any bloodline;
- **oily-fish frames** (mackerel) make a strong, often unpleasant stock: use them for a robust soup only, or discard;
- **freeze** the frames you won't use within the day.

**Practice:**
1. **A *fumet* and crisp skin (60 min active, 1 h in total).** Make a *fumet* from the plaice frames; strain it, cool
   it and log the cooling. Meanwhile, scrape the skins clean, lay them flat between two sheets of baking paper on a
   tray, and bake them until crisp.

**Look for:** a clear, fresh-smelling *fumet*; skin crisp and dry.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A bitter stock | Gills left in | Cut the gills out first |
| A cloudy, gluey *fumet* | A *fumet* boiled for two hours | 30 minutes at a simmer, no more |
| An oily, fishy stock | Mackerel frames in a delicate stock | White-fish frames only |

**Safety:** the *fumet* cooled by the §9 standard and logged; kept at most 2 days in the fridge, or frozen.

**Record:** the cooling log; a photo of the *fumet* and the skin.

### BUT-3.M3.L1 — Trimming and tying

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `KNF-2.M2.L1` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] trim a pork loin or shoulder; tie a roast evenly
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a trimmed, tied joint cooks evenly, holds its shape, and slices neatly at the table. The butcher's
knot takes ten minutes to learn and is used for every roast after this.

**You need:** a pork loin; a smaller joint or a piece of pork belly; the boning knife; kitchen string; the raw board;
salt for the dry brine; the roasting tin; the probe.

**Explanation:**

*Trimming a joint* (a pork loin or a shoulder):
- remove the silverskin, the thin, shiny membrane, with the knife tip under it and the blade angled up, so you take the
  membrane and not the meat;
- trim the fat cap to an even 5–8 mm, and score it;
- remove loose flaps and sinew, and keep them for stock or mince.

*Why tie:* an even cylinder cooks evenly, holds a stuffing, and slices neatly.

*The butcher's knot,* a slip knot that holds its tension while you tighten it, and two ways to tie:
- separate loops every 2–3 cm, each tied with the slip knot;
- one continuous string, with half-hitches along the joint.

Tie firmly, but not so tight that the string cuts into the meat.

*Seasoning:* a joint salted the day before (dry-brined, `SCI-2.M2`) tastes seasoned all the way through.

**Practice:**
1. **Trim and tie (60 min).** Salt the pork loin the day before. Trim it, tie it with separate loops, and roast it to
   the §9 figure for intact pork: 63 °C for 15 seconds at the thickest part, probed.
2. **Tie again (45 min).** Tie the smaller joint or a rolled belly with one continuous string; untie and retie it until
   the loops are even.

**Look for:** loops evenly spaced; an even cylinder; no silverskin left.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A bulging, misshapen roast | String so tight it cuts in | Firm, not tight |
| An uneven shape | Loops too far apart | Every 2–3 cm |
| Lost meat | Taking off half the meat with the silverskin | The blade angled up, against the membrane |

**Safety:** raw meat on its own board; the knife moving away from the body (`BUT-3.M4.L2`); the roast cooked to the
intact-meat figure of assessment §9.

**Record:** photos of the tied roast and the slice; the probe reading.

### BUT-3.M3.L2 — Butterflying and stuffing

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 h · builds on `BUT-3.M3.L1` · moves TS-FAB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] butterfly a joint or a leg of lamb; stuff and roll
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** butterflying opens a thick joint into an even sheet that can be seasoned inside, stuffed and rolled.
The spiral of the slice looks impressive, and it is the joint you will cook for guests in `BUT-3.M3.X1`.

**You need:** a pork shoulder steak or a boned lamb leg; a seasonal stuffing (herbs, breadcrumbs, mushrooms, onion, or
sausage meat); the boning knife; kitchen string; the raw board; the roasting tin; the probe.

**Explanation:**

*Butterflying:* a thick joint opened into an even sheet:
- a loin cut along its length, two-thirds through, then opened like a book;
- or rolled out with a spiral cut;
- a boned leg of lamb opened, and its thick parts cut and laid flat.

The aim is an even thickness, so it cooks evenly.

*Stuffing:*
- one that adds moisture and flavour: herbs, breadcrumbs, mushrooms, apple or dried fruit, sausage meat;
- cooled before it goes in;
- spread thin, leaving a border, so it doesn't squeeze out.

*Rolling and tying:* roll tightly from the long side, put the seam underneath, and tie every 2–3 cm.

*Safety:* a stuffed joint is cooked until the centre of the stuffing reaches 75 °C (stuffed foods, assessment §9).
Stuffing with sausage meat follows the same rule. The stuffing sits in the centre, so it is the last part to heat:
probe it, not only the meat.

*Resting and slicing:* rest the roast, cut the strings away, and slice across into rounds that show the spiral.

**Practice:**
1. **A butterflied, stuffed and rolled joint (75 min active, 2 h in total).** Butterfly the pork or lamb; season it;
   spread the cooled stuffing; roll, tie and roast it. Probe the centre of the stuffing until it reads 75 °C, then rest
   and slice it.

**Look for:** an even thickness when opened; a neat spiral in the slice; the stuffing's centre at 75 °C.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dry edges, raw middle | Uneven thickness | Cut the thick parts open until even |
| A food-safety risk | Warm stuffing in a raw joint | Cool the stuffing before it goes in |
| An undercooked centre | Probing the meat but not the stuffing | Probe the centre of the stuffing |

**Safety:** the stuffing cooled before it goes in, and its centre cooked to 75 °C (assessment §9); raw meat on its own
board.

**Record:** photos of the opened joint, the tied roast and the slice; the probe log.

### BUT-3.M3.X1 — A stuffed, rolled roast for guests

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-but-3-m3-x1 -->
*framework, framework* — A stuffed, rolled roast for guests.
<!-- END generated:exercise-but-3-m3-x1 -->

**Task:** prepare and cook a stuffed, rolled roast for four to six guests, with the trim used. The joint is trimmed,
butterflied, stuffed, rolled and tied by you without guidance; the trim goes into a stock or a sauce; the centre of the
stuffing is probed to 75 °C.

**You need:** a pork loin or shoulder, or a lamb leg, from a Dutch butcher (about 1.5–2 kg); stuffing ingredients from
the season; kitchen string; the knives, the board, the scale and the probe; the roasting tin.

**Framework:** the stuffed roast.

| Part | What it is | Your decisions |
|---|---|---|

## BUT-3.M4 — Yield and costing

**Knowledge check:** the 8 items of `BUT-3.M4` in the [course file](../../05-courses/S3/BUT-3.md), 3 of them safety
items. Pass mark 80 %, with every safety item correct.

### BUT-3.M4.L1 — Yield tests

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m4-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `WRK-1.M3.L3` · moves KM-MATH→4, KM-PURC→3*

*Objectives:* [S] weigh whole, usable and trim; cost per usable kilo; [J] whole versus prepared
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** "buying whole is cheaper" is true only per usable kilo. A yield test turns the claim into a number,
and the same numbers let you cost a dinner before you shop.

**You need:** a whole chicken; the same weight of bought chicken parts (breasts, thighs, wings); the receipts; the
scale; the yield sheet; a calculator.

**Explanation:**

*The yield test:*
- weigh the whole item as bought: the as-purchased weight;
- weigh each usable part (the edible portion) and the trim;
- yield % = usable weight ÷ purchased weight × 100.

*Cost per usable kilo* = price paid ÷ usable weight. A whole chicken at €8 per kilo with a 65 % meat yield costs about
€12.30 per kilo of meat, before counting the value of the frame for stock.

*Valuing the by-products:* a frame that replaces a bought stock, fat that replaces bought fat. Count them at the price
of what they replace, or leave them out to stay conservative; say which you did.

*Cooking loss:* a second yield, from raw to cooked. A roast loses 20–30 % of its weight, which matters when you cost a
portion as served.

*The judgment: whole or prepared?* Compare the cost per usable kilo; add your time at a value you choose; then decide.
Whole often wins on cost and quality, prepared on time.

*Costing a menu:* the cost per portion from the measured yields. Scale with care, because not everything scales in a
straight line:
- seasoning: a roast twice the size has less surface per kilo, so it needs less than twice the salt;
- braising liquid: a wider pot needs less than double;
- time: a larger roast takes longer, but not twice as long.

**Practice:**
1. **A yield test (60 min).** Break down the whole chicken, and weigh every part and the trim. Weigh the bought parts.
   Price both on the yield sheet, per usable kilo.

**Look for:** a yield sheet that adds up to the purchased weight within a few grams.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A sheet that doesn't add up | Weighing after trimming but forgetting the trim | Weigh every part, trim included |
| A wrong conclusion | Comparing the price per kilo as bought instead of per usable kilo | Always per usable kilo |
| An over-salted roast | Scaling salt linearly for a roast twice the size | Scale the salt by surface, and taste |

**Safety:** raw poultry handled as in `POU-2.M1`; the parts back in the fridge while you calculate.

**Record:** the yield sheet.

### BUT-3.M4.L2 — Boning safely

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-but-3-m4-l2 -->
*1 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `KNF-1.M1.L3` · moves SA-PHYS→4*

*Objectives:* [S] mesh glove, stable board, cutting away from the body
<!-- END generated:lesson-but-3-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** boning is the knife work most likely to cut you: a flexible blade, force against bone, and greasy
hands. A safe station prevents almost all of it, and all three items of this module's check that are safety items come
from here.

**You need:** the boning knife; the cut-resistant glove; the board; a damp cloth or a non-slip mat; paper towel; the
phone for filming in `BUT-3.M4.X1`.

**Explanation:**

*Where boning cuts happen:*
- the knife slips off a bone;
- the cut runs towards the holding hand or the body;
- the handle is greasy.

*The safe station:*
- a stable board on a damp cloth or a non-slip mat, at hip height;
- a dry, grease-free handle and dry hands: wipe both often with paper towel;
- a mesh (cut-resistant) glove on the hand that holds the meat, because that is the hand in the knife's path;
- the knife moving away from the body and from the holding hand;
- short, controlled strokes with the tip, not force;
- the knife never left under trim, or in the sink.

*A sharp knife is a safe knife* (`KNF-2`): a blunt boning knife needs force, and force makes it slip.

*If the knife falls,* step back and let it fall. Never try to catch it, by the blade or by the handle.

**Practice:** theory only (1 h): set up the boning station as above, and explain each choice aloud (as you will on film
in `BUT-3.M4.X1`). Then write the station as a checklist of six lines for the inside of a cupboard door.

**Look for:** you can set up the station and explain each part of it.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A cut on the holding hand | Cutting towards the holding hand | Cut away from it; the glove on that hand |
| The knife slips | A slippery handle | Wipe the handle and your hands dry |
| A deep cut | Catching a falling knife | Step back; let it fall |

**Safety:** this lesson is the boning-safety part of `SA-PHYS`: the glove on the holding hand, the knife moving away
from you, a dry handle, and a falling knife left to fall.

**Record:** the checklist.

**Before you start.** In `HEA-2.M3` you grilled on a grill pan and under the oven grill. This course moves outdoors, to
a charcoal fire, where you control the heat source itself:
- **the fire:** fuel, airflow, zones and the lid; reading heat by hand and by thermometer; safety around fire, coals,
  ash and carbon monoxide;
- **direct grilling:** meat, fish, vegetables, fruit and flatbread, seared hot and finished in the cool zone;
- **indirect cooking:** the lidded grill as an oven, and wood smoke as a flavour;
- **low-and-slow barbecue:** a pork shoulder or ribs over many hours, through the stall, to a target texture;
- **embers and the plancha:** vegetables in the coals, cast iron over the fire;
- **a live-fire meal** for guests, planned like a service.

*Every protein reaches its figure in
[assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment):* poultry 75 °C, minced meat 75 °C, fish and
intact meat 63 °C for 15 seconds, intact beef steaks by the steak row. Fire gives uneven heat, so the probe matters more
outdoors than indoors. Smoke here is a flavour, not a preservative: cold smoking and smoked cures are outside the
program.

*Without outdoor space,* a cast-iron grill pan and the oven grill teach the browning and the doneness, but not fire
control. You then reach `TS-FIRE` level 2 at most, and the record says so; `MS-S3` accepts the oven grill on the same
terms.

The produce is seasonal: grilling vegetables in summer (courgette, peppers and aubergine from Dutch greenhouses, sweet
corn, spring onions), and roots and onions for the embers in autumn; Dutch pork, beef and chicken; North Sea fish
(mackerel, herring) whole for the grill. Plan about five weeks in the grilling season, May to September, mostly at
weekends. The low-and-slow cook takes a whole day.

**Have ready:** a lidded charcoal grill; a chimney starter; natural firelighters; lump charcoal or briquettes without
lighter fluid; dry hardwood chunks; heat-resistant gloves; long tongs and a wide fish spatula; the probe, and an oven
thermometer for the grate; a grill brush; a metal ash bucket with a lid; the fire blanket and a bucket of water or sand;
a cast-iron skillet; kitchen shears; the fire log and the control-point plan of `SAF-3.M2.X1`.

### FIR-3.M1.L1 — Fuel, grills and safety

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-FIRE→1, SA-PHYS→4*

*Objectives:* [K] briquettes, lump charcoal, wood and gas; kettle, kamado and open grills; [K] fire, burns and carbon monoxide (never indoors or in a closed space); [K] flare-ups, charring and smoke compounds, and how to limit them
<!-- END generated:lesson-fir-3-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a fire is the one heat source in this program that can poison you without a flame in sight. Before
you light one, you choose the fuel and the place, and you know what to do with the ash.

**You need:** the journal; your grill and its manual, or a shop's description of one; the fire-safety checklist.

**Explanation:**

*Fuel:*
- **lump charcoal:** lights fast, burns hot and unevenly, leaves little ash;
- **briquettes:** even and long-burning, more ash; choose ones without lighter fluid added;
- **wood:** chunks for smoke or a wood fire; only dry, untreated hardwood (beech, oak, fruit woods), never painted or
  treated wood;
- **gas:** clean and convenient, but no fire to manage and little smoky flavour.

*Grills:* the kettle (a lid and two vents: a versatile oven), the kamado (thick ceramic, very stable heat), and the open
grill or brazier (direct heat only).

*How a fire cooks:* mostly by radiant heat from the coals, which you feel on your hand, plus convection under the lid.
Heat falls off fast with distance.

*Safety:*
- **carbon monoxide:** a charcoal grill is never used indoors, in a garage, a tent or a closed porch, and never brought
  inside while warm;
- the grill on a stable, non-flammable surface, away from walls, hedges and parasols;
- no lighter fluid, petrol or spirits on a lit fire;
- heat-resistant gloves, long tongs, a bucket of water or sand and the fire blanket nearby;
- children and pets kept away;
- ash only into a metal bucket with a lid, and only when completely cold: embers can stay hot for a day.

*Flare-ups, charring and smoke compounds.* Fat dripping on the coals flares, and leaves soot and smoke compounds on the
food; charred, blackened parts contain compounds best limited. To limit them, trim excess fat, cook over a two-zone
fire and move the food away from flames, avoid blackening, and cut off burnt bits.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): choose a fuel for three cooks (a quick weeknight grill, a lidded roast, a day-long
cook) with the reason for each. Then choose the place for your grill: walk the garden or balcony with the checklist, and
photograph the spot, with the water bucket, the blanket and the ash bucket in place.

**Look for:** you can choose a fuel for a cook and set up a safe place for the grill.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A fireball | Lighter fluid on a lit fire | A chimney starter; nothing liquid on a lit fire |
| A burnt parasol | The grill under a parasol | Open sky, away from walls and hedges |
| A bin fire | Warm ash in a plastic bin | A metal bucket, and only when cold |
| Carbon monoxide poisoning | A grill in the garage "because it's raining" | Never indoors or in a closed space |

**Safety:** this lesson is the fire-safety part of `SA-PHYS`; the checklist is used at every fire of the course.

**Record:** the fuel choices and the photo of the spot.

### FIR-3.M1.L2 — Building and controlling the fire

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m1-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 2 h · builds on `FIR-3.M1.L1` · moves TS-FIRE→2, JD-CUES→4, SA-PHYS→5*

*Objectives:* [S] light with a chimney, set two- and three-zone fires, control with vents and lid; [J] read heat by the hand test and the lid thermometer; [S] handle hot grates, coals and ash safely
<!-- END generated:lesson-fir-3-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a fire you control is a stove with a hot, a medium and a cool burner, and an oven with a lid. One
you don't control burns the outside of everything and leaves the inside raw.

**You need:** charcoal; the chimney starter; firelighters or newspaper; the grill; the gloves and tongs; an oven
thermometer for the grate; a few sausages and two vegetables; the probe; the fire log.

**Explanation:**

*The chimney starter:* paper or natural firelighters underneath, charcoal on top. It is ready when the top coals are
covered in grey ash, after 15–25 minutes.

*Zones:*
- **two-zone:** all the coals on one side (direct, hot) and none on the other (indirect, cool);
- **three-zone:** a thick hot bank, a thinner medium area, and an empty cool zone;
- the cool zone is where food goes when it flares or browns too fast.

*Vents and lid.* The bottom vent feeds air to the fire: more air, more heat. The top vent lets smoke and heat out, and
draws air through. Make small changes, then wait 10–15 minutes before judging.

*Reading heat:*
- **the hand test:** your palm held about 10 cm above the grate; count the seconds before you must move it: about 2 for
  very hot, 4–5 for medium, 7 or more for low;
- **the lid thermometer,** checked against an oven thermometer on the grate, because lid thermometers read the dome,
  not the grate;
- write the hand-test estimate before the reading: cue, then reading.

*Handling hot grates and coals:* heat-resistant gloves and long tongs; the grate lifted by its handles; coals moved with
tongs or a small shovel, never with your hands.

**Practice:**
1. **Light and zone (60 min).** Light a chimney, build a two-zone fire, and grill a few sausages and vegetables over the
   zones. Log your hand-test estimates against the grate thermometer.
2. **Hold temperatures (90 min active, 2 h in total).** Hold the lid at 120 °C and then at 180 °C for 20 minutes each by
   the vents, logged every 5 minutes (`FIR-3.M1.X1`).

**Look for:** coals grey-ashed before spreading; the lid within ±15 °C of the target for 20 minutes; hand-test estimates
close to the readings.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Smoky, sooty food | Spreading black coals | Wait for grey ash |
| A fire that never settles | Opening the lid every two minutes | Lid closed; check every 5 minutes |
| Temperature swings | Big vent changes and impatience | Small changes; wait 10–15 minutes |
| Burnt food with nowhere to go | No cool zone | Always leave one side empty |

**Safety:** the checklist ticked before lighting; the chimney set down on the grate or a non-flammable surface; gloves
on for every touch of the grate.

**Record:** the hand-test log and the lid log.

### FIR-3.M1.X1 — Two zones, two temperatures

<!-- BEGIN generated:exercise-fir-3-m1-x1 -->
*drill, recipe* — Light a chimney, build a two-zone fire, and hold the lid at 120 °C and at 180 °C for 20 minutes each; log the vent positions.
<!-- END generated:exercise-fir-3-m1-x1 -->

**Task:** build and control a fire by the vents, and read heat by hand before the thermometer. Light a chimney, build a
two-zone fire, and hold the lid at 120 °C and then at 180 °C (±15 °C) for 20 minutes each, logging the vent positions.
Grill vegetables and a simple protein over the two zones to their §9 figures, and film the setup, the lighting and the
handling of the grate and coals with the fire-safety checklist.

**You need:** charcoal; the chimney; firelighters; an oven thermometer for the grate; sausages or chicken thighs; two
seasonal vegetables; the gloves, tongs and probe; the phone on a stand; the fire-control sheet and the checklist.

**Recipe:** the fire-control session.
1. **Set up** the grill in its safe spot; tick the checklist aloud; start filming.
2. **Light** the chimney; when the top coals are grey, pour them onto one side for a two-zone fire.
3. **Grill** the vegetables and the protein, moving them between the zones. Probe the protein: sausages 75 °C (minced
   meat), chicken thighs 75 °C (poultry). Log each, with your cue first.
4. **Close** the lid with the top vent over the cool side. Set the vents to bring the lid to 120 °C.
5. **Hold** 120 °C for 20 minutes; log the lid, the grate thermometer, the vent positions and a hand estimate every 5
   minutes.
6. **Open** the vents to bring the lid to 180 °C; hold it for 20 minutes, logged the same way.
7. **Close** both vents to put the fire out; leave the grill where it is.
8. **Dispose** of the ash when it is completely cold, into the metal bucket.

**Show:**
- **`TS-FIRE` level 2:** a chimney lit, a two-zone fire built, vegetables and a simple protein grilled to doneness. The
  lid log at 120 and 180 °C is the first part of level 3, which `FIR-3.M2` to `FIR-3.M4` complete.
- **`SA-PHYS` level 5, a live-fire service:** the setup, the coals and the grate handled safely on video, with the
  checklist complete. The other services of the row are listed in `SAF-3.M2.X1`.
- **`JD-CUES` level 4, in part:** hand-test estimates against grate readings in an unfamiliar situation, adding to the
  entries of `SLW-3.M5.X1` and `SLW-3.M5.X2` to at least 8 of 10 within tolerance.

AI review watches the video and reads the log: *competent* is the checklist complete, gloves on for every touch, and
both temperatures held within ±15 °C; *not yet* is a bare hand near the grate, or a log with gaps.

**Time:** 2.5 h.

### FIR-3.M2.L1 — Grilling meat

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m2-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-FIRE→3, TS-PRO→4*

*Objectives:* [S] steaks, chops, sausages and skewers; [J] manage flare-ups; sear hot, finish in the cool zone; reverse sear
<!-- END generated:lesson-fir-3-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** most grilled meat goes wrong in one of two ways: burnt outside and raw inside, or grey and dry. Two
zones, the lid and the probe solve both.

**You need:** a steak; a pork chop; sausages; chicken thighs cut for skewers; skewers (soaked, if wooden); the two-zone
fire; the probe; the log.

**Explanation:**

*Sear hot, finish cool.* Thick cuts are seared over the hot zone, then moved to the cool zone with the lid on until the
core is near the target. Thin cuts are cooked over direct heat only.

*Reverse sear.* A thick steak is cooked in the cool zone with the lid on until about 10 °C below the target, then seared
hard over the coals. It is even inside, with a crust outside.

*The cuts and their figures* (assessment §9):

| Cut | Figure |
|---|---|
| Steak | The steak row: intact, seared on every surface |
| Pork chop | 63 °C for 15 seconds |
| Sausages and burgers | Minced meat: 75 °C |
| Chicken thighs on skewers | Poultry: 75 °C |

*The judgment: flare-ups.* Move the food to the cool zone, and close the lid briefly to starve the flames. Never water,
which throws up ash and steam.

*Doneness.* Cue first (the feel, the colour of the juices), then the probe in the thickest part. Allow for carryover
after the grill.

**Practice:**
1. **Steaks and chops (60 min).** Grill a steak by direct heat, and a pork chop seared and finished cool. Write the cue,
   then probe, and log both.
2. **Sausages and skewers (60 min).** Cook sausages slowly in the medium zone, and chicken skewers hot, each to its §9
   figure.

**Look for:** a crust without black char; the core within 3 °C of the target after resting; sausages browned without
bursting.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Burnt and raw | Sausages over the hottest zone | The medium zone, turned often |
| Dry burgers | Pressing burgers flat with the spatula | Leave them; turn once |
| Undercooked chicken | Pinkish chicken skewers served "because they look done" | Probe each: 75 °C |

**Safety:** minced meat and poultry to 75 °C, probed; raw meat on its own tray and board, and a clean plate for the
cooked meat; the tongs that touched raw meat washed before they touch cooked food.

**Record:** the cue-then-probe log.

### FIR-3.M2.L2 — Grilling fish and seafood

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m2-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `FSH-2.M3.L5` · moves TS-FIRE→3, IN-FSH→3*

*Objectives:* [S] whole fish, skin-on fillets and prawns without sticking; [K] a clean, hot, oiled grate
<!-- END generated:lesson-fir-3-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a whole mackerel from the grill is one of summer's best meals, and one of the easiest to ruin: the
skin sticks, the fish tears, and half of it stays on the grate.

**You need:** a whole mackerel or a sea bream bought whole; oil; salt; the grill brush; a wide fish spatula or a fish
basket; the probe; the phone for the freshness photos.

**Explanation:**

*A clean, hot, oiled grate.* Brush the grate clean, heat it well, and oil the fish lightly, not the grate. Fish sticks
when the grate is dirty or not hot enough, or when it is turned too soon: it releases when the skin has crisped.

*Whole fish:* a whole mackerel or herring, slashed on both sides, cooked over medium heat and turned once with a wide
spatula, or in a fish basket.

*Fillets:* skin-on, skin side down for most of the time, turned once; or on a plank or foil for thin, delicate fillets.

*Prawns and mussels:* prawns on skewers over high heat for a few minutes; mussels in a pan on the grill until they open.
Mussels that stay closed are thrown away (`FSH-2.M4`).

*Doneness:* 63 °C for 15 seconds at the thickest part (assessment §9); the flesh opaque and flaking at the bone.

*Buying for the grill.* Firm, oily fish suit it best. Judge freshness at purchase (`FSH-2.M1`): clear eyes, red gills, a
fresh sea smell.

**Practice:**
1. **A whole fish (60 min).** Buy a whole mackerel or sea bream, and photograph the eyes and gills. Slash, oil and salt
   it; grill it over medium heat, turning it once when it releases; probe it at the backbone and serve it.

**Look for:** the skin crisp and intact; the fish releasing without tearing; 63 °C at the backbone.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Fish stuck to the grate | A cold or dirty grate | Brush clean; heat well; oil the fish |
| Torn skin | Turning the fish too early | Wait until it releases |
| Fillets through the grate | Thin delicate fillets straight on the grate | A plank, foil or a basket |

**Safety:** fish to 63 °C for 15 seconds at the thickest part (assessment §9); mussels that stay closed are discarded.

**Record:** the freshness photos; the probe reading.

### FIR-3.M2.L3 — Vegetables, fruit and flatbreads

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m2-l3 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `HEA-2.M3.L2` · moves TS-FIRE→3, TS-VEG→4*

*Objectives:* [S] grilled and charred vegetables, fruit for dessert, flatbread on the grate; [J] which vegetables need blanching first
<!-- END generated:lesson-fir-3-m2-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** vegetables are where the grill's flavour shows most, and where a few choices decide everything: cut
thick, blanch the dense ones, and know which deserve a char.

**You need:** three seasonal vegetables, one of them dense (potatoes, carrots, beetroot, celeriac or fennel); oil; salt;
a flatbread dough (yeasted or yoghurt); herb oil; fruit in season; a dairy topping or a crumble.

**Explanation:**

*Straight on the grate:* courgette, peppers, aubergine, spring onions, leeks, mushrooms, sweet corn, chicory halves.
Oiled, salted, and cut thick enough to stay on the grate.

*The judgment: blanch first?* Dense vegetables (potatoes, carrots, beetroot, celeriac, fennel) are parboiled or steamed
until nearly tender, then grilled for colour. On the grill alone they burn outside before they cook through.

*Charring.* A deep char on peppers and aubergine is meant: the skins are peeled away after, and the flesh underneath is
soft and smoky. Put them in a covered bowl for a few minutes after the grill, and the skins slip off. On most other
vegetables, aim for colour without black: turn them when the grate has marked them, and move them to the cool zone if
they colour before they soften.

*Fruit for dessert:* Dutch apples, pears, plums or rhubarb in season, grilled until caramelised, with a dairy topping or
a crumble.

*Flatbread on the grate:*
1. Roll a simple yeasted or yoghurt dough thin.
2. Grill it for a minute or two a side, until puffed and spotted.
3. Brush with herb oil (`FLV-3.M1.L2`).

**Practice:**
1. **Vegetables (60 min).** Grill three vegetables, one blanched first, until tender and coloured.
2. **Flatbread and fruit (60 min).** Grill flatbreads on the grate, and make a grilled fruit dessert.

**Look for:** vegetables tender through and coloured outside; flatbread puffed and spotted.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Burnt outside, raw inside | Raw potato slices burnt outside | Parboil dense vegetables first |
| Lost vegetables | Thin courgette ribbons falling through the grate | Cut thick, or lengthwise |
| Doughy bread | A thick flatbread raw inside | Roll it thin |

**Safety:** the herb oil from the fridge, used within days (assessment §9); hot peppers peeled when cool enough to hold.

**Record:** photos; a note on which vegetable you blanched and why.

### FIR-3.M3.L1 — Indirect roasting

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 h · builds on `POU-2.M3.L1` · moves TS-FIRE→3, TS-DRY→4*

*Objectives:* [S] a spatchcocked chicken or a joint by indirect heat; [J] hold the temperature with the vents; [K] carryover outdoors
<!-- END generated:lesson-fir-3-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** with the lid on and the food away from the coals, the grill becomes an oven with smoke. A
spatchcocked chicken is the classic first roast: flat, fast, and crisp all over.

**You need:** the chicken of `FIR-3.M3.X1`, salted the day before; kitchen shears; a drip tray; charcoal and a second
chimney; the probe; the fire log.

**Explanation:**

*The grill as an oven:*
- the food in the cool zone, the lid on;
- the top vent over the food, so the heat and smoke draw across it;
- a drip tray under the food, to catch the fat before it flares.

*Spatchcocking:* cut out the backbone with shears and press the bird flat. It cooks faster and more evenly than a whole
bird, and all the skin faces up.

*The judgment: holding the temperature.* About 180–200 °C for a chicken, by the vents. For a long cook, add a few lit
coals from a chimney rather than raw charcoal, which smokes as it lights.

*Carryover outdoors.* Wind, cold and an open lid change it. Probe the bird in the thickest part of the thigh and of the
breast: both must read 75 °C (assessment §9). Predict and log the rise after the grill, but don't rely on it for safety.

*Crisp skin:* dry skin (salted the day before, uncovered in the fridge), and a minute over direct heat at the end if
needed.

**Practice:**
1. **A spatchcocked chicken (90 min active, 2 h in total).** Roast the bird of `FIR-3.M3.X1` indirectly; log the lid and
   the core every 10 minutes; probe thigh and breast.

**Look for:** the lid within ±15 °C of the target; 75 °C in thigh and breast; crisp, golden skin.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Burnt underneath | The bird over the coals | The cool zone; a drip tray |
| Hours longer than planned | Opening the lid constantly | Lid closed; check every 10 minutes |
| Undercooked thighs | Probing only the breast | Probe the thigh too: both 75 °C |

**Safety:** poultry to 75 °C in thigh and breast (assessment §9); the raw bird handled as in `POU-2.M1`, with its own

### FIR-3.M3.L2 — Wood smoke as a flavour

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 h · builds on `FIR-3.M3.L1` · moves TS-FIRE→3, FL-ARCH→3*

*Objectives:* [K] clean versus heavy smoke; wood chunks, chips and planks; [S] add smoke to a roast or to vegetables; [J] how much is enough
<!-- END generated:lesson-fir-3-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a little clean smoke makes food taste of the fire; too much, or the wrong kind, makes it bitter.
Smoke is a seasoning, and like salt it has to be measured.

**You need:** one dry hardwood chunk (beech, oak, apple or cherry); onions, beetroot or celeriac for two trays; the
indirect fire; the tasting form.

**Explanation:**

*Clean against heavy smoke.* Thin, blue, almost invisible smoke tastes sweet and aromatic. Thick white or grey smoke,
from smouldering, damp or starved wood, tastes bitter and acrid.

*Wood:*
- **chunks:** slow, steady smoke;
- **chips:** quick and short;
- **planks:** gentle, for fish;
- dry hardwoods only: beech, oak, and fruit woods (apple, cherry) are mild; never treated or resinous wood.

*Using it:* a chunk or two on the coals at the start of an indirect cook, with the vents open enough for a clean burn.
Smoke is absorbed best by cool, moist surfaces, so most of the flavour goes on early.

*The judgment: how much is enough?* A little smoke is a seasoning; too much makes food bitter and dominates the dish.
Start with one chunk, taste the result, and add a second next time only if the tasters ask for more.

*Not preservation.* Hot smoke here flavours food that is cooked to its §9 figure. Cold smoking and smoked cures are
outside the program.

**Practice:**
1. **Smoked vegetables (90 min active, 2 h in total).** Roast onions, beetroot or celeriac indirectly with one chunk of
   wood, against the same vegetables roasted without smoke (in the oven, or before the chunk goes on). Taste them side
   by side, and judge the smoke on the form.

**Look for:** thin blue smoke; a smoky note the tasters notice but don't find harsh.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Bitter, acrid food | Thick white smoke for hours | One chunk; vents open for a clean burn |
| Sour smoke | Wet chips smouldering | Dry chunks on hot coals |
| Hardly any smoke flavour | Smoke added at the end only | At the start, while the food is cool |

**Safety:** only untreated hardwood; smoke kept away from neighbours' windows and from your own open doors.

**Record:** the tasting form.

### FIR-3.M4.L1 — Low-and-slow principles

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m4-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SLW-3.M1.L1`, `SAF-3.M1.L1` · moves SC-PROT→4, SA-HRPR→4*

*Objectives:* [K] collagen and time, the stall and evaporation, bark, rubs, mops and wrapping; [K] long-cook safety: keep the meat out of the danger zone, rest, cool and reheat safely
<!-- END generated:lesson-fir-3-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a pork shoulder cooked for a day is the slowest dish in the program, and the one where the clock
lies most: the core stops rising for hours. Knowing why, and where the risks are, keeps you calm and safe.

**You need:** the journal; your notes from `SLW-3.M1` and `SAF-3.M1.L1`.

**Explanation:**

*Collagen and time* (`SLW-3.M1`). A pork shoulder or ribs cook at a grill temperature of 110–130 °C for many hours,
until the collagen has melted and the meat pulls apart: a core around 90–95 °C for pulled pork, and ribs that bend and
crack on the surface.

*The stall.* The core stops rising for hours, around 65–75 °C, because evaporation from the surface cools the meat as
fast as the fire heats it. It ends by itself, or sooner when the meat is wrapped.

*Bark:* the dark crust from the rub, the smoke and the drying surface. Wrapping softens it.

*Rubs, mops and wrapping:*
- a rub of salt, sugar and spices, ideally the day before;
- a vinegar mop to keep the surface moist;
- wrapping in paper or foil to push through the stall.

*Long-cook safety* (assessment §9):
- the meat goes on cold from the fridge onto a fire already at temperature, and the cook is never paused and resumed;
- the finished meat is far above every §9 cooking figure;
- **resting and holding:** wrapped, in a warm, insulated box, held at 57 °C or above, checked with the probe;
- **leftovers:** cooled by the standard (57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, to 5 °C within 6 hours), kept at most 2 days or
  frozen, and reheated until steaming hot, once.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): plan a timeline for a 3.5 kg shoulder to be served at 19:00, working back from
serving: the rest, the cook with a stall of several hours, the fire, and the rub the day before. Mark each control point
from the safety list above, and the figure for each.

**Look for:** you can explain the stall, and plan a timeline that allows for it.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Tough, dry meat | Raising the fire to "beat" the stall | Wait, or wrap |
| Chewy shoulder | Pulling the shoulder at 75 °C because the timer says so | Cook to texture, around 90–95 °C |
| A food-safety risk | A shoulder left warm on the counter overnight | Hold at 57 °C or cool by the standard |

**Safety:** this lesson is the long-cook part of `SA-HRPR`: the start, the holding at 57 °C and the cooling standard of
assessment §9.

**Record:** the timeline with its control points.

### FIR-3.M4.L2 — Pork shoulder or ribs

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m4-l2 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 10 h · builds on `FIR-3.M4.L1` · moves TS-FIRE→3, TS-CMB→4*

*Objectives:* [S] trim, rub, cook at a steady 110–130 °C, wrap or not, rest; [J] judge doneness by probe feel, not only by temperature
<!-- END generated:lesson-fir-3-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** this is the cook itself: a whole day of holding a fire steady, with the patience to wait out the
stall and the judgment to stop on feel, not on the number.

**You need:** the shoulder or ribs of `FIR-3.M4.X1`; the rub; wood chunks; plenty of charcoal and the chimney; a spray
bottle of vinegar mop; butcher's paper or foil; an insulated box and towels; a two-probe thermometer if you have one;
the log.

**Explanation:**

*Trimming:* remove excess hard fat and loose bits. For ribs, pull off the membrane on the bone side.

*The cook:*
1. Rub the meat the day before.
2. Set an indirect fire with a chunk of wood, and hold it at 110–130 °C.
3. Add lit coals from a chimney as needed; log the fire and the meat every 30–60 minutes.
4. Mop or spray if the surface dries; wrap at the stall if the timeline needs it.
5. Finish when the probe slides in like into soft butter.
6. Rest it wrapped for at least an hour, held at 57 °C or above.

*The judgment: doneness by probe feel.* The temperature guides; the feel decides. A probe that meets resistance means
more time, whatever the number says.

*Timing:* ribs cook in roughly 5–6 hours; a shoulder of 3–4 kg in 10–12 hours. Start early: a shoulder that is ready
early can be held, but one that is late can't be hurried.

**Practice:**
1. **The cook (3 h active over about 10 h).** Cook the shoulder or ribs of `FIR-3.M4.X1`, logging the fire and the meat,
   and marking the stall.

**Look for:** a steady 110–130 °C; a dark bark; probe-tender; meat that pulls apart in long strands.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dry or uneven meat | Fire swinging between 90 and 180 °C | Small vent changes; lit coals added |
| A cook that never ends | Opening the lid too often | Check by the probe, not by looking |
| Juice everywhere, dry meat | Slicing a shoulder straight off the grill | Rest it wrapped for an hour |

**Safety:** the meat cold onto a hot fire; never paused; held at 57 °C or above; the fire never left alone, and the
grill cooled before it is covered or moved.

**Record:** the fire and meat log, with the stall marked.

### FIR-3.M4.L3 — Barbecue sauces and sides

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m4-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** barbecue is rich, fatty and sweet. The plate needs acid and crunch to stay a pleasure after the

**Safety:** a sauce brush used on raw meat is not used on cooked meat; the sauces are kept in the fridge and used

### FIR-3.M5.L1 — Cooking in the embers

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-fir-3-m5-l1 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `VEG-1.M4.L1` · moves TS-FIRE→3, TS-VEG→4*

*Objectives:* [S] ember-roasted vegetables (aubergine, onion, beetroot, potato), steak on the coals, a plancha or skillet over the fire; [J] when the char is flavour and when it is burnt
<!-- END generated:lesson-fir-3-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the oldest way of cooking is laying food in the fire. An onion or a beetroot roasted in the embers
tastes sweet and smoky in a way no oven gives, and the fire that cooked dinner does it for free.

**You need:** a fire burnt down to glowing embers; whole onions, beetroot and a potato; a thick steak and lump charcoal,
if you will cook it on the coals; a cast-iron skillet; mushrooms and a small fish; long tongs; a skewer; a dressing.

**Explanation:**

*In the embers.* Whole vegetables with a protective skin (onions, beetroot, potatoes, celeriac, aubergine, leeks) are
laid straight in hot coals that have burnt down. Turn them now and then, until soft to a skewer. The burnt skin is
peeled away to reveal smoky, sweet flesh.

*Steak on the coals.* A thick steak laid directly on glowing lump charcoal, the ash blown off first, and turned once. It
follows the steak row of assessment §9: intact, with every surface seared.

*A plancha or skillet over the fire:* a cast-iron pan on the grate, for food that would fall through or needs fat: small
fish, mushrooms, eggs, flatbread with oil.

*The judgment: char as flavour, or burnt?*
- **flavour:** char on a skin that is peeled away, or a thin blistering;
- **a fault:** black, bitter char on the surface you eat.

*Sizes and times.* Choose vegetables big enough to survive the coals: a large onion or beetroot takes the better part of
an hour, a small one turns to cinders. Test with a skewer through the skin.

**Practice:**
1. **Ember vegetables (60 min).** Roast onions, beetroot and a potato in the embers; peel them and dress them.
2. **Plancha (60 min).** Cook mushrooms and a small fish on the skillet over the fire; probe the fish to 63 °C.

**Look for:** soft to a skewer through the skin; smoky, sweet flesh with no bitter edge.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Burnt skins, raw centre | Embers still flaming | Wait until the flames have died down |
| Bitter mouthfuls | Eating the burnt skin | Peel it away |
| Nothing left | Vegetables too small, burnt to cinders | Large, whole vegetables |

**Safety:** long tongs and gloves for the coals; the hot skillet handled with a dry cloth or glove, and set down on a
non-flammable surface; fish to 63 °C for 15 seconds (assessment §9).

**Record:** photos and the skewer times.

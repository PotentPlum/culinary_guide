**Before you start.** In S2 you braised a first stew and roasted joints to a core temperature. This course is about
**time** as the main tool:
- why tough, collagen-rich cuts become tender only with long, moist, gentle heat, and how to choose the cut and the
  method for the time you have;
- the families of braise (brown, white, vegetable and legume), the pot-roast, and the braising liquid turned into a
  finished sauce;
- the same dish moved between the pot, the pressure cooker, the slow cooker and a low oven, with the changes predicted;
- **sous vide:** precise temperature, equilibrium cooking, and the difference between doneness and pasteurisation.

*Safety is part of the method, not a footnote.* The low-temperature cooks follow the time-temperature tables of
[assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment), taught in `SAF-3.M1.L3`:
- **the poultry table** for chicken and duck, nothing below 60 °C;
- **the steak row** for intact beef steaks, **the roast table** for whole roasts, and 63 °C for 15 seconds for other
  intact meat and fish;
- **the fish and risk-group rows** for anything served below those.

A cook below a table is a gate *Fail*, and is repeated. Food cooked ahead is cooled by the standard of `SAF-3.M1.L1` and
kept as leftovers, at most 2 days.

The cuts are Dutch: beef shin (*schenkel*), cheek (*wang*), chuck and brisket; pork shoulder (*schouder*) and belly;
lamb shoulder and neck; chicken legs. The vegetables are seasonal: leeks, fennel, savoy and red cabbage, celeriac and
root vegetables in winter, and beans from `LEG-2`. Plan four to five weeks: the braises run in the background on weekend
afternoons, and each feeds the household twice.

**Have ready:** a heavy lidded casserole (*braadpan*); the probe; a fine sieve, a ladle and a skimmer; the pressure
cooker, if you have one; a slow cooker, if you have one (the low oven does the same); for sous vide, a circulator if you
have one, or a heavy pot for the stove-top method; zip-top freezer bags or a vacuum sealer; closed-cell foam tape; the
cooling log, the sous vide log and the control-point plan of `SAF-3.M2.X1`; the journal.

### SLW-3.M1.L1 — Collagen to gelatin

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m1-l1 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M2.L1` · moves SC-PROT→4*

*Objectives:* [K] time and temperature; why braised meat can be dry and tender at once
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a fillet steak needs five minutes and a shin three hours, and cooking them the other way round ruins
both. The reason is two proteins that react to heat in opposite ways; once you know them, you can cook any cut.

**You need:** the journal; your notes from `SCI-2.M2`.

**Explanation:**

*Two kinds of muscle protein, two behaviours* (`SCI-2.M2`):
- **the muscle fibres** (myosin and actin) tighten and squeeze out water as they heat: from about 50 °C, and more and
  more above about 65–70 °C;
- **the collagen** of the connective tissue shrinks at about 60–65 °C, and only slowly dissolves into gelatin: faster
  the hotter it is, but over hours.

A tender cut has little collagen, so it is cooked quickly and only as far as the fibres allow. A tough cut has a lot, so
it needs hours for the collagen to dissolve, and the fibres will be fully cooked by then.

*Why braised meat can be dry and tender at once.* By the time the collagen has melted, the fibres have lost much of
their water. The gelatin coats them and thickens the sauce, so the meat feels moist. Cooked too long or too hot, the
fibres turn stringy and dry all the same.

*Time and temperature together.* The same tenderness comes from a shorter cook at a livelier simmer or a longer one at a
gentler heat. A gentle oven around 140–160 °C, or a liquid that barely trembles, gives the widest window between tender
and stringy. A hard boil narrows it to minutes.

*Resting in the liquid.* Cooling in the braising liquid lets the meat take some of it back. A braise is often better the
next day: cooled by the standard of `SAF-3.M1.L1`, stored, and reheated until hot throughout.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): for four cuts (a fillet, a chuck, a shin and a chicken leg), write which protein
decides the cooking, the method, and roughly how long. Then explain in a paragraph, from the two proteins, why a shin
needs three hours and a fillet five minutes. Keep it for `SLW-3.M1.X1`.

**Look for:** you can explain from the two proteins why a shin needs three hours and a fillet five minutes.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stringy, dry meat | A hard boil | A liquid that barely trembles, or a gentle oven |
| Dry edges on the meat | Lifting the meat out and leaving it to dry | Rest and cool it in the liquid |
| Tough or falling apart | Judging by time only | Test with a fork from the planned time |

**Safety:** a braise cooled for the next day follows the standard of `SAF-3.M1.L1`.

**Record:** the four cuts and the paragraph.

### SLW-3.M1.L2 — Choosing cuts and methods

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m1-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 0 practical sessions · builds on `MEA-2.M2.L1` · moves IN-MEA→3, CK-METH→4*

*Objectives:* [J] shin, cheek, shoulder, brisket, legs; method by cut and by time available
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** the cheapest cuts make the best braises, if you match them to the right method and the time you
have. This lesson is the judgment behind every braise of the course.

**You need:** the journal; a butcher's price list or a supermarket's meat shelf, in person or online.

**Explanation:**

*The braising cuts, and what they give:*

| Animal | Cut | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| Beef | Shin | Gelatinous; falls apart |
| Beef | Cheek | Silky, very gelatinous |
| Beef | Chuck, brisket | Beefy; slices or shreds |
| Beef | Oxtail | Rich, gelatinous, on the bone |
| Pork | Shoulder | Forgiving; shreds |
| Pork | Belly | Rich and layered |
| Pork | Hock | Gelatinous, for long cooks and stocks |
| Lamb | Shoulder, neck | Sweet and fatty |
| Chicken, duck | Legs | Shorter braises, about an hour |

*The judgment: method by cut.*
- a large piece of shoulder or brisket pot-roasts or slow-roasts;
- shin and cheek braise in liquid;
- cubed chuck stews;
- chicken legs braise briefly.

*The judgment: method by the time you have.*
- a weekday: the pressure cooker, or chicken legs;
- a weekend afternoon: a braise;
- a day at work: a slow cooker;
- overnight: a low oven.

*Buying.* The cuts are cheap. Ask the butcher, and buy on the bone where possible, because the bone adds gelatin and
flavour. Look for marbling and visible connective tissue: they are what the long cook turns into tenderness.

**Practice:** theory only (1.5 h): for six situations (for example "beef cheek, Saturday afternoon"; "pork shoulder,
out all day"; "chicken legs, Tuesday at 18:00"), name a method and the expected time, with the reason. Then choose three
cuts you have never cooked (for example oxtail, pork cheek and lamb neck) for `SLW-3.M1.X1`, and check the price of
each.

**Look for:** for a given cut and the time available, you name a method and the expected time.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A dry braise | A lean cut (topside) in a long braise, which dries | A cut with collagen and fat |
| Chewy, rubbery meat | A shin in a quick stir-fry | Shin needs hours of moist heat |
| Dinner at midnight | Ignoring the time available | Choose the method by the time you have |

**Safety:** none beyond the usual raw-meat rules (`MEA-2`).

**Record:** the six situations and the three cuts.

### SLW-3.M2.L1 — Brown braise

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m2-l1 -->
*3 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 h · builds on `MEA-2.M6.L1` · moves TS-CMB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] sear, deglaze, braise in wine or stock; skim; [J] doneness
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** the brown braise is the backbone of winter cooking in the Low Countries: *hachee*, *stoverij*,
*carbonade flamande*. It is a sequence of steps, each adding flavour, that works for any collagen-rich cut.

**You need:** the cut for `SLW-3.M2.X1`; onions and seasonal aromatics; tomato paste if you use it; wine, beer or cider;
stock; a *bouquet garni*; the casserole and its lid; baking paper; a ladle; a fork.

**Explanation:**

*The steps:*
1. Dry and season the meat. Sear it in batches until deeply brown (`MEA-2.M4`), and set it aside.
2. Brown the aromatics. Add tomato paste if used, and fry it brick-red.
3. Deglaze with wine, beer or cider, scraping up the browning, and reduce.
4. Return the meat. Add stock to come about two-thirds of the way up, and the *bouquet garni*.
5. Cover, and cook gently in a 140–160 °C oven, turning the meat once.
6. Skim the fat, and check with a fork from the planned time.

*Liquid.* Too much dilutes the sauce and boils the meat; too little scorches. A sheet of baking paper pressed onto the
surface under the lid (a *cartouche*) slows evaporation.

*The judgment: doneness.* A fork slides in and comes out without lifting the meat. The meat pulls apart along its
fibres, but holds its shape.

*Regional examples:*
- *hachee*: Dutch beef and onions with vinegar and bay;
- *carbonade flamande*: beef in beer;
- *stoverij*: the Flemish beef stew;
- *bœuf bourguignon*: beef in red wine.

**Practice:**
1. **A brown braise (2 h active, 4 h in total).** Cook the braise of `SLW-3.M2.X1` by the steps above, with the time
   predicted.

**Look for:** a deep brown sear; a liquid that barely trembles; meat that is fork-tender and holds its shape.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Grey meat, no sear | A crowded pan that steams | Sear in batches, with space |
| Boiled, stringy meat | The meat covered with liquid | Two-thirds up; a *cartouche* |
| A greasy sauce | The fat not skimmed | Skim, or chill and lift it off |

**Safety:** oil spatters when wet meat goes in: dry it first and lay it away from you. The heavy lid and pot are lifted
with dry cloths, and the lid opened away from your face.

**Record:** the plan, the predicted and actual times, and a photo.

### SLW-3.M2.L2 — White braise

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m2-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 3 h · builds on `SAU-2.M3.L3` · moves TS-CMB→3*

*Objectives:* [S] *fricassée* or *blanquette*: no browning, a velvety sauce
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a white braise gives a pale, velvety dish with a delicate flavour: the *fricassée* and the
*blanquette* of French home cooking. It practises the opposite skill to the brown braise: cooking without colour.

**You need:** chicken legs or pork shoulder in pieces; mushrooms and leeks; butter; flour; white stock; cream; lemon; a
*bouquet garni*; the casserole; a fine sieve; the probe.

**Explanation:**

*No browning.* The meat (veal, chicken, rabbit or pork) is blanched or sweated without colour, so the sauce stays pale.

*Two classics:*
- ***blanquette*:** the meat simmered in white stock with aromatics; the liquid thickened at the end with a *roux*
  (`SAU-2.M3`), and finished with cream and egg yolk (a *liaison*). The sauce is brought back to at least 71 °C on the
  probe without boiling once the yolks go in (the egg-thickened row of assessment §9);
- ***fricassée*:** the meat sweated in butter without colour, dusted with flour, then simmered in stock and finished
  with cream.

*The velvety sauce.* Strain it, reduce it and skim it, and season it with a few drops of lemon. Scum that is not skimmed
turns it grey and cloudy.

*Doneness:* chicken to 75 °C at the core (the poultry row of assessment §9); pork and veal until tender, tested with a
fork like a brown braise.

**Practice:**
1. **A *fricassée* (90 min active, 3 h in total).** Make a chicken-leg or pork *fricassée* with mushrooms and leeks.
   Probe the chicken in the thickest part, and bring the sauce to *nappe*: a line drawn through it on the back of a
   spoon stays clean.

**Look for:** an ivory sauce at *nappe*; no colour on the meat; if you made a *liaison*, smooth, not scrambled.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A beige, muddy sauce | Browning the meat | Sweat without colour, on lower heat |
| Scrambled sauce | Boiling after the liaison | Heat to 71 °C on the probe, never boil |
| A grey, cloudy sauce | Unskimmed scum | Skim during the simmer; strain at the end |

**Safety:** chicken to 75 °C at the core; an egg *liaison* to at least 71 °C (assessment §9).

**Record:** the probe readings and a photo of the sauce on a spoon.

### SLW-3.M2.L3 — Braising vegetables and legumes

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m2-l3 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 2 h · builds on `LEG-2.M1.L2` · moves TS-CMB→4*

*Objectives:* [S] fennel, leeks, cabbage, beans; [J] adapt time and liquid
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m2-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** braising turns sturdy winter vegetables silky and sweet, with a glaze of their own juices. With
beans, a vegetable braise becomes a main course.

**You need:** chicory (*witlof*) or fennel; savoy or red cabbage; cooked beans from `LEG-2`; an apple; spices (clove,
bay or juniper); butter; stock; a wide lidded pan; a knife for testing.

**Explanation:**

*Vegetables that braise well:* fennel, leeks, chicory, celery, savoy and red cabbage, celeriac, carrots, onions, endive.
All are sturdy, with a structure that softens into silkiness.

*The method:*
1. Brown the vegetables, or don't, for a white braise.
2. Add a little liquid: stock, wine, or butter and water.
3. Cover, and cook gently until tender.
4. Uncover, and reduce the liquid to a glaze.

*Legumes in a braise.* Cooked beans (`LEG-2.M1`; dried common beans always cooked first by the bean row of assessment
§9) go in for the last 30–60 minutes, so they take on flavour without bursting.

*The judgment: time and liquid.* Adapt both to the vegetable's density and the size of the cut. A dense celeriac wedge
needs longer than half a leek; cut pieces of the same vegetable to the same size, so they are done together. A
vegetable that releases water (cabbage, chicory) needs less liquid than one that doesn't (celeriac).

**Practice:**
1. **Braised chicory or fennel (45 min).** Halve and brown it; braise it with butter and stock until tender; uncover and
   glaze.
2. **A cabbage and bean braise (75 min).** Braise savoy or red cabbage with apple and spices; add the cooked beans for
   the last part. Predict the time and the liquid, and log both.

**Look for:** tender to a knife, holding shape; a glossy glaze, not a puddle.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Boiled, watery vegetables | Too much liquid, so the vegetables boil | A little liquid; uncover to reduce |
| Mushy, split beans | Beans added at the start and burst | The last 30–60 minutes |
| Some hard, some soft | Uneven pieces | Cut to the same size |

**Safety:** dried kidney and other common beans are soaked and boiled by the bean row of assessment §9 before they go
into a braise.

**Record:** the predicted and actual times and liquid; photos.

### SLW-3.M3.L1 — Pot-roasting

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 h · builds on `SLW-3.M2.L1` · moves TS-CMB→4*

*Objectives:* [S] a whole joint or bird in a covered pot; [K] how it differs from a braise
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a pot-roast gives a juicy, sliceable joint and its sauce from one pot, with little work. It sits
between a roast and a braise, and is judged by the probe like a roast.

**You need:** a whole chicken or a pork loin; leeks and root vegetables; butter or oil; the casserole and its lid; the
probe; a timer; the log.

**Explanation:**

*A pot-roast* (*poêlé*) cooks a whole joint or bird in a covered pot with little liquid, on a bed of aromatics and fat,
in a moderate oven. The meat steams and roasts at once.

*How it differs from a braise:*
- little liquid: a few centimetres, or only the juices;
- often a tender or medium cut (a whole chicken, a pork loin, a rolled shoulder), served sliced and juicy;
- the lid comes off at the end to colour the skin.

*Doneness by the figures of assessment §9:*
- a chicken to 75 °C in the thickest part of the thigh;
- pork to 63 °C for 15 seconds;
- a rolled shoulder cooked longer, until tender.

*Carryover in a pot* (`HEA-2.M2`). A covered pot holds heat, so the core keeps rising after the pot leaves the oven.
Predict the rise, and log the core every 5 minutes to see whether you were right.

*Dutch examples:* pork pot-roasted with apple and sage; *gestoofde kip* in a *braadpan*.

**Practice:**
1. **A pot-roast (90 min active, 4 h in total).** Pot-roast a whole chicken or a pork loin on leeks and root vegetables.
   Before it comes out, write the carryover you expect. Log the core every 5 minutes after the oven.

**Look for:** juicy slices; coloured skin; the carryover predicted within 2 °C.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A pale, boiled joint | Too much liquid, so the joint boils | A few centimetres at most |
| No colour | The lid on throughout | The lid off for the last part |
| Undercooked chicken | Pulling a chicken below 75 °C and hoping | Back in the oven; probe again |

**Safety:** a chicken that reads below 75 °C in the thigh after resting goes back into the oven and is probed again
(the poultry row of assessment §9).

**Record:** the carryover log and the prediction.

### SLW-3.M3.L2 — From braising liquid to sauce

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m3-l2 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SAU-2.M2.L2` · moves TS-SAU→4*

*Objectives:* [S] degrease, reduce, thicken or mount; [J] balance gelatin, acid and salt
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** the liquid in the pot is half the dish. Degreased, reduced and balanced, it becomes a sauce that
coats the meat; left as it is, it is a greasy puddle.

**You need:** the liquid of `SLW-3.M2.X1` or of the pot-roast; a ladle; a fine sieve; a wide pan; cornflour or the
braised vegetables; cold butter; vinegar or wine; a spoon for *nappe*.

**Explanation:**

*The four steps:*
1. **Degrease:** skim the fat with a ladle, or chill the liquid and lift off the set fat (the next-day braise).
2. **Reduce:** strain, then reduce in a wide pan to concentrate. Taste as it goes, because salt concentrates too.
3. **Thicken or mount** (`SAU-2.M2`):
   - by reduction alone, if the liquid is rich in gelatin;
   - with a little starch slurry, or the puréed vegetables of the braise;
   - with cold butter whisked in at the end for gloss (*monter au beurre*).
4. **Balance,** then salt last.

*The judgment: gelatin, acid and salt.* A gelatin-rich sauce coats well but can feel sticky on the lips. A few drops of
vinegar or wine lift it. Salt is adjusted last, after reducing, because a sauce salted early ends too salty.

*The target: nappe.* A line drawn with a finger through the sauce on the back of a spoon stays clean. Test it often

### SLW-3.M4.L1 — Pressure cooking

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m4-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SAF-3.M2.L2` · moves SC-HEAT→4, RD-ADPT→3*

*Objectives:* [K] pressure and boiling point; [S] safe use; [J] adapt times and liquid
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a pressure cooker turns a weekend braise into a weekday dinner. It changes more than the time,
though, and used carelessly it is one of the hazards of `SAF-3.M2.L2`.

**You need:** a pressure cooker and its manual; the cut and aromatics of `SLW-3.M2.X1`; a chicken carcass or soaked
chickpeas; the probe; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Pressure and boiling point.* Sealed, the pot builds pressure, and water boils hotter: about 115–120 °C at full
pressure. Collagen dissolves much faster, so a braise takes about a third of the time.

*What changes:*
- almost no evaporation, so less liquid (the minimum the manual names) and a thinner sauce, reduced after opening;
- browning still happens in the open pot, before the lid goes on;
- flavours meld less than in a long braise; a short simmer with the lid off helps.

*Safe use* (`SAF-3.M2.L2`):
- never more than two-thirds full, and half for beans and grains, which foam;
- the valve checked before use;
- the pressure released as the manual says: usually natural release for meat and foaming foods;
- the lid never forced.

*The judgment: adapting a recipe.*
- the time to about a third;
- the liquid to the minimum;
- vegetables that would turn to mush added after a first cook, under pressure again for a few minutes or simmered with
  the lid off.

**Practice:**
1. **A pressure-cooked braise (60 min).** Adapt the braise of `SLW-3.M2.X1` to the pressure cooker; predict the time and
   the liquid, then cook it and reduce the sauce after opening.
2. **Stock or beans (45 min).** Make a chicken stock or cook soaked chickpeas in the pressure cooker, and compare it
   with the pot version you made in S2.

**Look for:** tender in about a third of the time; the sauce reduced to *nappe* after opening.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Foam from the valve | Overfilling | Two-thirds at most; half for beans |
| Food sprays out | A quick release on a foaming pot | Natural release, as the manual says |
| A watery dish | The sauce served thin | Reduce it with the lid off |

**Safety:** the fill line, the valve check and the release as the manual says (`SAF-3.M2.L2`); the lid opened away from
your face.

**Record:** the predicted and actual times and liquid; the comparison with the pot.

### SLW-3.M4.L2 — Slow cooker and low oven

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m4-l2 -->
*1.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 8 h · builds on `SAF-3.M1.L1` · moves RD-ADPT→3*

*Objectives:* [J] adapt a braise to a slow cooker or a low oven; [K] the safety limits
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a slow cooker or a low oven cooks while you work or sleep. Both are gentle, which is good for
collagen and has safety limits you need to know.

**You need:** a slow cooker, or a heavy lidded casserole for a low oven; the braise of `SLW-3.M2.X1`, browned first in a
pan; the probe; the cooling log.

**Explanation:**

*The slow cooker* heats a ceramic pot gently for 4–10 hours. Little evaporates, so use less liquid; it doesn't brown, so
brown the meat and aromatics first in a pan.

*The safety limits* (assessment §9):
- the food must still end at the §9 figures: poultry 75 °C; braises until tender and hot throughout;
- dried common beans (kidney beans and their relatives) are never cooked in a slow cooker, which may not get hot enough
  (the bean row of §9): they are soaked and boiled first by the bean rule;
- frozen meat is thawed in the fridge first, so that it doesn't spend hours in the growth range while it slowly heats;
- leftovers are cooled by the standard, not left in the switched-off pot.

*The low oven,* at about 120–150 °C, does the same job with a heavy lidded casserole, and is often better: the oven
browns the top, and the heat is even.

*The judgment: adapting a braise.*
- less liquid than on the stove;
- the time roughly doubled on low;
- thicken at the end, by reducing the liquid in a pan.

**Practice:**
1. **A low-oven braise (30 min active, 8 h in total).** Adapt the braise of `SLW-3.M2.X1` to a low oven overnight, or to
   the slow cooker; predict the liquid and the time. In the morning, check that it is tender and hot throughout, then
   cool what you won't eat by the standard and log it.

**Look for:** the liquid level as predicted; the meat tender and hot throughout at the end.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A toxic dish | Dried kidney beans in the slow cooker | Soak and boil them first, by the §9 bean rule |
| Hours in the growth range | Frozen meat straight in | Thaw it in the fridge first |
| A watery sauce | A sauce left unreduced | Reduce it in a pan at the end |

**Safety:** the bean rule, the thawing rule and the cooling standard of assessment §9, as above.

**Record:** the prediction, the morning check, and the cooling log.

## SLW-3.M5 — Sous vide

### SLW-3.M5.L1 — Sous vide principles

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m5-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 h · builds on `SAF-3.M1.L3` · moves SC-HEAT→4, TS-MOD→1*

*Objectives:* [K] precise temperature, equilibrium, time versus pasteurisation; the stove-top method without a circulator
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** sous vide gives a doneness no pan can match, edge to edge. It also cooks below the usual core
temperatures, which is safe only by a table. This lesson teaches both halves.

**You need:** eggs; the circulator, or a heavy pot and the instant-read thermometer for the stove-top method; zip-top
bags; foam tape; the sous vide log; a timer.

**Explanation:**

*Equilibrium cooking.* The food sits in water at the exact temperature you want at its core, so it can't overcook past
that point. Doneness depends on the temperature; the time makes it hot through and, for safety, keeps it long enough at
that temperature.

*Time to the core.* The thicker the piece, the longer it takes to reach the bath temperature: roughly with the square of
the thickness, so twice as thick takes about four times as long. A probe pushed through a piece of closed-cell foam tape
on the bag shows when the core is there, without letting water in.

*Doneness against pasteurisation* (`SAF-3.M1.L3`, assessment §9). At 60 °C a chicken breast has its texture as soon as
the core gets there, but it is safe only after 35 minutes at that core temperature (the poultry table). Hotter means
shorter: at 65.6 °C, 5 minutes. The time starts when the core reaches the bath, not when the bag goes in.

*Bags:* food-safe zip bags with the air pressed out by lowering them into water, or vacuum bags. No metal clips touching
the food.

*Without a circulator:* a heavy pot of water on the lowest flame, the thermometer in the water, and the heat adjusted
every few minutes to stay within ±1 °C. It works for cooks of up to about an hour; a longer cook needs a circulator.

*The program's limits:* poultry from 60 °C upwards; other foods by their §9 row; nothing kept warm in the bath after its
time.

**Practice:**
1. **Eggs (60 min active, 2 h in total).** Cook eggs at 63 and 65 °C, and compare the yolks and whites. Hold the bath on
   the stove-top or with a circulator, and log its temperature every 5 minutes.

**Look for:** the water within ±1 °C of the target throughout (the log); the yolks as you predicted.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A bag that floats, uneven cooking | Air pockets in the bag | Press the air out in water; weigh it down |
| Undercooked, unsafe food | Starting the clock when the bag goes in | Time from when the core reaches the bath |
| The wrong doneness | A bath that drifts 3 °C | Check and adjust every few minutes |

**Safety:** soft eggs from raw eggs are not served to risk groups (assessment §9).

**Record:** the bath log; photos of the eggs.

### SLW-3.M5.L3 — Finishing and vegetables

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-slw-3-m5-l3 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SLW-3.M5.L2` · moves TS-DRY→4, JD-CUES→4*

*Objectives:* [S] dry and sear after the bath without overcooking; vegetables sous vide; [J] when sous vide is worth it
<!-- END generated:lesson-slw-3-m5-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** sous vide meat out of the bag is grey and soft-skinned; the sear makes the dish, and done badly it
undoes the precision of the bath. Vegetables, cooked the same way, keep a flavour boiling washes out.

**You need:** a steak or a chicken breast from the bath; a bowl of ice water; paper towel; a heavy pan or a hot grill;
carrots or beetroot; butter and salt; bags; a ruler for the grey band.

**Explanation:**

*Finishing without overcooking:*
- out of the bag, and patted very dry, because a wet surface steams instead of searing;
- seared in a very hot pan (or under a hot grill) for the shortest time possible, about 30–60 seconds a side;
- the edges seared too, for a steak (the steak row);
- chilling the surface briefly in ice water before searing gives more time for a crust without a grey band.

*Vegetables sous vide.* At about 83–85 °C plant cell walls soften. Carrots, beetroot, celeriac and leeks cooked in the
bag with butter and salt keep their flavour and colour, and are firmer and more intense than boiled.

*The judgment: when is sous vide worth it?*
- **yes:** thick, even pieces where precision matters (a thick steak, a chicken breast, a pork tenderloin); cooking
  ahead for guests; dense vegetables;
- **no:** thin pieces; dishes led by browning; braises that want reduction.

**Practice:**
1. **Finishing (45 min).** Sear one steak or breast straight from the bath, and another chilled first in ice water.
   Cut both and measure the grey band under the crust.
2. **Vegetables (60 min).** Cook carrots or beetroot sous vide, and compare them with the same vegetable roasted and
   boiled, for texture and colour.

**Look for:** a deep crust in under a minute a side; no grey band, or one under 2 mm.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No crust, grey surface | A wet surface that steams | Pat it very dry |
| A thick grey band | Searing for minutes | A very hot pan, 30–60 seconds a side |
| Raw, crunchy vegetables | Vegetables at 60 °C, still raw | About 83–85 °C |

**Safety:** a very hot pan smokes: the extractor on, the meat laid away from you. The steak row asks for every surface
seared.

**Record:** the grey-band measurements; the vegetable comparison.

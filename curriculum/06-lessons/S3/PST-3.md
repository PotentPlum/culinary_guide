**Before you start.** `BAK-2` taught short pastry, cakes, pastry cream and the first tart. This course adds the pastry
work most home bakers avoid because it seems difficult, and shows that it is mostly temperature and timing:
- **choux:** a cooked paste leavened by steam, for *éclairs*, profiteroles and savoury *gougères*;
- **laminated dough:** how layers of butter and dough rise, made as rough puff for tarts, pies and *palmiers*;
- **tarts:** fruit tarts, baked fillings and savoury pies, all with a crisp base;
- **creams, ganache and mousses:** *crème anglaise* variations, ganache as an emulsion, mousses set with gelatine or
  chocolate;
- **chocolate:** types, melting without seizing, and tempering by the seed method;
- **planning:** a dessert built from components made over three days.

The method follows the CIA's baking and pastry fundamentals [S9]: weighed formulas, temperatures checked with a
thermometer, each element judged in cross-section. The fruit is Dutch and seasonal: rhubarb and strawberries in early
summer, plums in late summer, apples and pears from autumn. Chocolate, vanilla and citrus are pantry goods.

*Pastry's safety points* ([assessment §9](../../04-assessment.md#9-safety-figures-used-in-assessment)):
- pastry cream, custards and *crème anglaise* cooked to at least 71 °C, cooled by the cooling standard, kept at most
  2 days;
- anything with raw egg made with pasteurised egg for every guest, and kept covered in the fridge for at most 24 hours;
- cream fillings kept in the fridge, out of it for at most 2 hours.

Plan five to six weeks. Laminated dough and multi-day desserts need several short sessions over a few days; most bakes
are household desserts, or go into the freezer.

**Have ready:** the 1 g scale and the 0.1 g scale; the instant-read thermometer; piping bags with a plain round and a
star nozzle; the rolling pin; a tart tin or ring; a metal pie tin of about 22 cm; a heatproof bowl for a bain-marie; the
stick blender; glasses, ramekins or rings for mousses; a baking stone or steel (optional); a wire rack; baking paper and
baking beans; a pastry brush; the formula sheets; the journal.

### PST-3.M1.L1 — Choux paste

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m1-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `BAK-2.M1.L2` · moves TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] cook the *panade*, add eggs to the right consistency, pipe; [K] steam leavening
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m1-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** choux is the most surprising dough in pastry: a paste cooked in a pan that puffs into a hollow shell
with no yeast or baking powder. Once you can judge the egg, it is quick and reliable.

**You need:** for about 30 small choux: 125 g water, 125 g milk, 110 g butter, 4 g salt, 5 g sugar, 140 g plain flour,
about 250 g beaten egg (5 eggs, weighed); a heavy saucepan; a wooden spoon or a stand mixer; a piping bag with a plain
round nozzle; baking paper; the scale.

**Explanation:**

*The panade.* Bring the water, milk, butter, salt and sugar to the boil, the butter fully melted. Off the heat, add all
the flour at once and stir hard. Back on a medium heat, stir until the paste comes away from the pan and a thin film
forms on the bottom, about 2 minutes. Cooking the flour gelatinises the starch, so it can hold the steam later.

*Adding the eggs.* Move the paste to a bowl and let it cool slightly, a few minutes, so the eggs don't cook. Beat in the
egg in parts, each part fully absorbed before the next. Stop when the paste is glossy and falls from the spoon in a "V"
that holds. The amount varies with how much water evaporated in the pan, so it is judged, not fixed: you may not need
all of it.

*Steam leavening.* In a hot oven the water in the paste turns to steam and puffs the shell; the egg proteins and the
starch set around the hollow. That is why the oven door stays shut while the shells rise.

*Piping.* A plain round nozzle:
- **rounds:** the bag held upright, close to the tray, squeezed, then stopped and lifted;
- **éclairs:** the bag at an angle, piped in even lengths;
- **finish:** points smoothed with a wet finger; a thin egg wash.

**Practice:**
1. **The paste (60 min).** A batch of choux paste, the egg added to the "V"; piped into rounds and *éclair* lengths;
   baked (`PST-3.M1.L2`).
2. **Consistency (45 min).** One batch split three ways, with too little, just enough and too much egg; baked side by
   side and photographed.

**Look for:**
- a film on the pan base;
- a glossy "V" off the spoon;
- even piping.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A runny paste | All the egg added at once | Add it in parts; stop at the "V" |
| Shells that spread flat | Too much egg | Hold back the last egg; judge the "V" |
| Scrambled bits in the paste | Eggs into a hot paste | Cool the paste for a few minutes first |

**Safety:** the panade is hot and sticky: stir with a long spoon and keep your hand out of the steam. Choux contains
gluten, egg and milk (`SAF-1.M4`).

**Record:** the weight of egg used, photos of the "V" and the three consistencies baked.

### PST-3.M1.L2 — *Éclairs*, profiteroles and *gougères*

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m1-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `PST-3.M1.L1` · moves TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] shapes, baking and drying out; fillings; [J] collapse and hollowness
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m1-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a choux shell is only good if it is hollow, dry and crisp when it reaches the table. Baking,
drying and the moment of filling decide that.

**You need:** a batch of choux paste (`PST-3.M1.L1`); pastry cream (`BAK-2.M5`) or whipping cream; dark chocolate for a
glaze; aged Gouda for the *gougères*; piping bags; a small nozzle for filling; a wire rack; a serrated knife.

**Explanation:**

*Baking.* A hot oven, around 200 °C, to puff; then turned down to dry and set. Keep the door shut for the first two
thirds of the time: open it early and the steam escapes and the shells collapse. They are done when they are golden all
over, including the cracks, and feel light for their size.

*Drying out.* Pierce each shell underneath, or split it, and return it to the switched-off oven with the door ajar for
10 minutes, so the inside dries. The result is a hollow, crisp shell that doesn't go soft.

*Gougères.* Beat grated cheese (aged Gouda works well) into the paste, pipe small rounds, top with a little more
cheese, and bake. Serve warm, as a starter or with drinks.

*Fillings.* Pastry cream (`BAK-2.M5`), whipped cream, or a *crème diplomate* (pastry cream lightened with whipped
cream). Fill close to serving, from underneath with a small nozzle, so the shell stays crisp. Dip the tops in melted
chocolate or a glaze.

*Collapse and hollowness:*
- **collapsed shells:** underbaked, the door opened early, or too much egg;
- **doughy inside:** not dried out;
- **cracked, uneven shells:** the oven too hot or the paste too stiff;
- **small and dense:** too little egg, or an oven not hot enough.

**Practice:**
1. **Profiteroles and *éclairs* (75 min).** Baked, dried out, filled with pastry cream, glazed with chocolate.
2. ***Gougères* (60 min).** Cheese choux for a starter; one shell cut in cross-section and photographed.

**Look for:**
- golden, light for their size;
- hollow and dry inside;
- still crisp an hour after filling.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Collapsed shells | The oven opened at 10 minutes | Keep it shut for the first two thirds |
| Soft shells at the table | Filled hours ahead | Fill close to serving |
| Soft, wet insides | Not dried out | Pierce and dry in the cooling oven |

**Safety:** cream-filled pastries are kept in the fridge and out of it for at most 2 hours (§9); the pastry cream is
cooked to at least 71 °C and cooled by the standard (§9).

**Record:** cross-section photos, the baking times, and the time between filling and serving.

### PST-3.M2.L1 — How lamination works

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m2-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · builds on `SCI-2.M3.L2` · moves SC-STRC→4, IN-DAI→4*

*Objectives:* [K] layers of dough and butter, steam and gluten; butter temperature
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m2-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** puff pastry rises because of how it is built, not because of an ingredient. Understanding the
layers, and the butter temperature that keeps them, makes every laminated dough you will ever make easier.

**You need:** 150 g plain flour; 3 g salt; about 75 g iced water; 100 g cold butter; the rolling pin; a ruler; a sharp
knife; cling film; the fridge; a lamp or a window for the photos.

**Explanation:**

*Layers.* A dough and a fat are folded so they form many thin, separate layers. In the oven, the water in the dough and
the butter turns to steam and pushes the layers apart; the gluten holds them; the butter fries them crisp.

*Folds.* Each "turn" multiplies the layers: a letter fold (in thirds, like a letter) triples them. After one turn, three
butter layers; after two, nine; after three, twenty-seven. Rough puff uses fewer, quicker turns than classic puff, with
the butter in pieces rather than one block.

*Butter temperature.* The butter must be cold and pliable at the same time, about as firm as the dough:
- **too warm:** it melts into the dough and the layers disappear;
- **too cold:** it shatters into flakes, and the layers break.

Rests in the fridge between turns keep the butter there and relax the gluten. Press the dough with a finger: it should
feel like the butter, cool and firm, with no greasy patches.

*The butter itself.* Butter with a higher fat content, often sold as baking butter, and a waxy texture laminates more
easily: it bends rather than breaking, and holds less water to escape. Reason your choice (`IN-DAI`).

*Gluten.* A lean dough with moderate gluten, mixed only until it holds together. Overworked dough shrinks in the oven
and turns tough.

**Practice:**
1. **Fold and see (60 min).** A small dough laminated with cold butter (the flattened block folded in, as for classic
   puff), rested, and cut after one, two and three turns; the layers photographed against the light.

**Look for:**
- visible, separate layers in the cut;
- the butter pliable, not greasy or brittle.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No layers in the cut | Butter melted through the dough | Back in the fridge as soon as it feels greasy |
| Butter breaking into flakes | Too cold, or the rests skipped | Let it soften briefly; rest after every turn or two |
| Dry, tough layers | Flouring heavily between folds | Dust lightly; brush off the excess |

**Safety:** none beyond the kitchen basics: the knife cuts straight down, away from your fingers.

**Record:** the three cut photos, labelled by the number of turns, and a note on the butter's feel.

### PST-3.M2.L2 — Rough puff

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m2-l2 -->
*3 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 4 h · builds on `PST-3.M2.L1` · moves TS-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [S] make rough puff with folds and rests; [J] manage butter temperature
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m2-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** rough puff gives most of the rise of classic puff for a fraction of the work, and it is the dough
of the course's pies and *palmiers*. Making it twice, the second time with your own corrections, is how it becomes
yours.

**You need:** for about 650 g: 250 g plain flour, 4 g salt, 250 g cold butter (a high-fat baking butter if available),
about 125 g iced water; the rolling pin; a ruler; a pastry scraper; cling film; the turn log.

**Explanation:**

*The method:*
1. cube the cold butter (or grate it frozen) and mix it loosely into the flour and salt, the pieces left whole;
2. add the iced water and bring it together to a shaggy dough with visible butter; don't knead;
3. roll it into a rectangle about three times as long as it is wide;
4. letter-fold it in thirds; turn it 90°;
5. roll and fold again; that is two turns;
6. wrap it and rest it in the fridge for 30 minutes;
7. repeat, two turns at a time, for four to six turns in total.

*Managing butter temperature.* A cool kitchen, a cold surface, quick work. When the butter shows signs of softening
(greasy or sticky patches, butter smearing on the pin), the dough goes straight back into the fridge, even mid-turn.
Log each rest and how the butter felt.

*Shape.* Keep the corners square and the thickness even: roll in both directions, not only away from you, and turn the
dough rather than the pin.

*Storing.* Wrapped, in the fridge for a day or two; or frozen.

*Shrinkage.* Rest the dough before cutting and again after shaping, so it doesn't shrink in the oven.

**Practice:**
1. **A batch of rough puff (90 min active, 4 h in total).** Made with the full number of turns and rests; logged.
2. **A second batch (60 min).** Made with the corrections from the first; the two compared in a test bake of a few
   strips.

**Look for:**
- streaks of butter in a smooth dough;
- square corners and an even thickness.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Butter breaking through the surface | Too warm, or too large a piece | Patch with flour; chill; cut the butter smaller next time |
| Uneven, lopsided rise | Rolled in one direction only | Roll both ways; turn the dough |
| Shrunken shapes | No rest before baking | Rest after shaping, 20–30 minutes in the fridge |

**Safety:** a cold dough needs force: roll from the centre outwards with the weight of your body, not by pressing on the
ends of the pin.

**Record:** the turn log (turns, rests, the butter's feel) for both batches, and the corrections.

### PST-3.M2.L3 — Using puff pastry

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m2-l3 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `PST-3.M2.L2` · moves TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] tarts, pies, *palmiers*; [J] bake to full rise and colour
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m2-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** puff pastry is only as good as its cutting and baking. A dragged knife seals the layers; a pale bake
leaves them soggy. Both are easy to avoid.

**You need:** a batch of rough puff (`PST-3.M2.L2`); apples or pears; sugar; butter; a beaten egg; a pie filling (leeks
and cheese, or mushrooms); a sharp knife or cutters; a baking tray; baking paper; a fork.

**Explanation:**

*Cutting.* A sharp knife or cutter, straight down: dragging or twisting seals the layers at the edge, and they won't
rise. Keep the egg wash off the cut edges for the same reason.

*Shapes:*
- **a *tarte fine*:** a thin, free-form tart with a border, topped with sliced apple or pear, butter and sugar;
- **a pie lid:** cut to size, sealed onto the rim, with a vent for the steam;
- ***palmiers*:** the dough rolled out in sugar, both long sides rolled to the middle, sliced and baked;
- **cheese straws:** strips with grated cheese, twisted.

*Full rise and colour.* A hot oven, around 200–220 °C, to puff. Bake until deep golden right through, not pale: pale
puff is soggy inside. For a tart that should stay flat, dock the base with a fork, or weigh it down with a second tray
for part of the bake.

*Savoury and sweet.* An apple or pear *tarte fine*; a leek and cheese pie; a mushroom pie with a puff lid.

**Practice:**
1. **Sweet (60 min).** *Palmiers* and an apple *tarte fine*.
2. **Savoury (60 min).** A pie with a puff lid; the lid cut and photographed in cross-section.

**Look for:**
- an even, tall rise;
- deep golden and crisp throughout.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Edges that don't rise | Egg wash on the edges, gluing the layers | Brush the top only |
| Soggy inside | Pale, underbaked layers | Bake on to deep golden |
| A tart that puffed into a dome | The base not docked or weighted | Dock it; weight it with a tray |

**Safety:** *palmiers* caramelise: the sugar on the tray is hot enough to burn; let them cool on the rack before
handling. A pie filling is cold when the lid goes on (`PST-3.M3.L3`).

**Record:** the cross-section photo of the lid, and the baking times and colour of each item.

### PST-3.M3.L1 — Fruit tarts

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m3-l1 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 4 h · builds on `BAK-2.M5.L1` · moves TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] pastry cream base, fruit arrangement, glaze
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m3-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a fruit tart shows off the season's fruit on a crisp base and a smooth cream. Every component is
simple; the art is keeping the base crisp under the cream until it is eaten.

**You need:** sweet pastry (`BAK-2.M3`) for a 22–24 cm tart tin; pastry cream (`BAK-2.M5`); seasonal fruit
(strawberries in June, plums in late summer, pears or apples in autumn); apricot jam or a fruit jelly for the glaze;
dark chocolate or an egg for sealing; baking paper and baking beans; a pastry brush; the thermometer.

**Explanation:**

*The components:*
- **a case:** sweet pastry blind-baked fully (`BAK-2.M3`);
- **a cream:** a layer of pastry cream (`BAK-2.M5`);
- **fruit:** fresh, arranged;
- **a glaze:** warmed, sieved apricot jam or a fruit jelly, for shine and to keep the fruit from drying.

*Keeping the base crisp.* Bake the case fully, until golden, not pale. Seal it: brush the inside with a thin layer of
melted chocolate, or with egg wash returned to the oven for a few minutes. Assemble close to serving.

*The cream.* Cook the pastry cream until it boils and thickens (well past 71 °C), then cool it by the standard (57 °C to
21 °C within 2 hours, to 5 °C within 6 hours in total, §9): spread thin on a tray, covered on the surface. Beat it
smooth before spreading.

*Fruit arrangement.* Dry the fruit; cut it evenly; arrange it in overlapping rows or circles from the outside in. One or
two fruits, not a fruit salad.

*Seasonal choices.* Strawberries in June; plums in late summer; poached pears or apples in autumn and winter.
Juicy fruit needs a firmer cream under it.

**Practice:**
1. **A fruit tart (90 min active, 4 h in total).** The case blind-baked and sealed; pastry cream made and cooled;
   assembled and glazed close to serving.

**Look for:**
- a crisp base when sliced;
- neat rows of fruit;
- an even shine.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A soggy base under the cream | Pale, unsealed case | Bake it golden; seal it with chocolate or egg wash |
| Juice pooling on the cream | Wet fruit | Dry the fruit; glaze to seal it |
| A tired, weeping tart | Assembled a day ahead | Components ahead; assembly on the day |

**Safety:** the pastry cream is cooled by the standard and kept at most 2 days; the tart is kept in the fridge and out
of it for at most 2 hours (§9). Wash the fruit before cutting.

**Record:** the plan, the cooling times of the cream, and a slice photo showing the base.

### PST-3.M3.L2 — Baked fillings

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m3-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 1 practical session · builds on `BAK-2.M3.L3` · moves TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] *frangipane*, custard, lemon; [J] judge the set
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m3-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** a baked filling sets in the oven, and judging that set is the whole skill: too soon and it runs,
too late and it curdles or cracks.

**You need:** a blind-baked sweet pastry case; for a custard tart: eggs, cream, milk, sugar, vanilla or nutmeg; or for
a lemon tart: lemons, eggs, sugar, cream; or for a *frangipane*: butter, sugar, eggs, ground almonds (or a nut-free mix
of flour and semolina) and pears or plums; the probe; a wire rack.

**Explanation:**

*Frangipane.* Butter and sugar creamed, eggs beaten in, then ground almonds and a little flour. For a nut-free version,
use ground toasted seeds, or a mix of flour and semolina. Spread it in the case, press in sliced pears, plums or
apples, and bake until set and golden.

*Custard fillings.* A baked custard (eggs, cream or milk, sugar) poured into a blind-baked case and baked in a low oven,
so it sets without bubbling or curdling (`EGG-1`). A lemon tart is the classic sharp version (citrus as a pantry good).
Fill the case on the oven shelf, so it doesn't spill on the way.

*Judging the set:*
- **a custard** is set when the edge is firm and the centre wobbles like jelly as a whole, not in waves. Probe the
  centre: at least 71 °C (the egg-thickened row of §9). It finishes setting as it cools;
- **a *frangipane*** is set when a skewer comes out clean and the top springs back.

*Cooling.* At room temperature briefly, on a rack, then into the fridge within the 2-hour limit (§9). Slice it cold,
with a hot, dry knife.

**Practice:**
1. **A custard tart (90 min).** A baked custard or lemon tart; the set judged by the wobble and the probe; sliced when
   cold.

**Look for:**
- a smooth, glossy surface without cracks;
- a clean slice.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Bubbles, cracks, a curdled texture | The oven too hot | A low oven; take it out at the wobble |
| A liquid centre | Taken out too soon | Wobble as a whole, and 71 °C in the centre |
| A tart left out overnight | No cooling plan | Into the fridge within 2 hours |

**Safety:** the custard reaches at least 71 °C in the centre and goes into the fridge within 2 hours (§9). Almonds are a
tree nut (`SAF-1.M4`): the nut-free *frangipane* for an allergic guest, and the tart labelled.

### PST-3.M3.L3 — Savoury tarts and pies

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m3-l3 -->

*Objectives:* [S] savoury fillings, double-crust pies, venting; [J] a crisp base under a wet filling
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m3-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a savoury pie is a whole main course in pastry, and the hardest test of a crisp base, because the
filling is wet. Four habits solve it.

**You need:** short pastry (`BAK-2.M3`) or rough puff (`PST-3.M2.L2`); leeks or onions; eggs; cream and milk; Gouda or

- **heat from below:** a blind-baked base, or a metal tin on a preheated tray or stone, baked low in the oven;

- a crisp, browned base;
- a filling that holds its shape when sliced;
- no leaks.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|

## PST-3.M4 — Creams, ganache and mousses

items. Pass mark 80 %, with every safety item correct.

### PST-3.M4.L1 — *Crème anglaise* variations

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m4-l1 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `EGG-1.M4.L1` · moves TS-EGG→4*

*Objectives:* [S] infusions; a base for ice cream (used in `PST-4`)
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m4-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** *crème anglaise* is the mother of pastry creams: a sauce on its own, the base of a *bavarois* and a
chocolate *crémeux*, and the ice-cream base of `PST-4`. Infused and scaled with confidence, it opens all of them.

**You need:** for about 600 g: 250 g milk, 250 g cream, 6 yolks (about 110 g), 100 g sugar; a vanilla pod, or a herb
(bay, lemon verbena, thyme), coffee beans or tea; a heavy saucepan; a spatula; a sieve; a bowl set in iced water; the
thermometer.

**Explanation:**

*The base* (`EGG-1.M4`). Bring the milk and cream to a simmer. Whisk the yolks and sugar until pale. Pour a little hot
milk onto the yolks, whisking, then return it all to the pan. Stir over gentle heat, the spatula reaching every corner,
until the custard reaches *nappe*: it coats the spatula and a finger drawn through it leaves a clean line. That is
around 80–82 °C, well above the 71 °C the egg-thickened row of §9 asks for. Never boil it: at boiling the yolks curdle.
Strain it at once into a bowl set in iced water, and stir it as it cools.

*Infusions.* Infuse the milk before the yolks go in: bring it to a simmer with the flavouring, cover it, leave it for
10–30 minutes, and strain it. Vanilla, coffee beans, tea, herbs (bay, lemon verbena, thyme), toasted seeds, citrus
zest. Taste the milk: it should taste a little too strong, because the eggs and cream soften it.

*Variations:*
- **a *bavarois*:** *crème anglaise* set with gelatine and lightened with whipped cream;
- **a chocolate *crémeux*:** hot *crème anglaise* poured over chopped chocolate and blended;
- **an ice-cream base** (`PST-4`).

*Scaling.* A larger batch takes longer to thicken and to cool. The cooling standard applies (§9): from 57 °C to 21 °C
within 2 hours, and to 5 °C or below within 6 hours in total. A double batch cools in two bowls in the ice bath, or in a
shallow tray. Keep it covered in the fridge for at most 2 days.

**Practice:**
1. **Two infusions (45 min).** A vanilla and a herb *crème anglaise*, to *nappe* with the thermometer; cooled over ice.
2. **A double batch (45 min).** The same base doubled; the time to *nappe* and the cooling logged.

**Look for:**
- *nappe* at about 80–82 °C;
- no curdling;
- cooled over ice within the limits.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Curdled, grainy custard | Boiled | Off the heat at 82 °C; blend at once to rescue a slight curdle |
| Lumps from the edges | Corners not stirred | A spatula, every corner, constantly |
| A slow, risky cool | Left to cool in the pan | Strain straight into a bowl over ice |

**Safety:** at least 71 °C on the probe; cooled by the standard and logged; at most 2 days in the fridge (§9).

**Record:** the temperatures, the time to *nappe*, and the cooling log for the double batch.

### PST-3.M4.L2 — Ganache

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m4-l2 -->
*2 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `SCI-2.M4.L2` · moves SC-EMUL→3, TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] ratios for glaze, filling and truffles; [K] ganache as an emulsion; fix a split ganache
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m4-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** ganache is two ingredients and one ratio, and it covers glazes, fillings, truffles and whipped
toppings. Seeing it as an emulsion explains why it splits and how to bring it back.

**You need:** 400 g dark chocolate (60–70 %), chopped; 300 g cream; a little warm milk; three bowls; a spatula; the
stick blender; the scale.

**Explanation:**

*Ganache as an emulsion.* Chocolate (cocoa butter and cocoa solids) and cream (water and milk fat) are emulsified into
a smooth, glossy mass. The ratio sets its use:
- about **1 part chocolate to 1 part cream** (dark chocolate) for a glaze;
- about **2 to 1** for a firmer filling and truffles;
- **more cream** for a whipped ganache;
- milk and white chocolate need more chocolate for the same firmness, because they contain less cocoa solids.

*Making it.* Pour hot cream over the chopped chocolate and leave it for a minute. Stir from the centre outwards in
small circles until an emulsion forms in the middle, then widen the circles. Or blend it with a stick blender, the head
kept below the surface so no air goes in.

*Fixing a split ganache.* Oily and grainy means the fat has separated. Blend a spoon of warm milk or cream in from the
centre, and it comes back. A ganache that is too hot or overworked splits most easily.

*Which chocolate.* A real chocolate of 60–70 %, not compound chocolate: compound coating has other fats instead of
cocoa butter, and a waxy texture (`PST-3.M5.L1`).

**Practice:**
1. **Three ganaches (60 min).** Glaze (1 : 1), filling (2 : 1) and truffle ratios with dark chocolate, weighed; the
   textures compared when cool.
2. **Split and fix (30 min).** A ganache split on purpose (too hot, or over-stirred) and repaired; photographed at each
   step.

**Look for:**
- glossy and smooth;
- the ratio matching its use;
- the repair photographed.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Split, oily ganache | Boiling cream on fine chocolate | Cream just off the boil; blend in warm milk to rescue |
| Bubbles in the glaze | Whisked, not stirred | Stir from the centre; blender head under the surface |
| A waxy, dull ganache | Compound chocolate | Use real chocolate of 60–70 % |

**Safety:** ganache contains milk; most chocolate is labelled "may contain" nuts or milk (`SAF-1.M4`): read the label
for allergic guests. A ganache with cream is kept in the fridge.

**Record:** the three ratios and their textures, and the split-and-repair photos.

### PST-3.M4.L3 — Mousses

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m4-l3 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · elapsed 4 h · builds on `PST-3.M4.L2` · moves TS-BAK→4, RD-RATO→3*

*Objectives:* [S] base, aeration, setting; [K] gelatine: blooming, dosing by weight, setting and melting points (taught here, deepened in `MOD-4.M3`); ratios
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m4-l3 -->

**Why it matters:** a mousse is three parts held in balance: a flavoured base, a foam, and something to set it. Written
as a ratio, it can be made with any season's fruit, and varied on purpose.

**You need:** dark chocolate; cream; a seasonal fruit purée (cooked rhubarb, strawberries or plums, sieved); sugar; leaf
gelatine; the 0.1 g scale; a whisk or mixer; a large spatula; glasses or rings; the fridge.

**Explanation:**

*The three parts:*
- **a flavoured base:** melted chocolate, a fruit purée, or a *crème anglaise*;
- **aeration:** whipped cream, whisked egg whites or an Italian meringue;
- **a setting agent:** the chocolate itself, or gelatine.

*Two starting ratios:*
- **chocolate:** 1 part dark chocolate to 2 parts cream by weight; a third of the cream heated and made into a ganache
  with the chocolate, the rest whipped to soft peaks and folded in;
- **fruit:** purée and whipped cream in roughly equal weights, sweetened to taste, set with gelatine.

*Folding.* Fold the foam into the base in thirds, the first third stirred in to loosen it, the rest folded with a large
spatula, turning the bowl. Base and foam at compatible temperatures: the chocolate base just warm, so it doesn't
seize; the gelatine base cool and starting to thicken, so it doesn't set in strings.

*Gelatine:*
- **blooming:** sheets soaked in cold water and squeezed; or powder sprinkled over a measured cold liquid;
- **dosing by weight:** a percentage of the liquid it sets. Sheets differ in strength, so weigh them: take your pack's
  dose for a soft set, convert it to grams per 100 g of liquid, and log it;
- **setting and melting:** gelatine dissolves in warm liquid (never boiled), sets in the fridge over hours, and melts
  again close to body temperature, which gives the melt-in-the-mouth texture;
- **enzymes:** fresh pineapple and kiwi stop it setting unless cooked first;
- **origin:** gelatine comes from pork or beef, which matters for vegetarians and some faiths (`NUT-3`).

*Raw egg.* A mousse aerated with egg whites or with yolks that are not cooked is made with pasteurised egg for every
guest and kept covered in the fridge for at most 24 hours (§9). A mousse on a cooked base (a *crème anglaise* to
71 °C) and cream keeps at most 2 days. The two ratios above use no raw egg.

**Practice:**
1. **A chocolate mousse (60 min active, 4 h in total).** Chocolate as the setting agent; folded; set in glasses.
2. **A fruit mousse (60 min active, 4 h in total).** A seasonal purée with gelatine dosed by weight and whipped cream;
   set in rings and unmoulded.

**Look for:**
- light and smooth, no streaks of white;
- set enough to hold a spoon's shape, melting in the mouth.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Gelatine in strings | Boiled, or added to a cold base | Dissolve it in warm purée; fold when the base is cool |
| A dense mousse | The foam stirred, not folded | Fold in thirds with a large spatula |
| A rubbery or runny set | Sheets counted, not weighed | Weigh the gelatine; log the dose |

**Safety:** raw-egg mousses with pasteurised egg only, at most 24 hours; cream mousses in the fridge, out of it for at
most 2 hours (§9). Gelatine is an animal product: tell guests.

**Record:** both ratios as weighed, the gelatine dose, and photos of the set mousses.

### PST-3.M5.L1 — Chocolate types and melting

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m5-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · moves IN-BAK→3*

*Objectives:* [K] cocoa content, couverture, compound; [S] melt without burning or seizing
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m5-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** chocolate behaves differently by type, and a few degrees or a drop of water decide whether it stays
glossy or turns to grit. Reading the label is the first skill.

**You need:** dark, milk and white chocolate; a dark couverture and a compound (coating) chocolate to compare; a
heatproof bowl and a pan for a bain-marie, or a microwave; a dry spatula; the thermometer; the ingredients for a simple
brownie or ganache, twice.

**Explanation:**

*Types:*
- **cocoa content:** the percentage of cocoa solids and cocoa butter together. More means more bitter and intense;
- **couverture:** extra cocoa butter, so it flows well; for tempering and coating;
- **compound chocolate** (coating chocolate): some or all of the cocoa butter replaced by other fats. It needs no
  tempering, but it has a waxy texture and less flavour;
- **dark, milk and white:** milk and white chocolate contain milk solids and more sugar, and they burn and seize more
  easily.

*Reading a label.* The cocoa percentage; the fats in the ingredients list (cocoa butter only, or vegetable fats too);
the milk solids; the allergen line ("may contain nuts").

*Melting without burning.* Chop the chocolate evenly. Melt it over a bain-marie of barely simmering water, the bowl not
touching the water, or in short bursts in the microwave, stirring between them. Dark chocolate no hotter than about
50 °C; milk and white lower.

*Seizing.* A few drops of water, or steam from the pan, make melted chocolate thick and grainy. Prevent it with dry
bowls and tools, and with the water barely simmering. A seized chocolate is not wasted: it can still go into a ganache
or a sauce with more liquid.

*A comparison bake.* The same brownie or ganache made with two chocolates (for example a 70 % couverture and a compound
chocolate, or dark and milk), photographed in cross-section and explained: gloss, texture, flavour, set.

**Practice:**
1. **Melt and compare (60 min).** Dark, milk and white chocolate melted and their temperatures logged; couverture and
   compound tasted and compared; the comparison bake made with two chocolates, photographed in cross-section and
   explained.

**Look for:**
- smooth and glossy melted chocolate;
- no grain;
- you can read a chocolate label and say what it means for the dish.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Thick, grainy chocolate | Steam or water in the bowl | Dry tools; the water barely simmering; use it in a ganache |
| Burnt patches | A microwave run for two minutes | Short bursts, stirring between them |
| White chocolate gone thick | Overheated | A gentler heat; take it off early and stir |

**Safety:** the bain-marie's steam burns: lift the bowl with a cloth, tilting it away from you. Chocolate allergen lines
(`SAF-1.M4`) are read for every guest.

**Record:** the melting temperatures, the label notes and the comparison photos with your explanation.

### PST-3.M5.L2 — Tempering introduced

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m5-l2 -->
*2.5 h · 2 practical sessions · builds on `PST-3.M5.L1` · moves TS-BAK→4*

*Objectives:* [S] the seed method with a thermometer; [K] why tempering matters
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m5-l2 -->

**Why it matters:** tempered chocolate snaps, shines and releases from a mould; untempered chocolate sets dull and soft.
The seed method makes tempering possible at home with a bowl and a thermometer.

**You need:** about 500 g of dark couverture, two thirds chopped coarsely and one third chopped finely; a heatproof
bowl and a pan; a dry spatula; the thermometer; strips of baking paper; a knife; a cool room.

**Explanation:**

*Why tempering matters.* Cocoa butter can set in several crystal forms; only one (form V) gives a glossy surface, a
clean snap, a shrinkage that releases from moulds, and stability at room temperature. Untempered chocolate sets dull and
streaky (bloom) and soft.

*The seed method:*
1. melt two thirds of the chocolate fully: dark to about 45–50 °C;
2. stir in the remaining third, finely chopped, as "seed" crystals already in the right form;
3. stir until it cools to the working temperature: dark about 31–32 °C, milk about 29–30 °C, white about 28–29 °C. The
   maker's figures on the pack take precedence;
4. remove any unmelted seed.

*Testing.* Dip a knife tip or a strip of baking paper and leave it at room temperature. Tempered chocolate sets glossy
and snaps within a few minutes; untempered stays soft or sets streaky.

*Keeping it in temper.* Work at the working temperature. If it thickens as it cools, warm it briefly over the
bain-marie, a few seconds at a time, stirring, without going above the working range.

*The room.* A cool, dry kitchen without a draught or a fan blowing on the chocolate: humidity and cold air both spoil
the surface.

**Practice:**
1. **Temper dark chocolate (60 min).** By the seed method with a thermometer; tested on a strip; used for dipping
   (orange peel, biscuits, the profiteroles' tops).
2. **Temper again (60 min).** A second batch, logged, used for a few simple bars or shards on baking paper.

**Look for:**
- the test strip glossy and snapping;
- the temperatures logged;
- no streaks.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dull, streaky chocolate | The melt overheated, or no seed | Start again: melt fully, seed, cool to the working range |
| A batch that fails on the bars | The test skipped | Test a strip before using it |
| A grey bloom on the surface | A cold, humid room, or a fan | Work in a cool, dry room without a draught |

**Safety:** keep the chocolate bowl dry and clear of the steam; nothing else beyond the bain-marie's heat.

**Record:** the temperature log for each batch (melt, seed, working), and photos of the strip and the bars.

### PST-3.M6.L1 — Multi-day pastry planning

<!-- BEGIN generated:lesson-pst-3-m6-l1 -->
*2 h · 1 practical session · elapsed 2 d · builds on `BRD-2.M5.L1` · moves KM-PLAN→4*

*Objectives:* [S] a plan for doughs, creams and assembly over three days; component storage
<!-- END generated:lesson-pst-3-m6-l1 -->

**Why it matters:** a dessert of four components made on the day is a stressful afternoon; the same dessert planned
over three days is an easy assembly. Planning backwards from serving is how pastry kitchens work, and it works at home.

**You need:** the dessert you will make in `PST-3.M6.X1`; a three-day planning sheet; labels; containers; fridge and
freezer space; the cooling log.

**Explanation:**

*Working backwards.* Start from the moment of serving and place each component on the day it is best made, given how
long it keeps:
- **doughs** (short, rough puff): days ahead, chilled or frozen;
- **baked cases:** a day ahead, airtight;
- **creams and curds:** a day ahead, cooled by the standard, in the fridge;
- **mousses:** the day before, because they need hours to set; a raw-egg mousse no more than 24 hours ahead (§9);
- **glazes, tempered decorations and fruit:** on the day;
- **assembly:** at the last moment.

*Storage per component.* Each gets a container, a label with the date, a place (fridge or freezer), and a quality
limit. For example: a baked case goes soft after two days; a pastry cream is used within 2 days (§9).

*Control points:*
- the cooling of creams and custards (57 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours, to 5 °C within 6 hours in total, §9);
- the fridge at 4 °C;
- the time out of the fridge on the day, at most 2 hours.

*A plan against actual.* Write the planned time for each step; log the actual time beside it; note the slips and why
they happened. The next plan starts from those notes.

*An example, a plum tart with chocolate for Saturday:*
- **Thursday:** sweet pastry made and chilled; pastry cream made and cooled;
- **Friday:** the case blind-baked and sealed; plums poached;
- **Saturday:** chocolate tempered for shards; the tart assembled and glazed an hour before serving.

**Practice:**
1. **Plan and run (90 min of planning and logging, 2 days in total).** The plan of `PST-3.M6.X1` written and followed,
   with every component's day, storage and control point.

**Look for:**
- every component placed on a day with its storage;
- nothing made on the day that should have been made earlier.

**If it goes wrong:**

| Fault | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A frantic serving day | Everything made on the day | Plan backwards; move what keeps to earlier days |
| A runny mousse | Made two hours before serving | Make it the day before |
| Creams past their limit | Kept for five days | At most 2 days; label with the date |

**Safety:** the creams' cooling is logged; components are labelled and dated; nothing is out of the fridge for more than
2 hours on the day (§9).

**Record:** the plan, the actual times beside it, and the notes on the slips.

### PST-3.M6.X1 — A dessert for six from components made over three days

*brief, brief* — A dessert for six built from components made over three days.

**Task:** plan and make a multi-component dessert across three days, serving it on time.
- **The brief:** a plated or assembled dessert for six guests with at least four components from this course (for
  example a pastry base, a cream or mousse, a fruit element and a chocolate decoration), made over three days.
- **The constraints:**
  - a written plan with control points: cooling, storage, time out of the fridge;
  - allergens and risk groups asked about in advance (raw egg only as pasteurised egg, within 24 hours).
- **The criteria:** the plan against the actual times logged; every control point met, or a change reasoned; guest
  cards.
- **The record:** the plan, the log, photos of each component and the dessert, the cards and your review.

**You need:** the components' ingredients; seasonal Dutch fruit; labels and containers; the thermometer; the planning
sheet; the guest cards.

*Procedure:*
1. Write the three-day plan with storage and control points.
2. Make the components on their days; log the times and control points.
3. Assemble and serve; photograph; guest cards.
4. Compare plan and actual; note the changes.

**Show:** a multi-day plan with control points, the log showing every control point met or a reasoned change. This
meets the `KM-PLAN` level 4 row, as `FIR-3.M6.X1` does for a live-fire meal.
